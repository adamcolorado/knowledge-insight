This lesson evaluates what phenomenology brings to consciousness debates: precise descriptions of the structure of experience, such as pre-reflective self-awareness and the minimal self, and challenges to how the hard problem is framed. You'll be able to explain Zahavi's and Gallagher's accounts of pre-reflective self-awareness and the minimal self, and use phenomenology to assess one theory's description of experience. These tools let you contribute to consciousness research as a critic and a constructor, not only as a reader.

The Explanandum Problem: Phenomenology and the Description of Experience

Every theory of consciousness starts from a description of what it's trying to explain. Usually the description is brief and taken for granted. A theory says that experience is unified, or private, or made of qualities, or present only when information is broadcast, and then gets on with explaining it. The description rarely gets the scrutiny the explanation does.

That's a problem, because you can't explain what you've misdescribed. If a theory starts from a wrong account of experience, it can succeed on its own terms and still explain the wrong thing. A model of how the brain produces "a unified visual field" is only as good as the claim that visual experience is unified in the way the model assumes. The same goes for any theory that assumes experience is composed of discrete qualia, or that there is always a subject watching an inner display.

This is sometimes called the explanandum problem. The explanandum is what a theory sets out to explain; in consciousness science, the description of experience that a theory takes as its target. A theory's explanandum fixes what counts as success. It also fixes what counts as a counterexample. Disputes between theories that seem to be about mechanisms are sometimes really disputes about what experience is like.

Phenomenology has something specific to contribute here. Its whole method is the careful description of experience as it's lived, before theories about its causes are brought in. Phenomenologists have developed detailed accounts of structures that theories often pass over: how experience flows in time, how it's embodied, how it's given as one's own without reflection, and how what's seen is surrounded by what's implicit (Gallagher and Zahavi 2023).

Recent work has used such descriptions to criticize how theories characterize experience. A prominent example concerns integrated information theory, which begins from axioms that are meant to state self-evident properties of every experience. Tim Bayne argued that these axioms fail to do the work the theory needs from them. Some aren't self-evident, and others are too weak to constrain a theory (Bayne 2018).

For a researcher, the skill is being able to check a theory's starting description. You can ask what the theory says experience is like, whether that's accurate, and what follows for the theory if it isn't. That puts you in a position to contribute to consciousness science as a critic of its targets, not only as a reader of its results. This content reflects the field as of September 2026.

Pre-Reflective Self-Awareness and For-Me-Ness: Husserl, Sartre, and Zahavi

Introduction

A common assumption in philosophy of mind is that you become aware of your own experiences only when you reflect on them, by forming a thought about them. Phenomenologists have long argued that this gets the order wrong. Before any reflection, your experience is already given to you as yours.

This reading explains pre-reflective self-awareness, its lineage in Husserl and Sartre, Dan Zahavi's account of for-me-ness, and the main objection to it. This content reflects the field as of September 2026.

Two Kinds of Self-Awareness

Start with a contrast. Reflective self-awareness is an explicit act in which one turns attention to one's own experience and takes it as an object. You do this when you ask yourself how you're feeling, or notice that you've been staring at a screen.

Phenomenologists argue that reflection isn't the basic form of self-awareness (Gallagher and Zahavi 2023). Pre-reflective self-awareness is the implicit awareness of one's own experience as one's own, present in the experience itself without a separate act of reflection. When you see a cup, you're aware of the cup. You aren't thinking about your seeing. But the seeing isn't unconscious either. It's lived through, and lived through as yours.

The shift is from self-awareness as a second act aimed at a first one to self-awareness as a feature of the first act itself.

The Lineage

The idea has a long history in phenomenology (Gallagher and Zahavi 2023).

Husserl's analysis of time-consciousness is one source. He argued that the deepest level of experience, the flow in which experiences take shape in time, is aware of itself without a further act. Otherwise every act of awareness would need another act to be aware of it, without end (Husserl [1928] 1991).

Sartre made the point central. He distinguished awareness of an object from the awareness consciousness has of itself in being aware of that object. His term for the latter was non-positional self-awareness: in Sartre, consciousness's implicit awareness of itself as it is aware of an object, without positing itself as an object (Sartre [1943] 2018, introduction).

Zahavi: For-Me-Ness

Dan Zahavi develops these ideas into a systematic account (Zahavi 2005). His central notion is for-me-ness: Zahavi's term for the first-personal givenness of experience: the way each experience is given as mine, to me, without being an object of reflection.

Take the difference between your pain and someone else's. You can know about another person's pain by what they say and do. Your own pain isn't given to you that way. It's given in the first person. Zahavi argues that this first-personal givenness isn't an extra quality added to the pain, like its sharpness. It's the way any experience is present at all.

Two consequences follow. First, a minimal form of self is present in every experience, in its for-me-ness, before any concept of self or any narrative about one's life. Second, higher-order accounts of consciousness get the structure wrong. If an experience became conscious only when a further state represented it, that further state would need its own awareness, and the regress would begin again. On Zahavi's account, awareness of one's experience is built into the experience, not added by a second state (Zahavi 2005).

The Main Objection

Is for-me-ness really present in all experience? Critics argue that it isn't (Gallagher and Zahavi 2023). They point to states of deep absorption, in which there seems to be only the task and no sense of self. They point to some pathological and meditative states that are reported as selfless. And some argue that for-me-ness isn't found in experience at all, but read into it by a theory.

Zahavi's defenders reply that absorption removes reflective self-consciousness, not the first-personal givenness of the experience, and that reports of selfless states are themselves given in the first person. The debate is open, and it matters. If for-me-ness isn't universal, it can't be a structural feature that every theory of consciousness must explain.

Conclusion

Pre-reflective self-awareness is awareness of one's experience as one's own, built into the experience rather than added by reflection. It draws on Husserl's self-aware temporal flow and Sartre's non-positional self-awareness. Zahavi's for-me-ness names the first-personal givenness of every experience. Whether for-me-ness is present in all experience remains contested.

Key Terms

  • Reflective self-awareness: An explicit act in which one turns attention to one's own experience and takes it as an object.
  • Pre-reflective self-awareness: The implicit awareness of one's own experience as one's own, present in the experience itself without a separate act of reflection.
  • Non-positional self-awareness: In Sartre, consciousness's implicit awareness of itself as it is aware of an object, without positing itself as an object.
  • For-me-ness: Zahavi's term for the first-personal givenness of experience: the way each experience is given as mine, to me, without being an object of reflection.

Gallagher's Minimal Self: Ownership, Agency, and Thought Insertion

Introduction

Philosophers use "self" for very different things: a soul, a brain, a character, a life story. In 2000, Shaun Gallagher proposed a distinction that helped cognitive scientists sort these uses, and it's still widely used.

This reading explains Gallagher's distinction between the minimal self and the narrative self, his further distinction between the sense of ownership and the sense of agency, and the evidence he draws from pathology.

Minimal and Narrative Self

Gallagher distinguishes two notions of self (Gallagher 2000).

The minimal self, in Gallagher's account, is the self as the immediate subject of experience, constituted by the senses of ownership and agency, prior to any narrative extended in time. It's the self you are in the present moment of seeing, moving, or thinking. It doesn't depend on memory or on any idea of who you are. Gallagher describes it as the pre-reflective point of origin for action, experience, and thought.

The narrative self, in Gallagher's account, is the self as extended in time and shaped by the stories told about one's life, by oneself and by others. It draws on memory and intention. It's the self you describe when someone asks who you are. It has a past and a future, and it can be revised.

Minimal selfNarrative self
TimeThe present momentExtended over a life
Depends onThe structure of experienceMemory, language, and others
GivenPre-reflectivelyThrough reflection and storytelling
Can be lost whenOwnership or agency is disruptedMemory or self-narrative breaks down

The shift is from the self as a thing to be found to two different structures, one built into experience and one built over a life.

Ownership and Agency

Gallagher then divides the minimal self in two (Gallagher 2000). The sense of ownership is the pre-reflective sense that it is I who am undergoing a movement or thought, even when I'm not its cause. The sense of agency is the pre-reflective sense that I am the one who initiates or controls an action or thought.

In ordinary voluntary action, the two coincide. When you reach for a cup, the movement is yours and you're doing it. So they're easy to run together.

Gallagher separates them with an ordinary case. If someone pushes you, your body moves. The movement is yours, since it's your arm that swings. But you didn't initiate it. You have a sense of ownership without a sense of agency. The example carries a step in the argument: if the two can come apart, they're distinct components of the minimal self.

Evidence from Pathology

The distinction is useful for describing some psychiatric symptoms (Gallagher 2000). Consider thought insertion: a symptom reported in some cases of schizophrenia, in which a person experiences thoughts in their own mind as authored by someone else.

A person with thought insertion doesn't say that someone else is having the thought. They say the thought is in their own mind but that someone else put it there. On Gallagher's analysis, the sense of ownership is preserved: the thought is experienced as occurring in the person's own stream of consciousness. What's lost is the sense of agency: the person doesn't experience themselves as the thought's author.

This matters for research in two ways. It gives the phenomenological distinction empirical traction, since it helps describe a symptom precisely. And it suggests that the senses of ownership and agency may depend on different mechanisms, which cognitive neuroscience can investigate.

Conclusion

Gallagher distinguishes the minimal self, the immediate subject of experience, from the narrative self, which is extended over time. He divides the minimal self into the sense of ownership and the sense of agency. The involuntary-movement case separates them, and thought insertion shows ownership preserved while agency is lost.

Key Terms

  • Minimal self: In Gallagher's account, the self as the immediate subject of experience, constituted by the senses of ownership and agency, prior to any narrative extended in time.
  • Narrative self: In Gallagher's account, the self as extended in time and shaped by the stories told about one's life, by oneself and by others.
  • Sense of ownership: The pre-reflective sense that it is I who am undergoing a movement or thought, even when I'm not its cause.
  • Sense of agency: The pre-reflective sense that I am the one who initiates or controls an action or thought.
  • Thought insertion: A symptom reported in some cases of schizophrenia, in which a person experiences thoughts in their own mind as authored by someone else.

Phenomenology's Contributions and Its Challenges to the Hard Problem

Introduction

Consciousness science needs descriptions of experience, and phenomenology is the discipline that specializes in them. But phenomenologists haven't only offered descriptions for others to explain. They've also questioned how the problem of consciousness is posed.

This reading sets out phenomenology's three main contributions to the debates and its two main challenges to how the hard problem is framed. This content reflects the field as of September 2026.

Three Contributions

The first contribution is a sharper explanandum: what a theory sets out to explain; in consciousness science, the description of experience that a theory takes as its target. Phenomenologists have described structures of experience in detail (Gallagher and Zahavi 2021).

  1. Time. Experience isn't a series of instants. Each moment holds the just-past and anticipates the about-to-come.
  2. Embodiment. Experience is lived from a body that is the perceiver's own, not observed from outside.
  3. Self-awareness. Experience is given as one's own without reflection.
  4. Horizon. What's perceived is surrounded by what's implicit: the unseen back of a cup, the room around it.

A theory that leaves out any of these has an incomplete target.

The second contribution is method. Phenomenology offers disciplined ways of producing first-person reports. One is the second-person method: a method in which a trained interviewer helps a person describe a specific experience, guiding their attention without suggesting content (Gallagher and Zahavi 2021). Such methods have been paired with brain imaging, so that refined descriptions guide the analysis of neural data.

The third contribution is a critique of the qualia framework (Gallagher and Zahavi 2021). Much philosophy of mind treats experience as made of qualia: the qualitative, felt properties of experiences, such as the redness of seeing red or the painfulness of pain. Phenomenologists object to the picture of qualia as raw, inner feels that can be considered one at a time. The redness you see is given as the tomato's color, not as a property of an inner state. Experience is directed at the world, and its character can't be separated from what it presents. On this view, a theory that sets out to explain qualia, understood as inner feels, has misdescribed its target.

Two Challenges to Framing

The first challenge concerns the hard problem's starting point. The problem is often posed as a gap between an inner, private mind and a physical world described without any reference to experience. Phenomenologists argue that this is a Cartesian starting point, not a neutral one (Gallagher and Zahavi 2021). If experience is first defined as private and inner, and the world as what physics describes, then the gap between them is partly built into the definitions. This challenge is contested. The hard problem, as Chalmers states it, doesn't assume that the mind is a separate substance (Chalmers 1995). Critics of phenomenology reply that redescribing experience as embodied and world-directed relocates the problem rather than dissolving it.

The second challenge concerns the inner-theater model. Some theories picture consciousness as a stage where contents are displayed. Baars's global workspace theory uses this image explicitly, though as a metaphor for broadcast (Baars 1988). Daniel Dennett gave the model a name, the Cartesian theater: Dennett's name for the assumed place in the brain where everything comes together to be presented to an inner observer (Dennett 1991, ch. 5). Dennett rejected it from an illusionist direction. Phenomenologists reject it from theirs: experience isn't a display viewed by a subject, but the subject's own lived openness to the world. The two critiques converge on the target while disagreeing about almost everything else.

Conclusion

Phenomenology contributes a sharper explanandum, disciplined methods for first-person report, and a critique of the qualia framework. It challenges the hard problem's Cartesian starting point and the inner-theater model some theories assume. Both challenges are contested, and neither by itself decides between theories.

Key Terms

  • Explanandum: What a theory sets out to explain; in consciousness science, the description of experience that a theory takes as its target.
  • Second-person method: A method in which a trained interviewer helps a person describe a specific experience, guiding their attention without suggesting content.
  • Qualia: The qualitative, felt properties of experiences, such as the redness of seeing red or the painfulness of pain.
  • Cartesian theater: Dennett's name for the assumed place in the brain where everything comes together to be presented to an inner observer.

IIT's Axioms, the Limits of Phenomenology, and a Map of Contributions

Introduction

Integrated information theory is the theory of consciousness that most explicitly starts from phenomenology. That makes it the clearest test of what phenomenological description can do for a theory, and of what it can't.

This reading examines the critique of IIT's axioms as descriptions of experience, sets out the limits of phenomenology, and maps phenomenology's contribution and challenge to each position and theory in the debates. This content reflects the field as of September 2026.

The Case: IIT's Axioms

IIT begins with axioms: statements of properties it takes to be true of every conceivable experience, such as that experience is unified and definite. It then derives what a physical system must be like to have those properties (Albantakis et al. 2023). The theory claims its axioms are immediate and irrefutable.

Tim Bayne examined the axioms of an earlier formulation of the theory (Bayne 2018). His conclusion is that none of them does what the theory needs. An axiom must be self-evident, so that no one can reasonably reject it. It must also be substantive, so that it constrains which physical systems are conscious. Bayne argues that each axiom fails one test or the other. Some are self-evident only on a weak reading that constrains nothing. Others are substantive only on a strong reading that isn't self-evident. For example, he argues that not every experience is unified in the way the integration axiom claims, and that some cases of brain damage suggest otherwise. He proposes instead to treat consciousness as a natural kind, studied by finding the markers that cluster with it.

The shift here is from treating the axioms as descriptions to asking whether they are descriptions at all, or theoretical commitments stated in the language of experience.

The Limits of Phenomenology

Phenomenology can check a theory's description of experience. It can't, by itself, settle what experience ultimately is. Whether experience is physical, fundamental, or an illusion is a metaphysical question, and description doesn't answer it. Even among phenomenologists, whether the method is metaphysically neutral is debated (Zahavi 2025).

So phenomenology's verdicts are of a specific kind. It can say that a theory's target is misdescribed or incomplete. It can't say that the theory's proposed mechanism is false, or that a rival metaphysics is true.

Its contribution to each position is a better target, and its challenge is to the description each one starts from.

Guided Walkthrough: Checking IIT 4.0's Integration Axiom Against Experience

Introduction

Integrated information theory claims to start from experience itself. That claim can be checked. This reading models how to assess a theory's description of experience phenomenologically, using one of IIT's axioms as the case. It works through four steps, flags a common pitfall, and ends with an annotated evaluation table for the axiom. This content reflects the field as of September 2026.

Locating the Theory

The current statement of the theory is "Integrated Information Theory (IIT) 4.0" (Albantakis et al. 2023). It's open access, so you can read along. The axioms are set out near the start of the paper, before the postulates and the mathematics, which this reading doesn't need.

IIT 4.0 begins with a zeroth axiom and five further axioms, which it calls the essential properties of experience. Here they are, paraphrased, with short key phrases quoted.

AxiomIIT 4.0's claim about every experience
ExistenceExperience exists: there is something.
IntrinsicalityExperience "exists for itself," not relative to an outside observer.
InformationExperience is specific: it is "this one," not another.
IntegrationExperience is unitary: "a whole," irreducible to separate experiences.
ExclusionExperience is definite: it is "this whole," with determinate borders.
CompositionExperience is structured: composed of distinctions and the relations that bind them.

The theory claims these properties are immediate and irrefutably true of any conceivable experience. From each, it derives a postulate about what a physical substrate must be like.

Walking Through the Check

Step 1: Take one axiom and state the phenomenological claim it makes

Choose one axiom and restate it as a claim about experience, in plain terms. Take integration. The claim is that at any moment, your experience is one experience, not a collection of separate ones. When you see a blue book on a table, you don't have one experience of blue, another of the book's shape, and a third of the table. You have a single experience that includes all of them.

Stated this way, the claim can be checked by attending to experience.

Step 2: Test it against a described experience

Now test the claim against a case. The unity of a visual scene is the standard one. Look around the room you're in. The objects appear together, in one field, related to each other in space. It would be strange to describe this as several experiences that happen to occur at once.

Extend the case across the senses. As you read, you may hear traffic and feel the chair. These seem unified too. So far, the axiom fits.

Then look for hard cases. Bayne points to cases of brain damage in which a person can see the parts of an object but can't see them as one thing (Bayne 2018). The case doesn't refute the axiom, but it shows that "unified" needs specifying. Unity of the whole field and unity of each object's features are different claims.

Step 3: Check whether the axiom captures temporal flow, horizon, embodiment, and self-givenness

Next, check the axiom against structures phenomenologists have described.

  • Temporal flow. Your experience of a sentence holds the words just read and anticipates the next. The axiom speaks of experience as a whole, but its formulation doesn't say whether the whole extends in time. It's silent on the flow.
  • Horizon. The book's unseen back and the room beyond your view are part of how the scene is given, though not directly seen. It's unclear whether the "whole" includes these implicit elements or only what's explicitly present.
  • Embodiment. Your scene is given from a body, oriented around where you are and what you can do. The axiom doesn't mention this.
  • Self-givenness. Your experience is given as yours. IIT's intrinsicality axiom, that experience exists for itself, sounds close. But existing for itself, not relative to an observer, isn't the same as being given as mine. The resemblance needs checking, not assuming.

The shift here is from asking whether the axiom is true to asking whether it's complete.

Step 4: Judge whether the axiom is a description or a theoretical posit

Finally, ask what kind of claim the axiom is. Bayne argues that IIT's axioms face a dilemma (Bayne 2018). Read weakly, an axiom may be self-evident but constrain nothing. Read strongly, it constrains the theory but isn't self-evident.

Apply this to integration. "Experience is unitary" is close to a description: you can check it by attending. "Irreducible to separate experiences" goes further, and irreducibility is what the theory later measures in causal terms. If you can't find irreducibility in experience, only infer it, part of the axiom is a posit dressed as a description.

Key Considerations

Terms.

TermMeaning in this check

HorizonThe implicit context co-given with what's explicitly perceived

A common pitfall. The most common pitfall is rejecting a theory because of its metaphysics instead of evaluating its description.

  • IIT has implications many find implausible, such as that some very simple systems have some consciousness. That's a separate question from whether its axioms describe experience well.
  • A phenomenological check targets the starting description. It asks whether the explanandum is accurate and complete.
  • A good description can support a theory you reject. If the integration axiom fits experience, that counts in the theory's favor on that point, whatever you think of its identity claim.

GOAL
Help me evaluate what phenomenological methods contribute to and challenge in consciousness debates by examining pre-reflective self-awareness in my own experience and using it to evaluate one theory or position.

HOW TO RUN THE CONVERSATION
- Ask one question at a time, then wait for my reply. Keep each of your turns under about 120 words.
- Don't lecture. Explain a point only when I need it to continue, then return to my experience.
- Be attentive, patient, and collegial. Give me time to attend to my experience before answering. Define terms of art briefly on first use. If I prefer not to try the movement exercise, invite me to recall or imagine a case instead.
- Plain conversation only: no web search, files, or artifacts.

- Start now. Open with one or two warm sentences: this is an exploratory conversation, not a test; we'll start with my own experience, right now, and then bring a theory to it. Then ask the first question.

TOPICS, IN ORDER
1. Given as mine? Ask whether, as I read this, my experience is given as mine without my reflecting on it, and have me describe how. Help me describe pre-reflective self-awareness without turning it into a thought about myself. If I say I only notice my experience when I reflect, ask whether my experience was unconscious before I reflected, or only unnoticed.
2. Ownership and agency. Ask whether I can separate my sense of owning a movement from my sense of causing it. Suggest a simple test if helpful: let one arm be lifted by the other hand, then lift it myself. Help me describe the difference between the sense of ownership and the sense of agency.
3. Does the theory include it? Ask me to choose one theory or position on consciousness, and ask whether its description of experience includes what I just described. Help me state the theory's starting description. For example, a higher-order theory adds a second state, where phenomenology finds self-awareness built into the first. 4. Closing reflection. Ask what the theory needs to add or change. Help me state one specific addition or change, and one question phenomenology can't settle about the theory.

POSITIONS TO KEEP ACCURATE
- Pre-reflective self-awareness is the implicit awareness of one's own experience as one's own, without a separate act of reflection. Zahavi calls its first-personal givenness for-me-ness.
- Gallagher's minimal self is the immediate subject of experience, constituted by the senses of ownership and agency. The narrative self is extended over time.
- Ownership and agency can come apart, as in involuntary movement and thought insertion.
- Phenomenology contributes a sharper explanandum, disciplined first-person methods, and a critique of the qualia framework. It challenges the Cartesian starting point and the inner-theater model.
- Phenomenology alone doesn't settle metaphysical questions.

MISREADINGS TO CORRECT GENTLY
Keep every point tied to my own experience and chosen theory. When a misreading appears, name the accurate position briefly, then return to my experience.
- Pre-reflective self-awareness as introspection. It's built into the experience, not a second act aimed at it.
- For-me-ness as a feeling or quality, like warmth. It's the first-personal way any experience is given, not an extra quality.
- The minimal self as a soul or inner entity. It's a structure of experience, not a thing behind it.
- Phenomenology as refuting a theory outright. It can show a theory's description is incomplete or inaccurate, but can't by itself show its mechanism or metaphysics false.
- Phenomenology as mere introspective report. It's a disciplined method for describing structures, not a report of private states.

TO FINISH
After I state the change and the question, close in one short turn:
- Affirm one precise description I gave, in my own words where possible.
- Suggest one or two next steps that fit how the conversation went.
