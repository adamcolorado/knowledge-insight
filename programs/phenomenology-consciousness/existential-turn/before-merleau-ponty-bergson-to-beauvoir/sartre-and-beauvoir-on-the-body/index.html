Bergson to Beauvoir: Sartre and Beauvoir on the Body

This lesson compares Jean-Paul Sartre's and Simone de Beauvoir's accounts of consciousness and the lived body. Sartre offers a self-less consciousness, the look of the other, and three dimensions of the body. Beauvoir argues that the body is not a thing but a situation. You'll be able to explain each account and compare how they treat freedom, other people, and the constraints of embodiment. Their debate shapes current feminist, critical, and social approaches to embodiment and consciousness.

What you will be able to do

  • Compare Sartre's account of consciousness and the body with Beauvoir's account of the body as situation

Being Seen: Shame, Self-Consciousness, and the Socially Situated Body

Most accounts of the lived body describe it from the inside: how I feel my hand, how I move through a room. Yet one of the most powerful changes in how you live your body comes from outside. You're absorbed in something, and suddenly you realize you're being watched. Your posture, your face, and your hands become things someone else is seeing. Nothing about your body has changed physically. Everything about how you live it has.

A second case is less momentary. Walk into a room where you're the only one of your kind: the only woman at a meeting, the only person of your age, race, or accent. You may feel your body as conspicuous before anyone has looked at you. Here the look isn't one person's gaze. It's built into the situation, and it can persist long after the room empties. It shapes where you sit, how you speak, and what you expect others to notice first.

These experiences matter for consciousness research, because they show that self-consciousness isn't only a relation between a mind and itself. Dan Zahavi argues that shame is a form of self-consciousness mediated by others: in shame, I'm aware of myself as seen and evaluated by someone else, and that awareness alters how I experience myself (Zahavi 2014). He draws on Jean-Paul Sartre's analysis of the look (Sartre [1943] 2018, pt. 3, ch. 1), while questioning parts of it. On this view, any account of self-awareness that leaves out other people is incomplete.

The second case has become central to critical phenomenology. This approach extends classical phenomenology to show how social structures, such as race, gender, disability, and class, shape lived experience itself, not just the circumstances around it (Weiss, Murphy, and Salamon 2019). It draws heavily on Simone de Beauvoir's claim that the body is lived as a situation, charged with social meanings (Beauvoir [1949] 2010, vol. 1, pt. 1, ch. 1). What it means to be looked at depends on who is looking, who is looked at, and what that look has meant historically.

The two cases also mark the difference between Sartre's and Beauvoir's accounts of the body. One describes the look as a structure of any encounter between free consciousnesses. The other asks how that structure is lived by bodies placed differently in a social world. Knowing both lets you add the social dimension to any description of lived experience, and ask whether a given description has assumed a body that no one in particular has.

Sartre's Non-Egological Consciousness, the For-Itself, and the In-Itself

Introduction

Husserl held that a pure ego stands behind every act of consciousness (Husserl [1913] 2014, §57). The young Sartre, writing in 1936, argued that there's no such ego (Sartre [1936] 2004). Consciousness, he claimed, is impersonal, and the self is something it encounters, not something it contains.

This reading explains Sartre's non-egological account of consciousness in The Transcendence of the Ego, and the distinction between the for-itself and the in-itself that he develops later. Citations to Being and Nothingness give the part and chapter. Richmond's translation prints the French page numbers for cross-reference with the older Barnes translation (Sartre [1943] 1956).

No "I" in Consciousness

Sartre's first claim is negative. The ego isn't in consciousness, as its inhabitant or source. It's an object for consciousness, like any other object in the world (Sartre [1936] 2004). He calls this the transcendent ego: in Sartre's account, the ego as an object for consciousness, constituted in reflection, rather than an inhabitant or source of consciousness.

His evidence is ordinary experience. Take his example of running after a streetcar. While you run, you're conscious of the streetcar, and of it as having-to-be-caught. You aren't conscious of yourself running. There's no "I" in the experience. The "I" appears only if you stop and reflect: "I am running for the streetcar." Reflection produces the ego as its object. It doesn't find it already there.

Sartre calls the unreflective level pre-reflective consciousness: consciousness as absorbed in its object, without taking itself as an object and without an "I." Most of waking life is lived this way.

Self-Awareness Without an Object

If consciousness has no "I" in it, is it unaware of itself? Sartre says no. Consciousness is aware of itself, but not as an object. It has non-positional self-awareness: consciousness's implicit awareness of itself as it is aware of an object, without positing itself as an object.

The distinction is easiest to see with an example. When you count cigarettes in a case, you're positionally aware of the cigarettes: they're what you're counting. You're also aware, non-positionally, that you're counting. If someone asks what you're doing, you can answer at once, without having reflected. That readiness shows the self-awareness was already there (Sartre [1943] 2018, introduction).

Pre-reflective consciousnessReflective consciousness
Directed atThe object: the streetcar, the cigarettesConsciousness itself, taken as an object
Self-awarenessNon-positional, implicitPositional, explicit
Is there an "I"?NoYes: the ego appears as an object

The For-Itself and the In-Itself

In Being and Nothingness (1943), Sartre sets these ideas within a larger ontology built on two kinds of being (Renaudie 2026).

  • The in-itself (en-soi), in Sartre, is the being of things: full, self-identical, simply what it is. A stone is a stone. It has no gap within it, and no possibilities it could fail to be.
  • The for-itself (pour-soi) is the being of consciousness: never simply what it is, always open toward possibilities, and marked by lack and freedom. Consciousness is always beyond itself, toward what it isn't yet.

Sartre's famous formula for the for-itself is that it is what it is not and is not what it is. The waiter who plays at being a waiter, in Sartre's example, can never simply coincide with the role the way a stone coincides with being a stone (Sartre [1943] 2018, pt. 1, ch. 2). That gap is freedom, and it's also why, for Sartre, human beings are condemned to be free (pt. 4, ch. 1).

Conclusion

For Sartre, consciousness has no "I" within it. The ego is a transcendent object constituted in reflection. Pre-reflective consciousness is absorbed in its objects and aware of itself only non-positionally. In Being and Nothingness, the for-itself, consciousness, is set against the in-itself, the full being of things.

Key Terms

  • Transcendent ego: In Sartre, the ego as an object for consciousness, constituted in reflection, rather than an inhabitant or source of consciousness.
  • Pre-reflective consciousness: In Sartre, consciousness as absorbed in its object, without taking itself as an object and without an "I."
  • Non-positional self-awareness: In Sartre, consciousness's implicit awareness of itself as it is aware of an object, without positing itself as an object.
  • In-itself (en-soi): In Sartre, the being of things: full, self-identical, simply what it is.
  • For-itself (pour-soi): In Sartre, the being of consciousness: never simply what it is, always open toward possibilities, and marked by lack and freedom.

Sartre on the Look, Shame, and the Three Dimensions of the Body

Introduction

How do I know that other minds exist? Philosophers usually treat this as a problem of inference from behavior. Sartre argues that I don't infer the other at all. I experience the other directly, when I'm looked at.

This reading explains Sartre's account of the look and shame, and his account of the body in three dimensions. Citations to Being and Nothingness give the part and chapter.

The Look

Sartre's account of the look is in Part 3, chapter 1, of Being and Nothingness (Sartre [1943] 2018, pt. 3, ch. 1). His central example is a man looking through a keyhole. Moved by jealousy or curiosity, he's absorbed in what he sees. Then he hears footsteps in the hall and realizes someone is looking at him.

The look (le regard), in Sartre's account, is the other's presence as a subject for whom I am an object. It's how the other is given to me directly, not as an object I perceive, but as a freedom that perceives me. When I'm looked at, I no longer only see. I'm seen.

Sartre's point is that this experience doesn't depend on inference. I don't reason from the sound of footsteps to the existence of a mind. I'm struck, in my being, by the fact that I'm being looked at.

Shame

The feeling that reveals the look is shame: in Sartre, the recognition that I am the object the other sees. Shame isn't primarily a moral judgment on what I've done. It's the recognition that I have an outside, a being that the other sees and judges, which I can't see or control.

This outside is my being-for-others: in Sartre, the dimension of my being that exists only as seen and judged by another, and that I can't grasp from within. The other holds a secret about me: what I am, seen from outside. That's why, for Sartre, relations with others involve conflict. Each of us tries to recover our freedom from the other's look, or to capture the other's freedom.

The Three Dimensions of the Body

In Part 3, chapter 2, Sartre applies this account to the body (pt. 3, ch. 2). He distinguishes the three dimensions of the body: in Sartre, the body as lived by me, the body as an object for others, and my body as I exist it as known by others.

DimensionWhat it isEveryday example
1. The body livedMy body as I live it, not attended to; the point of view I am, rather than a thing I see. Sartre says it is "passed over in silence."Reading a book: I'm aware of the page, not my eyes
2. The body for othersMy body as an object for the other, and the other's body as an object for meA doctor examining my arm as an organism
3. My body as known by othersMy body as I experience it through the other's look: alienated, given to me as seen from outsideBlushing when I realize I'm being watched

The first dimension echoes pre-reflective consciousness. I don't perceive my eyes while reading. I see through them. The second is the body as other people and the sciences treat it. The third is where the look enters my bodily life. I feel my body as something others see, and I can't see it that way myself. Sartre's examples include shyness and blushing: the body felt as exposed, and escaping my control.

Conclusion

For Sartre, the look is the other's presence as a subject for whom I'm an object. Shame reveals it, and with it my being-for-others. The body has three dimensions: lived and passed over in silence, an object for others, and my body as I live it as known by others, alienated in their look.

Key Terms

  • The look (le regard): In Sartre, the other's presence as a subject for whom I am an object.
  • Shame: In Sartre, the recognition that I am the object the other sees.
  • Being-for-others: In Sartre, the dimension of my being that exists only as seen and judged by another, and that I can't grasp from within.
  • Three dimensions of the body: In Sartre, the body as lived by me, the body as an object for others, and my body as I exist it as known by others.

Beauvoir on the Body as Situation and Becoming a Woman

Introduction

Biology is often treated as the fixed ground on which culture builds. Simone de Beauvoir's The Second Sex (1949) rejects that picture without denying biology. The body, she argues, is neither a mere thing nor a mere construction. It's a situation.

This reading explains Beauvoir's account of the body as situation, Toril Moi's influential reading of it, and what Beauvoir means by becoming a woman. Citations to The Second Sex give the volume, part, and chapter.

The Body as Situation

Beauvoir's first chapter, "Biological Data," surveys what biology says about sexual difference (Beauvoir [1949] 2010, vol. 1, pt. 1, ch. 1). She doesn't deny the facts. She denies that they settle anything on their own.

Her reasoning draws on phenomenology: on Heidegger, Sartre, and above all Merleau-Ponty (Heidegger [1927] 2008; Sartre [1943] 2018; Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012). The body isn't simply an object that science describes. It's the way we have a hold on the world, and the outline of our projects. Beauvoir concludes that the body is not a thing but a situation.

A situation, in Beauvoir's sense, is the whole context in which a free existence finds itself and acts: body, history, economic conditions, and social meanings, all as lived. The body as situation is the body understood not as a fixed object with a predetermined meaning, but as lived within a situation, whose significance depends on the projects and social world in which it's taken up.

Biological facts, then, are real but not destiny. Muscle mass, reproduction, and hormonal cycles matter. But how they matter, what they enable, and what they prevent depend on the situation in which they're lived. A physical difference that's decisive in one society may be trivial in another.

Moi's Reading

Toril Moi's essay "What Is a Woman?" made "the body as situation" central to how Beauvoir is read today (Moi 1999). Much feminist theory from the 1960s on relied on the sex/gender distinction: the distinction between biological sex and socially constructed gender. Moi argues that Beauvoir's idea makes the distinction unnecessary.

On the sex/gender model, the body is a natural base and gender a cultural layer on top. On Beauvoir's model, there's no neutral base. The body is always already lived in a situation, and it's both a concrete, material thing and a bearer of social meanings. Moi holds that this lets us talk about women's bodies and lives without either biological determinism or the claim that bodies don't matter.

Becoming a Woman

The second volume of The Second Sex opens with Beauvoir's most famous claim. Paraphrased, it says that no one is born a woman: one becomes a woman (vol. 2, pt. 1).

Becoming a woman, in Beauvoir's sense, is the lived process through which a female human being takes up, and is shaped by, the meanings her society gives to womanhood, from childhood on. Beauvoir traces it through childhood, girlhood, and adult life. A girl learns to experience her body as fragile, as looked at, and as destined for others, long before she could choose otherwise.

The claim is phenomenological, not only sociological. "Becoming" doesn't mean that culture stamps a form on passive material. It means that womanhood is lived: taken up, resisted, and reinterpreted by a free existence, but within a situation she didn't make. That's why Beauvoir can hold both that women are free and that their freedom is constrained.

Conclusion

For Beauvoir, the body is not a thing but a situation: lived within projects and social meanings, so that biological facts matter only as lived. Moi reads this as dissolving the sex/gender distinction. Becoming a woman is the lived process of taking up and being shaped by a society's meanings of womanhood.

Key Terms

  • Situation: In Beauvoir, the whole context in which a free existence finds itself and acts: body, history, economic conditions, and social meanings, all as lived.
  • Body as situation: In Beauvoir, the body understood not as a fixed object with a predetermined meaning, but as lived within a situation, whose significance depends on the projects and social world in which it's taken up.
  • Sex/gender distinction: The distinction between biological sex and socially constructed gender, which Moi argues Beauvoir's idea of the body as situation makes unnecessary.
  • Becoming a woman: In Beauvoir, the lived process through which a female human being takes up, and is shaped by, the meanings her society gives to womanhood, from childhood on.

Beauvoir's Ethics of Ambiguity, Sartre Compared, and the Originality Debate

Introduction

Beauvoir was long read as a follower who applied Sartre's philosophy to women. That reading is now widely rejected, and the difference between her views and his is one of the main reasons.

This reading gives an overview of Beauvoir's The Ethics of Ambiguity, compares her account of freedom and the body with Sartre's, and notes the debate over her originality. It ends with the core concepts of both accounts in one table.

The Ethics of Ambiguity

In The Ethics of Ambiguity (1947), Beauvoir argues that human existence is fundamentally ambiguous (Beauvoir [1947] 2018). Ambiguity, in Beauvoir's sense, is the condition of human existence as both free subject and object, consciousness and body, transcending its situation and bound by it. We can't escape either side. Attempts to deny our freedom, or to deny our embeddedness in the world, are both forms of bad faith.

Two points matter for the comparison with Sartre.

  • Freedom is situated. Freedom is always exercised within a situation. And situations can widen or narrow the concrete possibilities open to someone.
  • Oppression limits freedom. Oppression is a situation imposed by others that cuts a person off from their possibilities. Beauvoir holds that one can't will oneself free without willing others free, since my freedom needs a world of free others to be realized.

Sartre and Beauvoir Compared

The otherThe look objectifies me, and relations with others involve conflictOthers can oppress, but reciprocity is possible, and my freedom depends on others' freedom
The bodyThree dimensions: lived, for others, and as known by othersA situation, charged with historical and social meanings, including those of sex

The central difference is in the last row. For the Sartre of Being and Nothingness, even a prisoner is free, because freedom gives the situation its meaning (Sartre [1943] 2018, pt. 4, ch. 1). Beauvoir takes situation more seriously. A woman raised to see herself as an object for others, or an enslaved person denied any horizon, isn't free in the same way as someone whose situation opens possibilities. Their freedom is real, but it's made smaller.

The Originality Debate

For decades, Beauvoir was presented as Sartre's disciple, applying his ideas. Since the 1980s, scholars have challenged this. Margaret Simons argues that Beauvoir developed key ideas independently, some before Sartre did, and that her philosophy influenced his (Simons 1999). Her early diaries and her novel She Came to Stay are among the evidence (Beauvoir [1943] 1949). Megan Burke summarizes the scholarly reassessment: Beauvoir is now read as an original philosopher whose account of situated freedom differs from Sartre's in ways that matter (Burke 2023, §2). How much she shaped Sartre's own work remains debated.

Conclusion

In The Ethics of Ambiguity, Beauvoir holds that existence is ambiguous, that freedom is situated, and that oppression limits freedom. Sartre treats freedom as absolute and the look as conflictual. Beauvoir holds that situations can enlarge or restrict freedom and that reciprocity is possible. Scholars now read her as an original philosopher, though how far she influenced Sartre is still debated.

Guided Close Reading: Sartre's Keyhole Scene and the Look in Being and Nothingness

Introduction

Sartre's keyhole scene is one of the most quoted passages in 20th-century philosophy. It's often cited as a vivid anecdote about embarrassment. In context, it's a tightly built argument: that other people are given to us directly, as subjects, in the experience of being looked at. This reading follows the scene move by move, links it to Sartre's account of the body, and marks where Beauvoir would add to it. It ends with a model paraphrase and a comparison point.

Locating the Passage

The scene is in Part 3, chapter 1, of Being and Nothingness, in the section titled "The Look" (Sartre [1943] 2018, pt. 3, ch. 1). Sarah Richmond's translation prints the French page numbers in the margin, so you can cross-reference the older Hazel Barnes translation (Sartre [1943] 1956). Key terms below are given in French, with a table of their usual English renderings under Key Considerations.

No free English translation is available. The free Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Sartre summarizes the scene and its argument (Renaudie 2026, §4.3).

Before you start, note Sartre's problem. He has argued that other philosophers, including Husserl and Heidegger, failed to show how I encounter another consciousness, as opposed to another body from which I infer a mind (Husserl [1931] 1960, §§42–62; Heidegger [1927] 2008, §26; Sartre [1943] 2018, pt. 3, ch. 1). The keyhole scene is his answer.

Walking Through the Argument

Step 1: Absorbed, unreflective looking through the keyhole

Sartre asks us to imagine that, moved by jealousy, curiosity, or vice, he has put his ear to a door and is looking through a keyhole. He's alone, and absorbed in the scene beyond the door.

At this moment, there's no self in view. Consciousness is pre-reflective: absorbed in its object, without taking itself as an object. Sartre stresses that there's no "I" to which he could attach his acts. He simply is his acts: the looking, the listening, the leaning toward the door. The situation is organized by the goal of seeing what's behind it. The keyhole is a means, and the door an obstacle.

Step 2: Footsteps in the hall

Then he hears footsteps in the hall. Someone is looking at him.

Notice what doesn't happen. He doesn't perceive a body, reason that it belongs to a mind, and infer that the mind is observing him. The footsteps are enough. The other's presence is given directly, as a look aimed at him.

Step 3: The sudden shift to being seen

With the footsteps, everything changes. Sartre says that he is suddenly affected in his very being. A self appears where there was none: not the reflective ego, but a self given to him through the other. He now exists as an object, a man bent at a keyhole, for someone else.

This self is his being-for-others (l'être-pour-autrui). He can't see it. He can't know exactly what the other sees: a voyeur, a jealous lover, a fool. Yet he is it. The other has a hold on him that he can't reach.

Step 4: Shame as recognition of oneself as the object the other sees

The feeling that reveals this is shame (la honte). For Sartre, shame is a recognition: I recognize that I am the object the other sees. It isn't first a moral verdict on eavesdropping, though it may become one. It's the immediate, felt recognition that I have an outside, and that it's in another's hands.

Shame is therefore evidence for the other's existence. I couldn't be ashamed before an object. Shame requires a subject who looks at me. In feeling it, I encounter that subject directly.

How the Scene Links to the Three Dimensions of the Body

The keyhole scene maps onto Sartre's later account of the body. Before the footsteps, his body is lived in the first dimension, passed over in silence: the point of view from which he looks, not something he sees. With the look, his body becomes an object for the other, the second dimension. And he lives his body as known by the other, the third dimension: bent, exposed, and caught. The blush of shame is that third dimension felt in the flesh.

A common misreading. The most common misreading treats the look as literal vision: another person's eyes directed at me. That's too narrow. Sartre is explicit that the look can be manifested by a rustle of branches, the sound of footsteps followed by silence, or a curtain moving at a window. None of these involves seeing a pair of eyes. What matters is the other's presence as a subject for whom I'm an object. In the keyhole scene itself, he never sees anyone. The footsteps are enough.

A brief contrast with Beauvoir. Sartre's scene is carefully general. The man at the keyhole could be anyone, and so could the one who looks. Beauvoir would add that looks aren't all alike (Beauvoir [1949] 2010; Burke 2023). How the look is lived depends on the situation of the one looked at: on gender, history, and social meaning. For a woman who has learned since girlhood to experience her body as looked at, being seen isn't a sudden break in absorbed freedom. It's a standing condition, woven into how she lives her body at all.

Summary

The keyhole scene moves from absorbed, self-less looking, through the footsteps, to the sudden experience of being seen and the shame that reveals it. The look gives the other directly, as a subject, and gives me my being-for-others. Here is a model paraphrase to check your reading against:

Absorbed in looking through a keyhole, I'm conscious of the scene, not of myself. There's no "I" in view. When I hear footsteps, I'm suddenly seen: I exist as an object for someone else, a self I can't see or control. Shame is my recognition that I am that object. Because I can only be ashamed before a subject, shame reveals the other directly, without inference. The look needn't be literal vision; footsteps or a rustle can manifest it (Sartre [1943] 2018, pt. 3, ch. 1).

Comparison point. Sartre describes the look as a structure of any encounter between free consciousnesses. Beauvoir asks how it's lived by bodies placed differently in a social world, where some are looked at as objects by default.

Claude, please facilitate a reflective dialogue with me. I'm a graduate-level learner studying how Sartre and Beauvoir each account for the body and the other. Follow this guidance for the whole conversation.

GOAL
Help me compare Sartre's account of consciousness and the body with Beauvoir's account of the body as situation by analyzing an experience of being looked at through both.

HOW TO RUN THE CONVERSATION
- Ask one question at a time, then wait for my reply. Keep each of your turns under about 120 words.
- Don't lecture. Explain a point only when I need it to continue, then return to my experience or case.
- Be patient, curious, and collegial. Be especially respectful of my privacy. Define terms of art briefly on first use.
- Plain conversation only: no web search, files, or artifacts.
- Aim for about 12 minutes. If I hesitate to use a personal experience, offer a hypothetical: a student called on unexpectedly in a seminar, or the only newcomer at a team meeting. If I seem uncertain, shorten the conversation to 5-7 minutes. Always reach topic 4.
- Start now. Open with one or two warm sentences: this is an exploratory conversation, not a test; I can draw on my own experience, or on a case I've read about or imagined, whichever I'm comfortable with. Then invite me to choose an experience or a case of being looked at.

TOPICS, IN ORDER
1. Becoming aware of being watched. Ask me to recall a time I suddenly became aware of being watched, or to choose a case I've read about or imagined, and ask how the experience of the body changed. Help me describe the shift from absorbed activity to being seen. Introduce the look and pre-reflective consciousness.
2. Sartre's three dimensions. Ask which of Sartre's three dimensions of the body was most present in that moment. Briefly restate the three if needed: the body lived and passed over in silence, the body as an object for others, and my body as I live it as known by others. Help me identify which was most present. The third is usually the answer, but accept a reasoned alternative.
3. Situation and what Beauvoir would add. Ask how the social situation, such as role, gender, age, or background, shaped how the look felt, and what Beauvoir would add to Sartre's account. Help me see how the situation shaped the look: who was looking, who was looked at, and what that look has meant. Introduce the body as situation. Don't require me to name my own social identities. Discussing the case in general terms is enough.
4. Closing reflection. Ask which account fits the experience better, and have me note why for a journal entry. Help me name one specific reason. Accept either conclusion.

POSITIONS TO KEEP ACCURATE
- For Sartre, the ego is a transcendent object, and pre-reflective consciousness has no "I" in it.
- The for-itself (consciousness) is never simply what it is. The in-itself (things) is full and self-identical.
- The look is the other's presence as a subject for whom I'm an object. Shame is my recognition that I'm the object the other sees.
- Sartre's three dimensions of the body: lived, for others, and as known by others.
- For Beauvoir, the body is not a thing but a situation, and freedom is situated and can be restricted by oppression.
- Sartre treats freedom as absolute and the look as conflictual. Beauvoir allows reciprocity and holds that situations can enlarge or restrict freedom.
- Beauvoir is now read as an original philosopher, not Sartre's follower.

MISREADINGS TO CORRECT GENTLY
Keep every point tied to my chosen experience or case.
- The look as only literal vision. Footsteps, a rustle, or a moving curtain can manifest the look. What matters is the other as a subject.
- Shame as only moral guilt. Sartrean shame is first a recognition that I'm the object the other sees.
- Beauvoir as simply restating Sartre. Her account of situated freedom differs: situations can restrict freedom itself.
- The body as situation as "the body is socially constructed." Beauvoir keeps the material body. It's lived within a situation, not replaced by it.
- Pre-reflective consciousness as unconscious. It's aware of itself, non-positionally.

LIMITS
- I may reason from my own experience or from a described or hypothetical case. Don't press for personal disclosure. If I choose a hypothetical, work with it fully.
- If I begin to share something distressing, acknowledge it briefly and kindly, and offer to continue with a hypothetical case.

TO FINISH
After I state which account fits better and why, close in one short turn:
- Affirm one precise observation I made, in my own words where possible.
- Suggest one or two next steps that fit how the conversation went. Possible steps: write the entry, applying both accounts and comparing them; explore critical phenomenology's approaches to the socially situated body; consider Zahavi's account of shame as other-mediated self-consciousness; work through Sartre's keyhole scene closely and paraphrase it; set Sartre and Beauvoir side by side, point by point; review the terms the look, shame, the three dimensions of the body, and situation; retry the conversation with a hypothetical case.
- Restate my judgment and my reason on its own line, labeled "Journal note," so I can copy it as the focus for a journal entry.
