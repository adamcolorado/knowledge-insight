Being-in-the-World: Ready-to-Hand and Present-at-Hand

This lesson covers Heidegger's analysis of equipment: how tools show up in use as ready-to-hand, how they become present-at-hand when they break down, and how each tool points to a whole network of purposes. You'll be able to use the distinction to analyze a skilled activity you know well, including its moments of breakdown. This analysis is the basis of influential accounts of skill and expertise and of well-known critiques of symbolic artificial intelligence.

What you will be able to do

  • Describe Dasein's being-in-the-world through the distinction between readiness-to-hand and presence-at-hand

Absorbed Skill, Breakdown, and Dreyfus's Heideggerian Critique of AI

Skilled activity hides its tools. When you type, you don't notice the keyboard. You notice the sentence you're writing. On a familiar drive, your feet work the pedals while your attention is on the traffic, or on nothing in particular. The keyboard shows up when a key sticks. The brake pedal shows up when it sinks farther than it should.

Martin Heidegger made this ordinary pattern the basis of an argument about what a world is (Heidegger [1927] 2008, §§15–16). On his account, we first meet things not as objects with properties but as equipment in use, things for doing something. Their properties come into view mainly when the doing is disrupted. If that's right, a description of skilled activity that starts from objects and their properties has started in the wrong place.

The claim has had a long research life, largely through the philosopher Hubert Dreyfus. With his brother Stuart, an engineer, he developed a model of skill acquisition in which a novice follows explicit rules, while an expert responds directly to the situation, often without being able to state the rules at all (Dreyfus and Dreyfus 1986). Expertise, on this model, isn't rule-following done faster. It's a different kind of engagement.

Dreyfus drew a sharper conclusion about artificial intelligence. Symbolic AI represented the world as facts and rules for manipulating them. Dreyfus argued that such systems couldn't capture the background of practical understanding that lets a skilled agent see what's relevant in a situation (Dreyfus 1992). He later argued that even approaches calling themselves "Heideggerian," which tried to replace representations with situated behavior, had failed. Fixing them, he claimed, would mean taking embodiment and absorbed coping more seriously still (Dreyfus 2007). His critiques were controversial, and AI has changed a great deal since, but the questions they raised about skill and relevance haven't gone away.

The same analysis matters for work on skill learning and for the design of tools and interfaces. In skill learning, it asks what changes when a learner's attention moves from the rules to the situation, and why an expert who stops to think about technique often performs worse. In design, a good tool, on this view, is one you don't notice while working, and a design flaw is often a breakdown waiting to happen. In each field, the distinction between a tool in use and a tool as an object gives you a precise way to describe absorbed, skilled activity, and to see what a rules-and-representations account leaves out.

Equipment, the In-Order-To, and Readiness-to-Hand in Being and Time

Introduction

Heidegger's most famous example is a hammer. He uses it to argue that we don't first encounter things as objects with properties, but as things for doing something, each one pointing to others.

This reading explains equipment, the in-order-to, readiness-to-hand, and the chain of references that ends in Dasein's own purposes. Citations to Being and Time give the section (§) and the German page (H.), which both English translations print in the margin.

Equipment and the In-Order-To

Heidegger begins his analysis of the world with what we deal with every day (Heidegger [1927] 2008, §15). His name for these things is equipment (Zeug): things encountered in use, as something in-order-to do something. The German word covers tools, instruments, clothing, furniture, and vehicles: anything we use.

Equipment has a structure that ordinary objects, considered as bearers of properties, don't. It points beyond itself. The in-order-to is the reference by which an item of equipment points beyond itself to what it's for and to other equipment. A pen is for writing. It refers to ink, paper, a desk, a lamp, and a room.

So Heidegger claims that, strictly speaking, there is no such thing as "an" equipment (§15, H. 68). A single item of equipment only is what it is within a whole of other equipment, which comes first. You don't assemble a workshop from isolated tools. You encounter each tool as part of a workshop.

Readiness-to-Hand

How does equipment show up when it's being used? Heidegger's answer is the hammer (§15, H. 69). When you hammer, you don't attend to the hammer. You attend to the work: the nail, the board, getting the shelf up. The hammer withdraws. And yet it's in hammering, not in staring at it, that the hammer is encountered most genuinely as what it is.

Heidegger calls this mode of being ready-to-hand: the way equipment is in use, withdrawn from attention and understood through what it's for. The noun is "readiness-to-hand" (Zuhandenheit).

Using equipment isn't blind. It's guided by a kind of practical sight that Heidegger calls circumspection (Umsicht): the sight that guides use, directed at the task and its context rather than at the tool. A carpenter's circumspection takes in the board, the angle, and the next nail, while the hammer stays in the background.

From Tool to Purpose

The references don't stop at the next tool. They form a chain that ends in something that isn't equipment at all (§18). The outline's example can be traced step by step.

  1. The hammer is in-order-to drive nails.
  2. The nails are in-order-to fasten boards.
  3. The boards are in-order-to build a house.
  4. The house is for shelter against bad weather.
  5. Shelter is for the sake of Dasein: a possibility of its own being, such as living in a home.

The whole of these interlocking references is the referential totality: the whole of interlocking in-order-to relations among equipment, within which any single tool is what it is. The chain ends in a for-the-sake-of-which (Worumwillen): a possibility of Dasein's own being, for the sake of which the activity is carried out (§18, H. 84).

The last step matters most. The network of equipment is held together by Dasein's purposes. Take away the for-the-sake-of-which, and the chain loses its point. A hammer in a world where no one builds anything is no longer a hammer in the full sense.

Conclusion

For Heidegger, we first meet things as equipment: in-order-to do something and belonging to a whole of other equipment. In use, equipment is ready-to-hand. It withdraws from attention while circumspection guides the task. Its references form a referential totality that ends in a for-the-sake-of-which, a possibility of Dasein's own being.

Key Terms

  • Equipment (Zeug): In Heidegger, things encountered in use, as something in-order-to do something. An item of equipment always belongs to a whole of other equipment.
  • In-order-to: In Heidegger, the reference by which an item of equipment points beyond itself to what it's for and to other equipment.
  • Ready-to-hand: In Heidegger, the way equipment is in use: withdrawn from attention and understood through what it's for. The noun is "readiness-to-hand" (Zuhandenheit).
  • Circumspection (Umsicht): In Heidegger, the practical sight that guides use, directed at the task and its context rather than at the tool.
  • Referential totality: In Heidegger, the whole of interlocking in-order-to relations among equipment, within which any single tool is what it is.
  • For-the-sake-of-which (Worumwillen): In Heidegger, the end point of the chain of references: a possibility of Dasein's own being, for the sake of which an activity is carried out.

Breakdown and Presence-at-Hand: Conspicuousness, Obtrusiveness, and Obstinacy

Introduction

If equipment withdraws in use, how does it ever come into view? Heidegger's answer is that it shows up when use is disrupted, and that the way it shows up depends on how the disruption happens.

This reading explains his three modes of disruption, the shift from readiness-to-hand to presence-at-hand, and his claim that the theoretical stance derives from that shift. Citations to Being and Time give the section (§) and the German page (H.).

Three Ways Use Goes Wrong

In §16, Heidegger describes three ways in which equipment can fail us (Heidegger [1927] 2008, §16, H. 72–74). Commentators, following Hubert Dreyfus, call these disruptions breakdowns (Dreyfus 1991). A breakdown is the disruption of absorbed use, when equipment is damaged, missing, or in the way, and the tool and its context become explicit.

  • Conspicuousness (Auffälligkeit) is the mode of breakdown in which equipment is damaged or unusable, so it stands out. The hammerhead flies off, or the pen runs dry. The tool is still there, but it no longer works.
  • Obtrusiveness (Aufdringlichkeit) is the mode in which equipment is missing, so its absence obtrudes. You reach for the hammer and it isn't there. What's missing becomes insistently noticeable because the work can't go on.
  • Obstinacy (Aufsässigkeit) is the mode in which something that isn't damaged or missing stands in the way of what you're doing. A box blocks the doorway you need, or a notification keeps covering the text you're editing.

In each case, something that had withdrawn now stands out.

What Breakdown Reveals

In each mode, the tool loses its readiness-to-hand, and it starts to show up differently. It becomes an object with properties: this hammer, with a loose head and a certain weight. Heidegger's name for this mode of being is present-at-hand: the way a thing shows up when regarded as a bare object with properties, detached from use. The noun is "presence-at-hand" (Vorhandenheit). Stambaugh and Schmidt translate it as "objective presence" (Heidegger [1927] 2010).

But more than the tool comes into view. When the hammer breaks, the job it was for becomes explicit too: the board, the house, the whole point of the work. In breakdown, Heidegger says, the context of references that had been taken for granted lights up, and with it the world (§16, H. 74–75). The broken tool is only the occasion. What's disclosed is the referential totality in which it had its place.

The Theoretical Stance

Presence-at-hand isn't limited to breakdowns. It's also the stance of theory. When you study a hammer as a physicist would, you look at it as a bare object with mass, shape, and hardness, stripped of its place in any activity.

Heidegger's claim is that this stance is derivative. Knowing, in the theoretical sense, is a founded mode of being-in-the-world (§13, H. 61). It arises when involved dealing with things is held back, and things are regarded as simply present. Theory, on this view, isn't wrong. It's a legitimate, powerful way of relating to beings. But it isn't the original way, and an ontology built on it will miss what things are in use.

This reverses a long tradition. Many philosophers took the object with properties as basic and treated use and value as layers added on top. Heidegger holds that the order is the other way around.

Conclusion

Equipment comes into view when use is disrupted: as conspicuous when damaged, obtrusive when missing, and obstinate when in the way. In breakdown, the tool becomes present-at-hand, and the context of references it belonged to lights up. The theoretical stance of presence-at-hand is, for Heidegger, derivative of involved use.

Key Terms

  • Breakdown: The disruption of absorbed use, when equipment is damaged, missing, or in the way, and the tool and its context become explicit.
  • Conspicuousness (Auffälligkeit): In Heidegger, the mode of breakdown in which equipment is damaged or unusable, so it stands out.
  • Obtrusiveness (Aufdringlichkeit): In Heidegger, the mode of breakdown in which equipment is missing, so its absence obtrudes.
  • Obstinacy (Aufsässigkeit): In Heidegger, the mode of breakdown in which something that isn't damaged or missing stands in the way of what you're doing.
  • Present-at-hand: In Heidegger, the way a thing shows up when regarded as a bare object with properties, detached from use. The noun is "presence-at-hand" (Vorhandenheit), translated "objective presence" by Stambaugh and Schmidt.

Worldhood, Significance, and Heidegger's Critique of Descartes's World

Introduction

"World" usually means everything there is, or the space that contains it. Heidegger means something different: the web of involvements in which things can matter at all.

This reading explains worldhood and significance, and Heidegger's argument that Descartes's world of extended things misses them. Citations to Being and Time give the section (§) and the German page (H.).

The World as a Web of Involvements

For Heidegger, the world isn't a container of objects. Worldhood, in his sense, is what makes the world a world: the structure of significance within which beings can show up as mattering (Heidegger [1927] 2008, §18).

The pieces of that structure are relations. A ready-to-hand thing has an involvement (Bewandtnis): it has its place in a context of use, involved in something, for something. The hammer has its involvement in hammering, hammering in fastening, fastening in protection against bad weather, and all of it for the sake of Dasein's being.

Heidegger calls the whole relational structure significance (Bedeutsamkeit): the whole of in-order-to, towards-which, and for-the-sake-of-which relations that makes up the structure of the world (§18, H. 87). Dasein is always already familiar with significance. That familiarity isn't a theory of the world. It's what lets you walk into a kitchen and see a knife as something to cut with, without inferring anything.

So when Heidegger says Dasein is being-in-the-world, the world in question is this web, not the physical universe. A physicist and a carpenter can stand in the same room and dwell in very different worlds.

The Two Modes Compared

Heidegger's distinction can be set out in four respects.

Ready-to-handPresent-at-hand
Mode of accessUsing, guided by circumspectionObserving, measuring, theorizing
AttentionOn the task; the tool withdrawsOn the thing itself, as an object
PropertiesShow up as serviceability: too heavy for this jobShow up as bare properties: weight 0.5 kg
ExampleThe hammer in hammeringThe hammer on a scale in a lab

The weight row makes the point concrete. In use, "too heavy" isn't a property of the hammer alone. It's a relation between the hammer, the task, and the one using it. Only in the present-at-hand stance does weight become a number that belongs to the object by itself.

Heidegger's Critique of Descartes

Heidegger argues that the tradition passed over worldhood. His main example is Descartes (§§19–21). Descartes took the world to consist of bodies whose essence is extension (Descartes [1641] 2017). On that view, the real properties of things are geometrical and measurable. Descartes calls such a body extended substance (res extensa): in his philosophy, material substance, whose essence is extension in length, breadth, and depth. Anything else about bodies, such as color, value, or usefulness, is secondary, added by the mind.

Heidegger's critique, at overview level, runs in three steps.

  1. Descartes starts from one mode of being, presence-at-hand, and treats it as the being of the world as such.
  2. As a result, he can't account for worldhood. Significance becomes a layer of value painted onto neutral objects.
  3. But that gets the order wrong. Presence-at-hand is derivative of involved dealings with things. The painted-on picture explains the original by the derivative.

Heidegger doesn't deny that the mathematical treatment of nature discovers something real. His claim is ontological: it can't be the starting point for understanding what a world is.

Conclusion

For Heidegger, the world is a web of involvements, with significance as its structure, not a container of objects. Ready-to-hand and present-at-hand differ in access, attention, and how properties show up. Descartes's world of extended things treats presence-at-hand as basic, and so it misses worldhood.

Key Terms

  • Worldhood: In Heidegger, what makes the world a world: the structure of significance within which beings can show up as mattering.
  • Involvement (Bewandtnis): In Heidegger, the way something ready-to-hand has its place in a context of use, involved in something, for something.
  • Significance (Bedeutsamkeit): In Heidegger, the whole of in-order-to, towards-which, and for-the-sake-of-which relations that makes up the structure of the world.
  • Extended substance (res extensa): In Descartes, material substance, whose essence is extension in length, breadth, and depth.

Absorbed Coping: Dreyfus, McDowell, and the Tool Terms in Translation

Introduction

Much of the English-language uptake of Heidegger's tool analysis runs through one book: Hubert Dreyfus's commentary on Division I of Being and Time. It turned the analysis into a theory of skill, and that theory drew one of the best-known debates in recent philosophy of mind.

This reading explains Dreyfus's reading of "absorbed coping," John McDowell's main objection to it, and the tool terms as they appear in the two English translations. Citations to Being and Time give the section (§) and the German page (H.).

Dreyfus and Absorbed Coping

Dreyfus reads the tool analysis as an account of skilled activity (Dreyfus 1991). His key term is absorbed coping: skilled, fluid activity that responds to the situation without explicit rules, deliberation, or mental representations. A skilled carpenter doesn't consult a rule for each stroke. The situation solicits the right response, and the carpenter responds.

On Dreyfus's reading, absorbed coping is our most basic way of being-in-the-world. It rests on a background of shared practices that can never be fully spelled out as rules or beliefs. Explicit, rule-governed thought, like presence-at-hand, arises mainly when coping breaks down.

The reading was enormously influential. It shaped work on skill and expertise, informed the critique of symbolic artificial intelligence, and helped bring Heidegger into cognitive science. It's also contested as an interpretation. Some scholars think it plays down Heidegger's interest in understanding and interpretation. Others think it plays down Division II.

The McDowell–Dreyfus Debate

The main philosophical objection came from John McDowell. In the debate that followed, collected in a volume edited by Joseph Schear, the question was whether absorbed coping is free of concepts (Schear 2013).

  • Dreyfus argued that absorbed coping is non-conceptual. Treating it as conceptual imposes a "myth of the mental" on it, reading reflective rationality back into skilled activity.
  • McDowell argued that in mature human beings, conceptual capacities are at work even in absorbed coping. They aren't a detached layer switched on only when coping breaks down. The expert's responsiveness is itself a form of rationality, even when nothing is said or thought explicitly.

The debate left both sides with refined positions rather than a winner. It remains a reference point for work on skill, perception, and the reach of conceptual capacities.

The noun forms follow the adjectives. Macquarrie and Robinson's "readiness-to-hand" and "presence-at-hand" become "handiness" and "objective presence" in Stambaugh and Schmidt. Dreyfus follows Macquarrie and Robinson, so most English commentary does too.

Conclusion

Dreyfus read the tool analysis as an account of absorbed coping: skilled activity without rules or representations, resting on a background of practices. McDowell objected that conceptual capacities are at work even in absorbed coping, and the debate remains open. Readers moving between translations should expect "handiness" and "objective presence" in place of "readiness-to-hand" and "presence-at-hand."

Guided Close Reading: The Hammer, Circumspection, and Breakdown in Being and Time §§15–16

Introduction

The hammer passage takes up less than a page of Being and Time, and the breakdown analysis only a few more. Together they carry much of Heidegger's case that we first meet things in use, not as objects. This reading follows both passages move by move. It ends with a model analysis of one breakdown that you can check your own analyses against.

Locating the Passage

The hammer passage is in §15, at H. 69. The breakdown analysis is in §16, at H. 72–76 (Heidegger [1927] 2008, §15, H. 69; §16, H. 72–76). Both English translations print the German page numbers in the margin, so you can follow along in either. Key phrases below are quoted from Macquarrie and Robinson. Where the translations differ, see the terms table under Key Considerations.

No free English translation is available. The free Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Heidegger summarizes the tool analysis (Wrathall 2025).

Before you start, note the setting. Heidegger has just said that the phenomenological task is to describe the beings we deal with every day, as we deal with them. He warns that the obvious starting point, "things" understood as bearers of properties, already carries an interpretation.

Walking Through the Argument

Step 1: The hammer is most itself in hammering

Heidegger claims that the hammer is encountered most genuinely not when you examine it, but when you use it. In his phrasing, the less we "stare at the hammer-Thing" and the more we take hold of it and use it, the more original our relation to it becomes, and the more it's encountered as what it is: equipment (§15, H. 69).

Notice the reversal. We usually assume that looking closely reveals a thing best. Heidegger says that for equipment, use reveals it best, because what equipment is is its in-order-to. Hammering discloses the hammer's "manipulability" in a way no inspection can.

Step 2: Looking "theoretically" misses this

Heidegger then turns to the look that merely inspects. However sharply you look at the hammer's shape and properties, you won't find its readiness-to-hand that way (§15, H. 69). Inspection sees an object with properties. It doesn't see what the hammer is for.

He adds a striking point: in use, the ready-to-hand must, as it were, "withdraw" (H. 69). What's in focus is the work to be produced, not the tool. The tool is most itself when it's least noticed.

Step 3: Circumspection is the sight proper to use

That doesn't mean use is blind. Heidegger insists that dealing with equipment has its own kind of sight, which he calls circumspection (Umsicht), literally "looking around" (H. 69). Circumspection takes in the task and its context: the nail, the board, the angle of the next stroke. It guides use without making the tool an object of attention.

This step matters for everything that follows. Readiness-to-hand isn't the absence of awareness. It's a different kind of awareness, directed at the work rather than the tool.

Step 4: Three modes of breakdown each reveal the world differently

In §16 Heidegger asks how the world itself ever comes into view, if everything in use withdraws. His answer is that it comes into view when use is disturbed (§16, H. 72–74).

  • Conspicuousness. The tool is damaged or unusable. It stands out as a thing with a defect: this hammer, with its loose head.
  • Obtrusiveness. The tool is missing. The more urgently we need it, the more its absence obtrudes, and the more what remains shows up as merely present.
  • Obstinacy. Something undamaged and not missing stands in the way. It becomes something we have to deal with before we can go on.

In each mode, the ready-to-hand shows a presence-at-hand that was there all along. And in each, the disturbance lights up the context of references: what the tool was for, and the work as a whole (§16, H. 74–75).

Why Breakdown Lights Up the Whole Context

Why does a broken hammer reveal more than a broken hammer? Dreyfus's reading makes the logic explicit (Dreyfus 1991). In smooth coping, the whole referential totality is at work but in the background. When one reference fails, the activity stops, and you're thrown back on what the activity was for. You notice not just the hammer but the unfinished shelf, the books waiting for it, and the afternoon you'd set aside. The breakdown is local, but what it reveals is the context. That's why Heidegger treats it as a way the world announces itself.

A common misreading. The most common misreading equates ready-to-hand with "unconscious." On this reading, when the hammer withdraws, you're simply unaware of it, like a reflex. That gets the passage wrong in two ways.

  • It ignores circumspection. Heidegger says use has its own sight. You're aware of the task and its context, and the tool is present within that awareness without being its focus.
  • It makes the distinction psychological. Readiness-to-hand and presence-at-hand are ways of being, ways things show up, not levels of attention in a mind. A tool can be ready-to-hand for a carpenter who's sharply aware of the work.

Ready-to-hand means withdrawn from focal attention and understood through what it's for. It doesn't mean unconscious.

Summary

The hammer passage argues that equipment is encountered most genuinely in use, where it withdraws and circumspection guides the task. The breakdown analysis shows how it comes into view: as conspicuous, obtrusive, or obstinate. In each case, the context of references lights up. Here is a model analysis of one breakdown episode:

Episode. I'm working through a data analysis on my laptop in a café. The trackpad, the spreadsheet, and the charts are ready-to-hand: I attend to the pattern in the numbers, not to the tools. My circumspection takes in which column to sort next. Breakdown. The battery warning appears, and I realize my charger is at home. Mode. Obtrusiveness: the charger is missing, and its absence becomes more insistent the lower the battery drops. What it revealed. The laptop suddenly shows up present-at-hand, as a device with a draining battery. More than that, the whole context lights up: the report due tomorrow, the colleague waiting on the charts, and the fact that the afternoon's work was for the sake of being a reliable collaborator (Heidegger [1927] 2008, §§15–16).

GOAL
Help me describe Dasein's being-in-the-world through the distinction between readiness-to-hand and presence-at-hand by analyzing the equipment of a skilled activity I practice, including a moment of breakdown.

HOW TO RUN THE CONVERSATION
- Ask one question at a time, then wait for my reply. Keep each of your turns under about 120 words.
- Don't lecture. Explain a point only when I need it to continue, then return to my activity.
- Be patient, curious, and collegial. Encourage concrete, sensory detail about the activity. Define terms of art briefly on first use.
- Plain conversation only: no web search, files, or artifacts.
- Aim for about 12 minutes. If I can't recall a breakdown, suggest an everyday one, such as a dead pen, a missing ingredient, or a frozen screen. If I seem uncertain, shorten the conversation to 5-7 minutes. Always reach topic 4.
- Start now. Open with one or two warm sentences: this is an exploratory conversation, not a test; any skill works, from cooking to coding; I can ask you to explain any term. Then ask me to name an activity I do skillfully.

TOPICS, IN ORDER
1. The activity going well. Ask me to name an activity I do skillfully, such as cooking, running, writing, playing an instrument, or coding. Ask what tools are involved, and whether I notice them while it's going well. Help me notice that, while the activity goes well, I attend to the task, not the tools. Introduce equipment and readiness-to-hand. Point out that I'm still aware, but of the task: this is circumspection.
2. The chain of purposes. Ask what each tool points to: in-order-to what, and for the sake of what? Help me trace at least two in-order-to relations, from tool to tool to task, and then to a for-the-sake-of-which: a possibility of my own being, such as being a good cook, a runner, or a reliable colleague. Introduce the referential totality.
3. A breakdown. Ask me to describe a time one of these tools broke down, went missing, or got in the way, and what became visible. Help me name the mode: damaged or unusable is conspicuousness, missing is obtrusiveness, and in the way is obstinacy. Ask how the tool showed up once it failed, drawing out the shift to presence-at-hand.
4. Closing reflection. Ask what the breakdown revealed about the whole activity, and have me note it for a journal entry. Help me see that the breakdown lit up more than the tool: the task, what it was for, and the people or deadlines involved.

POSITIONS TO KEEP ACCURATE
- Equipment is encountered in use, as in-order-to something, and always belongs to a whole of other equipment.
- Ready-to-hand equipment withdraws from attention in use. Circumspection guides use by attending to the task.
- The in-order-to relations form a referential totality that ends in a for-the-sake-of-which, a possibility of Dasein's own being.
- Breakdown comes in three modes: conspicuousness (damaged), obtrusiveness (missing), and obstinacy (in the way).
- In breakdown, the tool becomes present-at-hand and the wider context lights up.
- Presence-at-hand, including the theoretical stance, is derivative of involved use.
- Dreyfus reads this as absorbed coping. McDowell argues conceptual capacities are at work even there, and the debate is open.

MISREADINGS TO CORRECT GENTLY
Keep every point tied to my own activity.
- Ready-to-hand as "unconscious." Use has its own sight, circumspection. I'm aware of the task, not unaware.
- Ready-to-hand as a property of certain objects. It's a way things show up in use. The same hammer can be ready-to-hand or present-at-hand.
- Present-at-hand as bad or false. It's a legitimate stance, including the stance of science, but derivative, not original.
- Breakdown as only about the broken tool. Breakdown lights up the wider context of references.
- Mode confusion. If I misname a mode, restate the mapping: damaged is conspicuousness, missing is obtrusiveness, and in the way is obstinacy.

TO FINISH
After I say what the breakdown revealed, close in one short turn:
- Affirm one precise observation I made, in my own words where possible.
- Suggest one or two next steps that fit how the conversation went. Possible steps: write the journal entry, tracing the referential totality and analyzing the breakdown; consider whether McDowell's objection applies to my own skill; explore how Dreyfus used the analysis in his critique of symbolic AI; review circumspection and the three modes of breakdown; reread Heidegger's hammer passage; review the terms equipment, in-order-to, ready-to-hand, and present-at-hand; retry the conversation with a simpler activity, such as making coffee.
