Flesh and Chiasm: Flesh, Reversibility, Chiasm

This lesson explains the central concepts of "The Intertwining—The Chiasm": flesh as an "element" that is neither matter nor mind, the reversibility of touching and touched, and the chiasm, a crossing-over of perceiver and perceived. You'll be able to describe these concepts using Merleau-Ponty's examples and your own experience of touching and seeing. These concepts are widely used in current relational, ecological, and enactive accounts of perception.

What you will be able to do

  • Describe flesh, reversibility, and the chiasm as concepts for the entanglement of perceiver and perceived

Perceiver and World Entangled: Ecological and Enactive Accounts of Perception

When your hand rests on a table, you feel the table's smooth, cool surface. You can also feel your hand pressed by the table. The same contact can be lived as touching or as being touched, depending on where your attention falls. And when you look at a room, you see it from a place in it. Anyone else in the room can see you looking.

These are ordinary facts, but many theories of perception set them aside. A common model places the perceiver outside the world perceived. Signals travel from objects to sense organs, the brain processes them, and a representation of the world results. On this model, the perceiver's own body appears only as an input channel. That the perceiver is also a perceptible thing, touched as it touches and visible as it sees, plays no role in the explanation.

Two research traditions have questioned this model. Enactive approaches to mind, developed in the work of Evan Thompson and others, treat perception as something an organism does in its world. Perception isn't the reception of information by a detached system. It's a form of skilled engagement, in which the organism and its environment shape each other (Thompson 2007). Ecological approaches in philosophy go further. David Abram argues that perception is a reciprocal exchange between a sensing body and a sensuous world, and that modern thought has lost sight of this exchange by treating the world as inert material for a knowing mind (Abram 1996).

Both traditions draw on Maurice Merleau-Ponty's late work, and especially on a single chapter of his unfinished book The Visible and the Invisible. There he argues that the perceiving body and the perceived world are made of the same "flesh" (Merleau-Ponty [1964] 1968, ch. 4). The hand that touches can be touched, and the eye that sees is visible. Perceiver and perceived cross into each other, in a relation he calls the chiasm, but they never fully merge.

For a researcher, these concepts supply a vocabulary for describing perceiver and world as entangled rather than separated. That vocabulary matters for questions a detached model finds hard to state: how perceiving depends on being perceptible, how skilled action changes what an environment offers, and how studying perception from outside can miss the perceiver's place in the world. The concepts are demanding, and their interpretation is still debated. They are also among the most widely used ideas from phenomenology in current relational and ecological work on mind.

Flesh as Element and the Visibility of the Seer

Introduction

"Flesh" is the best-known term of Merleau-Ponty's late philosophy, and the most often misread. It doesn't mean the body's tissue, and it doesn't name a stuff out of which things are made.

This reading explains flesh as an "element" and the claim that the seer is visible. Citations to The Visible and the Invisible give the chapter, or "Working Notes" with the note's date.

Flesh Is Not Matter, Mind, or Substance

In the fourth chapter of The Visible and the Invisible, "The Intertwining—The Chiasm," Merleau-Ponty introduces flesh (chair): in Merleau-Ponty's late ontology, what the perceiving body and the perceived world share, neither matter nor mind nor substance, but the general visibility and tangibility in which both take part (Merleau-Ponty [1964] 1968, ch. 4).

He says what flesh isn't before he says what it is. It isn't matter, because matter is what physics describes: bodies in objective space, with no perceptibility of their own. It isn't mind, because flesh belongs to the visible and tangible world, not to a consciousness facing it. And it isn't substance, a thing that exists in itself and has properties. The three denials remove the three traditional candidates for what underlies experience.

Flesh as Element

To name what flesh is, Merleau-Ponty reaches for an old word. Flesh, he says, is an "element," in the sense in which early Greek thinkers spoke of water, air, earth, and fire. An element, in this sense, is a general manner of being, between the individual thing and the abstract idea, that is present wherever there is something of its kind.

Water in this sense isn't this glass of water or the concept of water. It's a way of being found in every pool, cloud, and drop. Flesh is similarly general. It's the way of being that anything visible or tangible has, including the seeing and touching body. That is why Merleau-Ponty can speak of the flesh of the body and the flesh of the world without claiming that the world is alive or conscious. The shift here is from asking what things are made of to asking what way of being they share.

The Seer Is Visible

The most direct evidence for flesh is the body's double status. I see the world, and I am visible in it. I touch things, and I can be touched. The body is a sentient–sensible: in Merleau-Ponty, a being that both perceives and is perceptible, seeing and visible, touching and tangible.

Merleau-Ponty draws a strong conclusion from this. Vision isn't a view from outside the visible. It happens from within the visible: in Merleau-Ponty's late writing, the perceptible world as seen, which includes the seeing body itself. The seer can see the visible only because the seer belongs to it. If my body weren't itself a visible thing among visible things, placed in the world with them, it couldn't see them.

This reverses a familiar order. A detached model treats being visible as irrelevant to seeing, a fact about the perceiver's body that the theory of vision can ignore. For Merleau-Ponty, it's a condition of seeing. The seer's visibility is what places the seer in the world it sees.

Conclusion

Flesh is neither matter, nor mind, nor substance. It's an element in the old sense: a general way of being shared by the perceiving body and the perceived world. The body is a sentient–sensible, both seeing and visible, and vision happens from within the visible, because the seer belongs to it.

Key Terms

  • Flesh (chair): In Merleau-Ponty's late ontology, what the perceiving body and the perceived world share, neither matter nor mind nor substance, but the general visibility and tangibility in which both take part.
  • Element: In the old sense Merleau-Ponty revives, a general manner of being, between the individual thing and the abstract idea, that is present wherever there is something of its kind.
  • Sentient–sensible: In Merleau-Ponty, a being that both perceives and is perceptible, seeing and visible, touching and tangible.
  • The visible: In Merleau-Ponty's late writing, the perceptible world as seen, which includes the seeing body itself.

Reversibility and the Chiasm: From Husserl's Touching Hands to Merleau-Ponty

Introduction

Merleau-Ponty's late ontology grows from a simple experiment: one hand touching the other. Husserl had used the experiment to show what makes a body lived. Merleau-Ponty uses it to show what makes perception possible at all.

This reading explains Husserl's source, Merleau-Ponty's account of reversibility, and the chiasm. Husserl citations give the section number; citations to The Visible and the Invisible give the chapter.

Husserl's Touching Hands

In Ideas II, Husserl describes touching one hand with the other (Husserl [1952] 1989, §36). The experience is a double sensation: the experience of one hand touching the other, in which each hand can be felt as touching or as touched. When my left hand touches my right, the right hand is a thing touched, with a surface and a texture. It's also a place where I feel the touch.

Husserl's point concerns the body. The touched hand carries sensings (Empfindnisse): sensations localized in the lived body and felt there, such as the touch-sensations in a hand, as opposed to properties of the object touched. Only my own body carries sensings this way. So the double sensation is how the lived body is given as mine, distinct from every other thing.

Reversibility

Merleau-Ponty takes up the same experiment and draws a different conclusion (Merleau-Ponty [1964] 1968, ch. 4). What interests him is that the roles can switch. The touching hand can become the touched, and the touched can become the touching. He calls this reversibility: in Merleau-Ponty, the relation by which touching and touched, or seeing and seen, can exchange roles, a relation always imminent but never fully realized.

The last clause is essential. I never feel both roles at once. When I try to catch my right hand in the act of touching things while my left hand touches it, the right hand's touching fades just as I reach it. The reversal is always about to happen and never completes.

Merleau-Ponty extends reversibility beyond touch. The seer is visible, and in principle can be seen by others, or catch sight of itself. He also extends it to the handshake, where I touch another's hand and am touched by it. Reversibility isn't a curious fact about hands. It's the general structure of perception, and it holds wherever perceiver and perceived are both flesh.

The Chiasm

The result is the chiasm: in Merleau-Ponty, a crossing-over and exchange between perceiver and perceived, body and world, or self and other, in which each passes into the other without merging (Toadvine 2023, §6). The word comes from the Greek letter chi (Χ), whose strokes cross. Merleau-Ponty also calls the relation an intertwining.

The table renders the crossing.

From the side of the perceiverCrosses intoFrom the side of the perceived
The hand that touches⟷The hand, or thing, that is touched
The eye that sees⟷The visible, including the seer
My body⟷The world, made of the same flesh
My perceiving of the other⟷The other perceiving me

At the center of the crossing, where the two strokes of the Χ meet, there's a gap. The two sides pass into each other, but they never coincide. That gap keeps perceiver and perceived distinct even as they intertwine.

How Merleau-Ponty Radicalizes Husserl

The difference from Husserl is a difference in what the experiment shows. For Husserl, the double sensation marks off the lived body from the rest of nature: my body alone carries sensings. For Merleau-Ponty, reversibility shows that the body and the world are of the same kind. The touching hand can be touched because it belongs to the tangible world. The experiment reveals a kinship between body and world, not a boundary.

Conclusion

Husserl used the touching hands to show how the lived body is given as mine. Merleau-Ponty uses them to show reversibility, which is always imminent and never complete, and which extends to vision and to other people. The chiasm is the crossing of perceiver and perceived, which intertwine without merging.

Key Terms

  • Double sensation: The experience of one hand touching the other, in which each hand can be felt as touching or as touched.
  • Sensings (Empfindnisse): Sensations localized in the lived body and felt there, such as the touch-sensations in a hand, as opposed to properties of the object touched.
  • Reversibility: In Merleau-Ponty, the relation by which touching and touched, or seeing and seen, can exchange roles, a relation always imminent but never fully realized.
  • Chiasm: In Merleau-Ponty, a crossing-over and exchange between perceiver and perceived, body and world, or self and other, in which each passes into the other without merging.

Divergence (Écart) and the Hinge: Why Reversibility Is Never Complete

Introduction

If perceiver and perceived are made of the same flesh, what keeps them apart? Merleau-Ponty's answer is a gap that belongs to the structure of perception itself.

This reading explains divergence (écart) and the hinge, and works through three examples. Citations give the chapter, or "Working Notes" with the note's date.

Why Reversibility Stays Incomplete

Reversibility, in Merleau-Ponty, is the relation by which touching and touched, or seeing and seen, can exchange roles, a relation always imminent but never fully realized. The question is why it's never fully realized.

A tempting answer treats the incompleteness as a limitation: we'd like to feel both roles at once, but our nervous system doesn't allow it. Merleau-Ponty rejects this. The incompleteness isn't a failure of an attempt at fusion. It's what makes perception possible. If touching and touched ever coincided, there would be no one touching anything. There would only be one undivided thing, and perception needs a perceiver and a perceived that are distinct, even when they're made of the same flesh.

Divergence (Écart)

Merleau-Ponty names the gap divergence (écart): in Merleau-Ponty, the gap or spacing that keeps perceiver and perceived, touching and touched, distinct within their reversibility, so that they never coincide (Merleau-Ponty [1964] 1968, ch. 4; Toadvine 2023, §6).

Écart can also be translated as "gap," "spread," or "deviation." Divergence isn't empty space between two things. It's a difference within one fabric, like a fold in cloth. The fold makes two surfaces out of one material, which can touch each other precisely because they are one cloth folded.

The Hinge

In the working notes, Merleau-Ponty describes the point where the two sides turn into each other as a hinge (charnière): in Merleau-Ponty, the point of articulation at which perceiver and perceived, or inside and outside, turn into each other, joined but not merged (Merleau-Ponty [1964] 1968, Working Notes).

A hinge joins two leaves of a door. They move together, each turning on the same pin, but they remain two leaves. The image fits reversibility. The body turns between touching and touched on one axis, and the two roles are joined at the hinge but never fused.

Three Examples

The concepts are easiest to grasp in cases. Each is an instance of the chiasm: in Merleau-Ponty, a crossing-over and exchange between perceiver and perceived, body and world, or self and other, in which each passes into the other without merging. Each also shows the divergence within it.

  1. Touching one's own hand. My left hand touches my right. The roles can reverse, and I can attend to the right hand as touching. But I never feel both at once. The divergence is the moment in which one role gives way to the other.
  2. Shaking another's hand. I touch another person's hand and am touched by it. The handshake is reversible, since each of us touches and is touched. But I never feel the other's touching as they feel it. Divergence here keeps self and other distinct, even in a shared bodily exchange.
  3. Seeing oneself in a mirror. I see my own face. The seer is visible, and here visible to itself. But I never catch myself in the act of seeing. My image looks back without being the seeing I am doing. The mirror shows the seer's visibility and the divergence within it at once.

Conclusion

Reversibility is never complete, and that's not a limitation. It's a structure. Divergence (écart) keeps perceiver and perceived distinct within one flesh, like a fold in cloth. The hinge is the point at which the two sides turn into each other without merging. Touching one's hand, shaking another's hand, and seeing oneself in a mirror all show the same structure.

Key Terms

  • Reversibility: In Merleau-Ponty, the relation by which touching and touched, or seeing and seen, can exchange roles, a relation always imminent but never fully realized.
  • Divergence (écart): In Merleau-Ponty, the gap or spacing that keeps perceiver and perceived, touching and touched, distinct within their reversibility, so that they never coincide.
  • Hinge (charnière): In Merleau-Ponty, the point of articulation at which perceiver and perceived, or inside and outside, turn into each other, joined but not merged.
  • Chiasm: In Merleau-Ponty, a crossing-over and exchange between perceiver and perceived, body and world, or self and other, in which each passes into the other without merging.

The Invisible of the Visible and the Concepts of the Late Ontology

Introduction

The title The Visible and the Invisible names a pairing, not an opposition. Merleau-Ponty's "invisible" isn't a second world behind the visible one. It belongs to the visible.

This reading explains the invisible of the visible and gathers the late ontology's concepts in one table. Citations give the chapter.

The Invisible of the Visible

Merleau-Ponty's term for this is the invisible of the visible: in Merleau-Ponty, the structures, meanings, and depth that belong to the visible world and make it visible without being visible things themselves (Merleau-Ponty [1964] 1968, ch. 4).

The invisible isn't what happens to be hidden, like the far side of the moon. That's visible in principle. Nor is it a realm of pure ideas separate from the senses. It's the invisible of this visible world: what the visible carries and depends on without displaying it as one more thing.

Three kinds of example make the idea concrete.

  1. Depth. I see things in depth, some nearer and some farther. But I don't see depth as an object alongside them. Depth is how the visible is arranged, invisible in itself.
  2. Structure. A face is visible. The likeness between a parent's face and a child's is real and can be seen, but it isn't located in either face as a visible feature. It's a relation that the visible carries.
  3. Ideas. Merleau-Ponty discusses a short musical phrase in Proust's novel (Proust [1913] 2003), which a listener grasps as an idea. The idea exists only through its sounds. It can't be separated from them and held on its own, but it isn't one of the sounds either. Ideas of this kind, he suggests, are the invisible lining of the sensible.

Why the Pairing Matters

The invisible of the visible completes the picture of flesh. If perception were only the reception of visible things, the meanings and structures in experience would have to come from somewhere else: from a mind that adds them. On Merleau-Ponty's account, they're already in the visible, as its depth and its lining. There's no need for a consciousness to supply what the flesh of the world already carries. This is one more way the late ontology avoids starting from a subject facing an object.

Divergence keeps them distinct, and the invisible of the visible gives the visible its depth and meaning (Toadvine 2023, §6).

Conclusion

The invisible of the visible isn't a hidden world. It's the depth, structure, and meaning that the visible carries, as a musical idea exists only through its sounds. With flesh, reversibility, the chiasm, the hinge, and divergence, it completes an ontology in which the world isn't given to a consciousness from outside.

Guided Close Reading: The Touching–Touched Hands in "The Intertwining—The Chiasm"

Introduction

The passage on touching hands in "The Intertwining—The Chiasm" is the hinge of Merleau-Ponty's late ontology. It takes an example from Husserl and turns it into the basis for a theory of what perception is. This reading works through the passage in three moves, compares Husserl's use of the example, flags a common misreading, and ends with a model paraphrase.

Locating the Passage

The passage comes in the fourth chapter of The Visible and the Invisible, "The Intertwining—The Chiasm" (Merleau-Ponty [1964] 1968, ch. 4). It follows Merleau-Ponty's discussion of vision and the seer's visibility and precedes his introduction of flesh as an element.

No free English translation is available. The free Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Merleau-Ponty summarizes the chapter (Toadvine 2023, §6). Key phrases below are paraphrased, with French terms given where they matter.

You can follow the passage with your own hands. Before you start, place your left hand on the back of your right hand while your right hand rests on a table.

Walking Through the Argument

Step 1: My left hand touches my right hand touching things

Merleau-Ponty sets up a three-part situation. My right hand touches a thing: a table, say. My left hand touches my right hand. So the right hand is at once touching the table and touched by the left hand.

Notice the question this raises. Can I feel the right hand as touching and as touched at the same moment? Try it. Attend to the table's surface under your right hand, then to the pressure of your left hand on it. The attention moves. The right hand is either the organ that feels the table or the thing that the left hand feels, depending on where attention falls.

Step 2: The roles reverse but never coincide

The second move describes what happens when I try to catch both roles. The right hand can pass from touching to touched, and back. But at the moment I'm about to feel it as both, one role gives way. Merleau-Ponty's claim is that the coincidence is always imminent and never realized. I'm always on the verge of it, and it slips away as I reach it.

The shift here is from a double sensation to a reversibility. The double sensation names two sensations in one hand. Reversibility names a relation in which the roles pass into each other, but always with a delay or a gap.

Step 3: The gap is not a failure but the structure of perception

The third move draws the conclusion. One might think the failure to coincide is a defect: we'd like to have both roles at once and can't. Merleau-Ponty argues the opposite. The gap is what makes touching possible. If toucher and touched ever coincided, there would be no touching, only an undivided thing. Perception needs perceiver and perceived to be distinct, and the gap keeps them so.

Because the hand that touches can also be touched, it belongs to the tangible world. It's of the same kind as what it touches. Merleau-Ponty generalizes. The seer is visible in the same way. And what perceiver and perceived share, while remaining distinct, is what he calls flesh.

Husserl's Use of the Example

Merleau-Ponty takes the example from Husserl, who describes one hand touching the other in Ideas II (Husserl [1952] 1989, §36). The two uses differ in what they conclude.

Husserl, Ideas II, §36Merleau-Ponty, "The Intertwining"
What the example showsSensings (Empfindnisse) localized in my bodyReversibility of touching and touched
Its roleConstitutes the lived body as mine, distinct from other thingsShows that the body belongs to the tangible world
VisionUnlike touch, sight doesn't give this doublingExtended to vision: the seer is visible
ResultA boundary between the lived body and natureA kinship between body and world: flesh

Husserl uses the example to mark off the lived body. For him, vision offers no parallel, since the eye doesn't see itself seeing as the hand feels itself touched. Merleau-Ponty uses it to connect the body to the world, and he extends it to vision. The seer, he argues, is visible and belongs to the visible, even if the eye never sees itself in the act.

A common misreading. The most common misreading takes "flesh" to mean the body's tissue: skin and muscle.

  • Flesh isn't anatomy. Merleau-Ponty says it's neither matter nor mind nor substance.
  • Flesh is shared. The world has flesh as much as the body does, in the sense that both are visible and tangible.
  • The touching hands are evidence, not the definition. The example shows that the body is of the same kind as what it perceives. That shared kind is flesh.

Summary

The passage moves from my left hand touching my right hand touching things, through roles that reverse without coinciding, to the gap as the structure of perception. Here is a model paraphrase of the argument to check your reading against:

When my left hand touches my right hand as it touches a table, the right hand can be felt as touching or as touched, but never both at once. The reversal is always about to happen and never completes. This isn't a defect. The gap keeps toucher and touched distinct, which is what makes touching possible. Because the touching hand can be touched, it belongs to the tangible world it touches, and the same holds for the seer, who is visible. What perceiver and perceived share, while remaining distinct, is flesh. Husserl used the same example to mark off the lived body. Merleau-Ponty uses it to show the body's kinship with the world (Merleau-Ponty [1964] 1968, ch. 4).
