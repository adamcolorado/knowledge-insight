The Body-Subject Essay

In this project, you'll write an essay that uses Merleau-Ponty's body-subject to explain a perceptual or bodily phenomenon, such as learning a new skill, a perceptual illusion, tool use, a dance, or adapting to a new keyboard. You'll show why empiricist and intellectualist accounts fall short, analyze the phenomenon through the body schema, habit, or the perceptual field, and extend your analysis to its social or late-ontological dimensions. This kind of analysis is the basis for the theoretical sections of research in embodied cognition and phenomenology.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain a perceptual or bodily phenomenon through Merleau-Ponty's body-subject, showing why rival accounts fall short
  • Integrate Merleau-Ponty's accounts of the body, others or expression, and the flesh into one analysis

Merleau-Ponty's Body-Subject in Embodied Cognition Research

Embodied cognition is now a large research field, and Merleau-Ponty is one of its most cited sources. The field holds that cognition depends on the body's form, capacities, and engagement with the world, not only on processes in the brain (Spaulding 2021). Researchers in it draw on Merleau-Ponty's concepts: the body schema, motor intentionality, habit, and the perceptual field (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012). Shaun Gallagher's work shows how these concepts can be made precise enough to guide research. His distinction between body schema and body image, for example, has been used to organize findings in neurology and psychology (Gallagher 2005).

That uptake brings a risk. Merleau-Ponty's terms are easy to borrow loosely. "Body schema" can drift into meaning a map in the brain, and "flesh" (Merleau-Ponty [1964] 1968, ch. 4) into meaning the body's tissue. When that happens, the concepts lose what made them useful. They stop describing how a phenomenon is lived and become labels for the mechanisms the field already assumed. Precise use of the concepts is what lets them add something.

Professional Context

You're a researcher drafting the theoretical framework section of a paper on embodied perception or skill for an interdisciplinary journal. The paper studies one bodily or perceptual phenomenon. The reviewers will come from psychology and neuroscience as well as philosophy. They know information-processing and representational models well, and some doubt that a philosophical framework can add anything to them.

Your task is to explain the phenomenon through Merleau-Ponty's body-subject in a way that shows cognitive scientists what the framework adds. The framework has to be conceptually precise, grounded in the phenomenon, and clear about what rival accounts miss.

What You'll Create

You'll write a 1,000–1,500 word essay on one perceptual or bodily phenomenon. The essay has five components:

  • a concrete description of the phenomenon
  • a critique of an empiricist or mechanistic account and an intellectualist account of it
  • an analysis through the body schema and motor intentionality
  • an analysis through habit and incorporation, or through the perceptual field
  • one extension: to others or expression, to a critical-phenomenological perspective, or to the late ontology of flesh

Skills in Action

The project draws Merleau-Ponty's accounts together. The critique of empiricism and intellectualism shows what rival accounts assume. The body schema and motor intentionality describe how the body understands its world before explicit thought. Habit and incorporation, or the perceptual field, describe how skill and perception are organized. The extension tests the account against others, expression, social situation, or the flesh. Each part supports the next: a precise critique makes clear what the positive analysis must avoid.

Portfolio Value

A finished essay is a research writing sample: a worked theoretical framework that uses phenomenological concepts precisely and shows what they add to cognitive science. It's the kind of text that could open the framework section of an empirical paper, support a proposal for interdisciplinary research on embodiment, or show a reviewer that you can use Merleau-Ponty's concepts rigorously.

Project Requirements and Instructions

Deliverable Overview

You'll write one essay: an explanation of one perceptual or bodily phenomenon through Merleau-Ponty's body-subject (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012), 1,000–1,500 words long. The essay has five required components. Components 4 and 5 each offer options:

  1. A concrete description of the phenomenon
  2. Why an empiricist or mechanistic account and an intellectualist account each fall short
  3. An analysis through the body schema and motor intentionality
  4. An analysis through habit and incorporation or through the perceptual field
  5. One extension: others or expression, a critical-phenomenological perspective, or the late ontology of flesh

Use a section heading for each component so your analysis is easy to follow. If you cite sources, use Chicago author-date citations and include a References list. The References list doesn't count toward the word limit. Citations are optional.

Detailed Requirements

1. The Phenomenon

Describe the phenomenon concretely, before you apply any theory. Choose one you can observe or perform: learning a new skill, a perceptual illusion, using a tool, a dance step, or adapting to a new keyboard. Say what it's like from within: what you notice, what you do, what changes. A reader should be able to recognize the phenomenon from your description alone.

2. Rival Accounts and Their Shared Error

Write a short empiricist or mechanistic account of the phenomenon: one that builds it from sensations, associations, reflexes, or physical mechanisms. Write a short intellectualist account: one that explains it by attention, judgment, inference, or representation. Then show why each falls short. Name what each misses in the phenomenon as you described it. Finally, identify the error they share: both take the determinate, objective world as given and read it back into perception and action. Merleau-Ponty calls this the prejudice of the world (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, Introduction, ch. 4).

3. Body Schema and Motor Intentionality

Analyze the phenomenon through the body schema and motor intentionality. Describe the body schema as a dynamic sense of the body's position and possibilities for action, not as a map or an image. Show how movement in the phenomenon is directed at things and tasks, not at coordinates.

4. Habit and Incorporation, or the Perceptual Field (Choose One)
  • Option A: Habit and incorporation. Describe how the phenomenon involves habit as a knowledge in the body, or how a tool or instrument is incorporated into the body schema, so that you perceive and act through it.
  • Option B: The perceptual field. Describe how the phenomenon organizes the perceptual field: its figure and ground, its horizons, its constancies, or its indeterminate margins.

Choose the option that fits your phenomenon best. You don't need to address the other.

5. One Extension (Choose One)
  • Option A: Others or expression. Extend the analysis through intercorporeality, speech as gesture, or painting and vision.
  • Option B: A critical-phenomenological perspective. Extend it through Fanon's historico-racial schema (Fanon [1952] 2008, ch. 5), Young's inhibited intentionality (Young [1980] 2005), or Ahmed's orientation (Ahmed 2006a). You may use a described case rather than your own experience.
  • Option C: The late ontology of flesh. Extend it through flesh, reversibility, and the chiasm.

Choose the extension that fits your phenomenon best. Use its concepts precisely.

Success Criteria

Your essay meets the requirements when it:

  • Describes the phenomenon concretely, before applying theory
  • Shows why both an empiricist or mechanistic account and an intellectualist account fall short, and identifies their shared error
  • Analyzes the phenomenon through the body schema and motor intentionality accurately
  • Analyzes it through habit and incorporation, or the perceptual field, accurately
  • Extends the analysis accurately through others or expression, critical phenomenology, or flesh
  • Keeps concepts precise and grounded in the phenomenon throughout

Quality Indicators

Adequate work includes all five components, gives a rival account of each kind, and uses Merleau-Ponty's concepts accurately.

Excellent work does more. It names the shared error of the rival accounts, the prejudice of the world, and shows it at work in the phenomenon. It describes motor intentionality as directed at tasks, not coordinates. Its components connect: the analysis in component 3 answers what the rival accounts missed, and the extension changes or deepens that analysis. Throughout, it describes the phenomenon as lived, rather than explaining it with neural mechanisms.

Step-by-Step Guidance

Step 1: Choose a phenomenon you can observe or perform. Pick something specific and repeatable: typing on an unfamiliar keyboard rather than "skill," or one illusion rather than "perception."

Step 2: Describe it before theorizing. Observe or perform it, and write down what it's like from within. Don't use any theoretical terms yet.

Step 3: Write short rival accounts and identify what they miss. Write a paragraph for each: a mechanistic account and an intellectualist account. Then compare each with your description, and name what it leaves out. Look for the assumption they share.

Step 4: Apply the body schema and motor intentionality. Describe how your body knows its way in the phenomenon, and what your movement is directed at.

Step 5: Choose habit or the perceptual field. If the phenomenon involves skill or a tool, choose habit and incorporation. If it turns on how things appear, choose the perceptual field.

Step 6: Choose one extension. Pick the one that fits: others or expression, a critical-phenomenological perspective, or flesh.

Step 7: Revise out causal and mechanistic language. Reread for words such as "brain," "neural," "processing," "representation," "signal," and "muscle memory." Rewrite each as a description of how the phenomenon is lived, unless you're stating a rival account.

Resources

  • Component checklist. Before you finish, confirm that your essay has these headed sections:
    1. The phenomenon
    2. Rival accounts and their shared error
    3. Body schema and motor intentionality
    4. Habit and incorporation or the perceptual field
    5. Extension: others or expression, critical phenomenology, or flesh
  • Annotated exemplar excerpt. The excerpt below shows a strong component 2 for a different phenomenon from yours: parallel parking a car. Use it as a model of stating rival accounts and naming their shared error, not as a template to copy.

A mechanistic account would say that parking is a chain of stimulus and response: the curb's image on the retina triggers a learned sequence of steering movements, reinforced over years. An intellectualist account would say that I estimate the gap's length, calculate the angle of approach, and judge when to turn the wheel. Neither matches what parking is like. I don't register a retinal image, and I don't calculate anything. I feel the car's rear corner approaching the curb, as if it were my own body's edge, and I turn when the space "looks right." Both accounts start from the same place. They assume a world of measured distances and fixed positions, and then ask how I get from that world to my movement: by association in one case, by judgment in the other. That is Merleau-Ponty's prejudice of the world. It reads the objective space of the finished world back into the parking, when what I live is a space organized by the task of fitting the car into the gap.

Annotations: The excerpt states each rival account fairly, in its own terms. It shows what each misses by returning to the description of the phenomenon. It names the shared error correctly and shows it at work in this case. It uses mechanistic vocabulary only to state the mechanistic account.

    • The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on bodily awareness covers the body schema and its distinction from the body image (de Vignemont 2024).
  • Your own notes. You may draw on any notes or journal entries you've written as you worked through these ideas.

Tips and Common Pitfalls

  • Choosing a phenomenon too broad to describe concretely. "Skill" or "perception" can't be described from within. "Learning to type on a split keyboard" can.
  • Summarizing Merleau-Ponty without applying him. Defining the body schema does little. Showing it at work in a specific movement does a lot.
  • Treating the body schema as a mental map. The body schema isn't a representation of the body. It's a dynamic sense of the body's possibilities for action.
  • Using "flesh" to mean the body's tissue. In the late ontology, flesh is what body and world share, neither matter nor mind. If you choose that extension, use the term in that sense.
