Body-Subject: Habit and Incorporation

This lesson explains Merleau-Ponty's account of habit as a kind of knowledge "in the hands," and how skilled use incorporates instruments, such as a cane, a car, or a keyboard, into the body schema. You'll be able to describe incorporation using his examples of the blind person's cane, the typist, and the organist, and apply them to a skill of your own. These analyses anticipate current research on tool use, skill acquisition, and the extended body.

What you will be able to do

  • Describe how habit and skill incorporate instruments and extend the lived body

Tool Incorporation, Body Representation, and the Extended Body

Where does your body end? The obvious answer is at the skin. Yet skilled use of tools complicates the answer. When you write with a pen, you feel the texture of the paper at the pen's tip, not the pen pressing on your fingers. When you drive a familiar car, you feel the road's surface through the tires, and you sense whether the car will fit through a gap without measuring it. The tool stops being something you feel and becomes something you feel through. A beginner holding the same pen feels mostly the pen: its grip, its weight, and the effort of controlling it.

This observation, familiar to anyone with a practiced skill, has become an experimental topic. Angelo Maravita and Atsushi Iriki reviewed evidence that tool use changes how the brain represents the body and the space around it (Maravita and Iriki 2004). In studies with macaque monkeys trained to retrieve food with a rake, some neurons that normally responded to visual stimuli near the hand began, after tool use, to respond to stimuli near the rake's tip. Related work with humans suggests that the space treated as "near the body" can expand to include a held tool. The findings are debated in their details, but they suggest that the body's boundaries, as the brain treats them, aren't fixed at the skin.

These findings connect to wider debates about embodied and extended cognition (Spaulding 2021). If the body can take in tools, could the mind extend beyond the brain, into notebooks, instruments, and devices? Or does incorporating a tool into the body differ from extending the mind? The debates turn on how skill, tools, and cognition relate.

Maurice Merleau-Ponty described these phenomena decades before the experiments. For him, habit isn't a stock of stored rules or reflexes. It's a knowledge in the hands, a bodily understanding that reorganizes how we perceive and act (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, pt. 1, ch. 3). His examples, including the blind person's cane, the typist, and the organist, remain touchstones in the literature.

His account gives you a way to describe skill as bodily understanding rather than stored rules. That matters in research on skill acquisition, where a rule-based picture shapes how learning is studied and taught. It matters in the design of tools and interfaces, which succeed when they can be incorporated. And it matters in philosophy of mind, where the question of where the body ends shapes the question of where the mind ends.

Habit as Motor Knowledge: The Feathered Hat and the Car

Introduction

Philosophy and psychology have usually explained habit in one of two ways: as a chain of reflexes worn in by repetition, or as knowledge that becomes so familiar we stop noticing it. Merleau-Ponty rejects both, and proposes a third kind of knowledge, which lives in the body itself.

This reading explains his account of habit in Part One, chapter 3, and his first examples: the woman with the feathered hat and the driver passing through a gate. Citations to Phenomenology of Perception give the part and chapter. Landes's translation prints the French page numbers, so learners using Colin Smith's translation can follow along (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2002).

Neither Reflex nor Intellectual Knowledge

Merleau-Ponty sets out two classical accounts of habit, parallel to the empiricist and intellectualist accounts of perception (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, pt. 1, ch. 3).

  • Mechanical reflex. On this account, habit is a set of automatic stimulus-response links, strengthened by repetition. But habits aren't rigid. A typist can type a new word she's never typed before, and a driver can handle a road he's never driven. A reflex account can't explain this flexibility.
  • Intellectual knowledge. On this account, habit is knowledge of rules, applied so often that it becomes unconscious. But the typist can't say where the letters are. The knowledge isn't available as rules, even on reflection.

Habit, for Merleau-Ponty, is a bodily capacity to respond to a kind of situation, acquired through practice, that is neither a mechanical reflex nor intellectual knowledge of rules. It's a motor knowledge: a practical knowledge that resides in the body and is available only in bodily effort, not in explicit representation. Merleau-Ponty describes it as a knowledge that is in the hands.

How Habit Is Acquired

What happens when a habit is acquired? Merleau-Ponty's answer is that the body grasps a motor significance: the practical meaning of a situation for movement, what it calls for and affords. Learning to dance is his example. The dancer doesn't analyze the movement into parts and reassemble them. The body catches the movement's overall form, and the parts follow.

Acquiring a habit is thus a kind of bodily understanding: in Merleau-Ponty's account, the body's grasp of the fit between an aim and a performance, which constitutes acquiring a habit. To understand, in this sense, is to experience the harmony between what one intends and what one does. The body understands when it has taken in a new meaning, not when a mind has stored a new rule.

The Feathered Hat and the Car

Merleau-Ponty's first two examples concern clearances.

  • A woman wearing a hat with a tall feather passes through doorways without catching it. She doesn't measure the doorway or calculate the feather's height. She senses, without thinking, where the feather is and what it can pass under.
  • A driver who knows his car can tell whether it will fit through a narrow gate or down a lane without comparing the width of the opening with the width of the car.

In both cases, the body's sense of its own extent has grown to include something that isn't part of the body as object: the feather, the car. The knowledge is exact, reliable, and not a matter of measurement. The body has made the feather's height and the car's width its own.

Conclusion

For Merleau-Ponty, habit is neither reflex nor intellectual knowledge. It's motor knowledge, a knowledge in the hands, acquired when the body grasps a motor significance. Acquiring a habit is a bodily understanding of the fit between aim and performance. The feathered hat and the car show the body sensing clearances it never measures.

Key Terms

  • Habit: In Merleau-Ponty, a bodily capacity to respond to a kind of situation, acquired through practice, that is neither a mechanical reflex nor intellectual knowledge of rules.
  • Motor knowledge: In Merleau-Ponty, a practical knowledge that resides in the body and is available only in bodily effort, not in explicit representation: a knowledge in the hands.
  • Motor significance: In Merleau-Ponty, the practical meaning of a situation for movement: what it calls for and affords.
  • Bodily understanding: In Merleau-Ponty, the body's grasp of the fit between an aim and a performance, which constitutes acquiring a habit.

The Typist and the Organist: Habit as Rearranging the Body Schema

Introduction

A skilled typist can type fast and accurately, yet may be unable to say where the letter "q" is on the keyboard. Merleau-Ponty treats this ordinary puzzle as a clue to what habit is.

This reading explains his examples of the typist and the organist, and the interpretation of habit as a rearrangement of the body schema. Citations to Phenomenology of Perception give the part and chapter.

The Typist

A person who types well knows where the letters are, in the sense that her fingers go to them. Yet if you ask her to point to the letter "q" on a blank keyboard, or to say where it is, she may have to type a word in her head, or move her fingers, to find out (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, pt. 1, ch. 3).

Merleau-Ponty draws the lesson. The typist's knowledge of the keyboard isn't knowledge of the locations of letters, stored as positions and retrieved on demand. It's a practical familiarity, available only in the typing itself. The keyboard has become part of her bodily space. Each key is present to her as a place her fingers can reach, not as a point she can name.

The Organist

The organist example makes the point with an unfamiliar instrument. An experienced organist arrives to give a concert on an organ he has never played. It has more or fewer keyboards than his own, and its stops are in different places. He needs only about an hour of practice before he can play it.

In that hour, he doesn't learn the position of each stop and pedal as a set of coordinates. Merleau-Ponty says he settles into the organ, as one settles into a house. He takes up its dimensions and directions and makes them his own. He takes the organ as a space of expression: in Merleau-Ponty's account of the organist, the instrument's space as inhabited by the player's body, through which he plays rather than which he maps. After the hour, he plays through it, not with a map of it.

Habit as Rearranging the Body Schema

These examples show what happens in acquiring a habit. The body schema (schéma corporel) is, for Merleau-Ponty, a dynamic, unified sense of the body's position and possibilities for action, not an inventory of parts or a mental image of the body. A habit, on his account, is a change in this schema. Komarine Romdenh-Romluc's commentary emphasizes the point: acquiring a habit rearranges the body schema, so that new possibilities of action become available to the body directly (Romdenh-Romluc 2011).

The rearrangement of the body schema, in this sense, is the process by which practice reorganizes the body's sense of its own possibilities, so that new instruments or skills become part of how it can act. What's rearranged isn't a list of positions. It's the whole field of what the body can do. And the rearranged capacities settle into the habitual body (corps habituel): in Merleau-Ponty, the layer of settled capacities and ways of acting built up over a lifetime, which projects a field of practical possibilities onto the world.

Conclusion

The typist knows the keyboard only in typing. The organist settles into an unfamiliar organ in an hour, inhabiting it rather than mapping it. Habit, on this account, rearranges the body schema, and its new capacities settle into the habitual body. In each of Merleau-Ponty's examples, the body knows precisely what it can't state.

  • Body schema (schéma corporel): In Merleau-Ponty, a dynamic, unified sense of the body's position and possibilities for action, not an inventory of parts or a mental image of the body.
  • Rearrangement of the body schema: The process by which practice reorganizes the body's sense of its own possibilities, so that new instruments or skills become part of how it can act.
  • Habitual body (corps habituel): In Merleau-Ponty, the layer of settled capacities and ways of acting built up over a lifetime, which projects a field of practical possibilities onto the world.

The Blind Person's Cane and the Incorporation of Instruments

Introduction

A blind person walking with a cane doesn't feel the cane pressing on the palm. She feels the curb, the gravel, and the step, at the cane's tip. Merleau-Ponty makes this example the paradigm of how instruments become part of the lived body.

This reading explains the cane example, the distinction between incorporation and extension, and habit as the power to dilate our being in the world. Citations to Phenomenology of Perception give the part and chapter.

The Cane

Merleau-Ponty's description is precise (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, pt. 1, ch. 3). For someone just learning to use a cane, it's an object. It's felt as pressure in the hand, and its position must be worked out. For an experienced user, that changes. The cane is no longer perceived for itself. Its tip has become an area of sensitivity: in Merleau-Ponty's cane example, the region at the tool's tip where sensation is felt once the tool is incorporated. The pressure in the hand gives way to the world at the end of the cane: the texture of the path, and the edge of the step.

The point of sensation has moved. It's no longer where the body, as object, meets the tool, but where the tool meets the world. The cane has been taken into the body schema. The lesson's close reading works through this passage.

Incorporation and Extension

Two related terms describe what's happened.

  • Incorporation is the process by which a tool becomes part of the body schema, so that one perceives and acts through it rather than perceiving it as an object.
  • Extension is the resulting enlargement of the body's reach and sensitivity to include the tool, so that sensation is felt at the tool's point of contact with the world.

Incorporation names how it happens, and extension names what results. The key contrast is between perceiving through a tool and perceiving it as an object. The new cane user perceives the cane. The experienced user perceives with it.

The contrast also explains why incorporation can be undone. When the cane catches in a grate, or the pen runs dry, the tool abruptly returns as an object, felt in the hand again, until use resumes. Incorporation is a way of using the tool, not a permanent change in what the tool is.

This contrast tracks the difference between the lived body and the body as object. The incorporated cane is part of the lived body. It isn't part of the body as object, which still ends at the skin.

Habit as Dilation

Merleau-Ponty generalizes. Habit, he says, expresses our power to dilate, or expand, our being in the world, or to change our existence by taking up new instruments. Dilation of being-in-the-world is his phrase for this power: habit as the capacity to enlarge one's existence by incorporating new instruments and skills.

This is why habit isn't a narrowing, as the word "routine" suggests. Every new habit is an expansion. The cane gives the blind person a new reach into the world. The car gives the driver a new body. The keyboard gives the writer a new mode of expression. In each case, the body's world grows, because the body itself has grown.

Conclusion

For the experienced user, the blind person's cane stops being an object and becomes an area of sensitivity, with sensation at the tip. Incorporation is the process by which a tool becomes part of the body schema, and extension is the resulting enlargement of the body's reach. Habit, for Merleau-Ponty, is the power to dilate our being in the world by taking up new instruments.

Key Terms

  • Area of sensitivity: In Merleau-Ponty's cane example, the region at the tool's tip where sensation is felt once the tool is incorporated.
  • Incorporation: The process by which a tool becomes part of the body schema, so that one perceives and acts through it rather than perceiving it as an object.
  • Extension: The resulting enlargement of the body's reach and sensitivity to include a tool, so that sensation is felt at the tool's point of contact with the world.
  • Dilation of being-in-the-world: Merleau-Ponty's phrase for habit as the capacity to enlarge one's existence by incorporating new instruments and skills.

Peripersonal Space, Tool Use, and Incorporation Compared

Introduction

Merleau-Ponty described incorporation from the first-person side (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, pt. 1, ch. 3). Neuroscience has since studied it from the other side, asking how the brain represents the space around the body when tools are in hand. The two approaches can inform each other, provided their claims aren't confused.

This reading points to current research on peripersonal space and tool use, compares incorporation with two rival accounts of skill, and gathers the lesson's core terms.

Peripersonal Space and Tool Use

Peripersonal space is the space immediately surrounding the body, within reach, which the brain treats specially and which can expand with tool use. Research reviewed by Angelo Maravita and Atsushi Iriki suggests that tool use can change how this space is represented (Maravita and Iriki 2004).

In monkeys trained to use a rake, some neurons that responded to visual stimuli near the hand began to respond to stimuli near the rake's tip. In humans, studies of how visual and tactile information interact near the body suggest that a held tool can extend the zone treated as near. The findings are a pointer, not a proof. They're debated in their details, and they describe brain representation rather than lived experience. But they fit, from the outside, the pattern Merleau-Ponty described from the inside.

Three Accounts of Skill

Merleau-Ponty's account of incorporation can be contrasted with two rival accounts of skilled tool use. Shaun Gallagher's work on the body schema supports the first column's distinction between practical, pre-reflective capacities and explicit knowledge (Gallagher 2005).

Research on peripersonal space suggests that tool use can expand the space the brain treats as near the body. It offers an outside view of what Merleau-Ponty described from within. Incorporation differs from both mechanical association and intellectual rule-following: the tool becomes part of the lived body, and skill meets new situations directly.

Guided Close Reading: The Blind Person's Cane and the Organist

Introduction

Two short passages in Part One, chapter 3, of Phenomenology of Perception carry Merleau-Ponty's account of habit: the blind person's cane and the organist. They're vivid and easy to quote, and easy to treat as illustrations of a theory stated elsewhere. Read closely, they are the theory. This reading works through both passages, asks what "understanding" means in them, and ends with a model paraphrase connecting the two examples.

Locating the Passages

Both passages are in Part One, chapter 3, in the section on habit (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, pt. 1, ch. 3). Landes's translation prints the French page numbers in the margin, so learners using Colin Smith's translation can follow along (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2002). Key phrases below are given in French, with typical English renderings under Key Considerations.

No free English translation is available. The free Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Merleau-Ponty covers the body-subject and habit (Toadvine 2023, §3).

Before you start, note the setting. Merleau-Ponty has rejected both the reflex account and the intellectual account of habit. He now needs examples that show what habit positively is.

Walking Through the Argument

Step 1: The cane ceases to be an object and becomes an area of sensitivity

Merleau-Ponty begins with the blind person's cane. At first, the cane is an object among others. Its pressure is felt in the palm, and its position must be judged. With practice, this changes. The cane stops being perceived for itself. Its tip becomes a zone of sensitivity (zone sensible), and it extends the scope and the active radius of touch. It becomes analogous to a gaze.

Notice two claims here. The first concerns where sensation is felt: at the tip, not in the hand. The second concerns what the cane is: no longer an object, but part of the apparatus of perceiving. The cane isn't something the blind person feels. It's something she feels with.

Merleau-Ponty adds that the cane's length isn't calculated. The user doesn't know it as a measurement, then add it to the length of her arm. Its reach is felt directly, as the reach of her own touch.

Step 2: The organist doesn't memorize positions but inhabits the instrument's space

The second passage turns to the organist, who has an hour to prepare for a concert on an unfamiliar organ. Merleau-Ponty insists that the organist doesn't use the hour to learn the positions of the stops and pedals, as if memorizing a map. He wouldn't be able to recite them afterward. Instead, he takes the measure of the instrument with his body. He takes up its directions and dimensions, and settles into it (s'installe), as one settles into a house.

Merleau-Ponty describes the stops and pedals as locations of affective or musical value, set out in an expressive space (espace expressif). They aren't points in objective space. During rehearsal and performance, the organist's gestures consecrate this space. The instrument becomes the place where the music happens.

Step 3: Habit is an understanding by the body

From both examples, Merleau-Ponty draws the general lesson. Habit is neither a mechanical reflex nor an intellectual knowledge. It's a knowledge in the hands, which is given only in bodily effort. To acquire a habit is for the body to understand (comprendre): to grasp the agreement between what we aim at and what's given, between the intention and the performance. The body is the anchor of this understanding.

What "Understanding" Means Here

"Understanding" usually means a mental achievement: grasping a proof or an argument. Merleau-Ponty uses the word deliberately for something the body does. He's claiming that what the organist does in his hour is genuinely understanding, a grasp of meaning, and that it's done by the body, not by a mind using the body.

This isn't knowledge "in the head," for two reasons. It can't be stated: the organist can't recite the stops' positions. And it can't be separated from performance: it's available only in playing. Yet it's exact, flexible, and meaningful. It adapts to a new instrument, and it serves the music. That combination, exact but unstatable, meaningful but bodily, is what Merleau-Ponty wants to show exists.

A common misreading. The most common misreading treats incorporation as a metaphor only, a vivid way of saying that tools feel familiar. That weakens Merleau-Ponty's claim.

  • He makes a literal claim about where sensation is felt. The curb is felt at the cane's tip. That's a description of experience, not a figure of speech.
  • He makes a literal claim about the body schema. The cane's reach is felt as the reach of touch, and the organ's space is inhabited. Both are real changes in what the body can do and feel.
  • Current research takes the claim seriously. Studies of peripersonal space treat tool incorporation as something that can be measured.

Summary

The cane passage shows a tool ceasing to be an object and becoming an area of sensitivity. The organist passage shows an instrument's space inhabited rather than mapped. Both show habit as an understanding by the body. Here is a model paraphrase connecting the two examples:

For the experienced cane user, the cane is no longer an object felt in the hand. Its tip has become an area of sensitivity, where the curb and the path are felt. For the organist, an unfamiliar organ is learned in an hour, not by memorizing positions, but by settling into its expressive space as one settles into a house. In both cases, the instrument has been taken into the body schema. Habit is neither reflex nor intellectual knowledge. It's the body's own understanding: a grasp of the fit between what it aims at and what it does, exact but unstatable (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, pt. 1, ch. 3).

Claude, please facilitate a reflective dialogue with me. I'm a graduate-level learner studying Merleau-Ponty's account of habit and the incorporation of tools. Follow this guidance for the whole conversation.

GOAL
Help me describe how habit and skill incorporate instruments and extend the lived body by analyzing a tool I use fluently, how it became part of my body schema, and what happens when I switch to an unfamiliar version.

HOW TO RUN THE CONVERSATION
- Ask one question at a time, then wait for my reply. Keep each of your turns under about 120 words.
- Don't lecture. Explain a point only when I need it to continue, then return to my tool.
- Be patient, curious, and collegial. Encourage concrete sensory detail. Define terms of art briefly on first use.
- Plain conversation only: no web search, files, or artifacts.
- Aim for about 12 minutes. If I struggle to name a tool, suggest a pen, a fork, a phone, or a car. If I seem uncertain, shorten the conversation to 5-7 minutes. Always reach topic 4.
- Start now. Open with one or two warm sentences: this is an exploratory conversation, not a test; any tool I use fluently works, from a pen to a bike to a musical instrument. Then ask me to name one.

TOPICS, IN ORDER
1. Feeling through the tool. Ask me to name a tool I use so fluently that I feel through it, such as a pen, a bike, a racket, or a keyboard, and ask where I feel its contact with the world. Help me locate sensation at the tool's tip or surface: the paper at the pen's point, the road through the tires. Introduce incorporation and the blind person's cane.
2. Learning it. Ask me to remember learning the tool: when did it stop being an object and become part of how I act? Help me describe the transition from attending to the tool to attending through it. Introduce habit as motor knowledge and as a rearrangement of the body schema. If I describe memorizing steps, ask whether I still use those steps now.
3. An unfamiliar version. Ask what happens when I switch to an unfamiliar version of the tool. Help me describe the brief return of the tool as an object, and how quickly I adapt. Connect this to the organist settling into an unfamiliar organ in about an hour.
4. Closing reflection. Ask whether incorporation is the same as having extended my mind, and have me note my answer for a journal entry. Accept any reasoned position. If helpful, note that incorporating a tool into the body schema and extending cognition beyond the brain are related but distinct claims.

POSITIONS TO KEEP ACCURATE
- Habit is neither mechanical reflex nor intellectual knowledge. It's motor knowledge, a knowledge in the hands.
- Acquiring a habit is a bodily understanding: grasping the fit between aim and performance.
- The feathered hat and the car show the body sensing clearances without measuring.
- The typist knows the keyboard only in typing. The organist inhabits an unfamiliar organ's expressive space.
- Habit rearranges the body schema and settles into the habitual body.
- An incorporated tool is perceived through, not perceived as an object. Sensation is felt at its tip.
- Research on peripersonal space offers an outside view of incorporation, as a pointer, not a proof.

MISREADINGS TO CORRECT GENTLY
Keep every point tied to my own tool. When a misreading appears, name the accurate position briefly, then return to my experience.
- Habit as memorized steps. Motor knowledge can't be stated as rules and is available only in performance.
- Habit as mere reflex. It's flexible and meets new situations directly.
- Incorporation as metaphor only. It's a claim about where sensation is felt and what the body can do.
- The body schema as a mental map. The typist's keyboard isn't mapped but inhabited.
- Incorporation as identical to extended mind. The two claims are related but can be distinguished.

TO FINISH
After I give my answer, close in one short turn:
- Affirm one precise observation I made, in my own words where possible.
- Suggest one or two next steps that fit how the conversation went. Possible steps: write the journal entry; explore research on peripersonal space and tool use; compare Merleau-Ponty's account with rule-based models of skill acquisition; revisit Merleau-Ponty's examples of the blind person's cane and the organ; review what habit, motor knowledge, and incorporation mean; try the conversation again with a pen, attending to the paper at its tip.
- Restate my answer on its own line, labeled "Journal note," so I can copy it as the focus for a journal entry.
