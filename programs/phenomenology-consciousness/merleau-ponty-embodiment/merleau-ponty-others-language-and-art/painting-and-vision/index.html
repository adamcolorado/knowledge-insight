Others, Language, Art: Painting and Vision

This lesson interprets Merleau-Ponty's essays on painting, "Cézanne's Doubt," "Indirect Language and the Voices of Silence," and "Eye and Mind," as accounts of how vision and expression work. You'll be able to explain Cézanne's "lived perspective," the painter's body as the site of vision, and painting as creative expression parallel to speech. These essays are central to current phenomenological aesthetics and to research on perception and art.

What you will be able to do

  • Interpret Merleau-Ponty's essays on painting as an account of expression and vision

Painting as Evidence About Perception: Cézanne and Merleau-Ponty

Most people have taken a photograph of a scene and found that it looks wrong. The mountain that dominated the view has shrunk to a low line on the horizon. The table that seemed to spread out in front of you has become a thin wedge. The photograph is accurate by one standard: it records the light that reached the lens from a single fixed point. By another standard, it misses what you saw.

That gap raises a question for anyone who studies perception. If the camera's image and the lived view differ, which one describes vision? Vision science usually starts from the image on the retina and asks how the brain builds a percept from it. On that approach, the photograph looks like the neutral starting point, and whatever perception adds is processing or error. But the experience of the scene doesn't present itself as a corrected photograph. It presents itself as the scene.

Painters have worked on this gap for centuries, because they have to make vision visible. They can't simply copy what's in front of them, since there's no single copy to make. Every painting is a decision about what seeing is like. So painting can serve as a kind of evidence about perception: a record of what careful looking finds that the camera doesn't.

Paul Cézanne (1839–1906) is the painter Merleau-Ponty returned to most often. Late in his life, Cézanne painted Mont Sainte-Victoire, near his home in Aix-en-Provence, dozens of times. He painted it again and again because he never thought he had got it right. In "Cézanne's Doubt," Merleau-Ponty reads this struggle as a philosophical one (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 1993). Cézanne, he argues, was trying to paint the world as it takes shape in perception, before it settles into the finished objects that science and habit assume. The tilted tables and uneven bowls in his still lifes aren't mistakes. They record how things appear to a moving, looking body.

This matters for research in two ways. First, it offers a test for any model of vision: does the model describe what perception is like, or only what the camera records? Second, it shows how a practice outside science can produce knowledge about experience. A painter's decisions about where to bend a line or shift a color are, on this view, findings about seeing. If you study perception, aesthetics, or the relation between art and science, Merleau-Ponty's essays on painting give you a way to treat art as evidence about how we see, not only as its product.

Cézanne's Project: Painting the World as It Emerges in Perception

Introduction

Cézanne's paintings are often described as bridges between Impressionism and Cubism. Merleau-Ponty's essay "Cézanne's Doubt" reads them differently: as an attempt to paint perception itself, at the point where the world takes form.

This reading explains Cézanne's project as Merleau-Ponty describes it and introduces lived perspective. Merleau-Ponty's essays on painting have no standard section numbers, so citations give the essay's original date only.

Between Sensation and Composition

Merleau-Ponty sets Cézanne's work against two options available to painters of his time (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 1993). The Impressionists tried to capture the play of light and atmosphere as it strikes the eye. The result, Cézanne thought, was that objects lost their weight and solidity and dissolved into light. The older academic tradition built paintings by composition: outline, perspective, and modeling in light and shade, arranged according to rules.

Cézanne refused to choose. He wanted to return to the solid object without giving up the evidence of the senses. Merleau-Ponty's point is that this refusal isn't a compromise between two methods. It rejects the distinction the two methods share, between the raw data of sensation and the order imposed on them by thought. That is the same distinction Merleau-Ponty attacks in his account of perception (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, Introduction).

Primordial Perception

What Cézanne wanted to paint, on Merleau-Ponty's reading, is primordial perception: Merleau-Ponty's term, in the Cézanne essay, for perception as it is lived before it settles into determinate objects and the distinctions of thought. We live among human-made things and habits of seeing, and we take the world's order for granted. Cézanne's painting suspends those habits. It shows the world as it organizes itself before our eyes, and it shows what Merleau-Ponty calls the base of nature on which human life has installed itself.

The shift here is from painting objects to painting the appearance of objects: the object in the act of taking form.

Lived Perspective

The most concrete result of this project is what Merleau-Ponty calls lived perspective: the perspective of perception as lived by a moving, embodied perceiver, in which things appear with the deformations of an unfolding view rather than according to a geometric construction. Merleau-Ponty cites studies of Cézanne's paintings showing that lived perspective isn't the same as geometric perspective: the system of linear perspective that represents a scene as projected onto a plane from a single, fixed viewpoint. Nor is it the perspective of a photograph, which fixes one such viewpoint mechanically.

Cézanne's still lifes and portraits contain "distortions" that break the rules of geometric perspective. Tables tilt toward the viewer. The edge of a table doesn't continue in a straight line behind the objects set on it. The openings of bowls are rounder than geometry allows. Merleau-Ponty argues that these aren't failures of technique. They track how a scene is lived, as the eye moves across it and takes it in from more than one position.

Color and Depth

Cézanne also refused to build form with outline. A firm outline, Merleau-Ponty notes, treats the contour as a line around the object, when in perception the contour is where the object turns away from us. Cézanne worked instead by modulation: Cézanne's technique of building form and depth through graded shifts of color rather than through outline and shading. Where he used lines, he often used several, so that the edge seems to be where the object is still taking shape.

Conclusion

On Merleau-Ponty's reading, Cézanne tried to paint primordial perception: the world as it takes form, before it settles into the objects of science and habit. His "distortions" follow lived perspective rather than geometric perspective, and he built form through color modulation rather than outline. His project refuses the split between sensation and composition that Merleau-Ponty rejects in perception.

Key Terms

  • Primordial perception: Merleau-Ponty's term, in the Cézanne essay, for perception as it is lived before it settles into determinate objects and the distinctions of thought.
  • Lived perspective: The perspective of perception as lived by a moving, embodied perceiver, in which things appear with the deformations of an unfolding view rather than according to a geometric construction.
  • Geometric perspective: The system of linear perspective that represents a scene as projected onto a plane from a single, fixed viewpoint.
  • Modulation: Cézanne's technique of building form and depth through graded shifts of color rather than through outline and shading.

Expression as Risk: Cézanne's Doubt, His Life, and His Work

Introduction

The title of "Cézanne's Doubt" names a real doubt. Cézanne was never sure his work meant what he hoped it meant, and he sometimes feared that its novelty came from a defect in his eyes or his temperament.

This reading explains how Merleau-Ponty treats that doubt: as a feature of creative expression, and as a problem about the relation between an artist's life and work. Citations give each essay's original date only.

Expression Without a Model

Merleau-Ponty's first claim is that the painter doesn't copy a meaning that exists before the painting (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 1993). Expression, in Merleau-Ponty's sense, is the embodiment of meaning in bodily conduct, as meaning is embodied in a gesture, rather than the outward sign of an inner meaning. The painting is such an embodiment. Its meaning comes into being in the work, not before it.

This has a consequence. If there is no finished meaning to copy, there is nothing to check the work against. That's the source of what can be called the risk of expression: in Merleau-Ponty, the uncertainty built into creative expression, since the meaning a work will have isn't available in advance to guide or guarantee it. Merleau-Ponty compares the artist to the first person who ever spoke, who couldn't know whether the utterance would be understood as anything more than a cry. The meaning of a new work depends on whether others take it up.

Cézanne's doubt, on this reading, isn't a personal weakness. It's the doubt that belongs to any expression that tries to say something new. The same structure appears in Merleau-Ponty's account of speech (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, pt. 1, ch. 6), where new expression takes up available words and makes them say something they haven't said before.

The Life and the Work

The essay also takes up a question critics had asked about Cézanne: did his art come from his temperament? Cézanne was withdrawn, anxious, and difficult, and some writers explained his painting as a symptom of his psychology.

Merleau-Ponty rejects biographical explanation: the attempt to explain an artwork's meaning by the facts of the artist's life, temperament, or psychology. The life doesn't explain the work. But he doesn't simply separate them either. His claim is that life and work communicate. A temperament is a given, as a body and a history are given. What the painter makes of it isn't given. Cézanne's work gave his temperament a meaning it didn't have on its own. In Merleau-Ponty's phrasing, the work to be done called for this life, rather than the life producing the work.

Freedom in a Situation

This leads to Merleau-Ponty's view of freedom. He discusses Freud's study of Leonardo da Vinci, which traces Leonardo's art to his childhood (Freud [1910] 1957). Merleau-Ponty grants that psychoanalysis can reveal meanings in a life. He denies that it determines what the artist does with them.

The view is one of situated freedom: the view that freedom doesn't escape one's given situation but takes it up and gives it a meaning. We are never free of our bodies, histories, and temperaments. But we aren't mere products of them. Freedom works on what is given, as Cézanne worked on his temperament and his motifs.

Conclusion

For Merleau-Ponty, painting is expression, not copying, so its meaning can't be checked against a model. This is the risk of expression, and it's the real content of Cézanne's doubt. Merleau-Ponty rejects biographical explanation of the work but holds that life and work communicate, and he treats freedom as situated: a taking up of what is given.

Key Terms

  • Expression: In Merleau-Ponty, the embodiment of meaning in bodily conduct, as meaning is embodied in a gesture, rather than the outward sign of an inner meaning.
  • Risk of expression: In Merleau-Ponty, the uncertainty built into creative expression, since the meaning a work will have isn't available in advance to guide or guarantee it.
  • Biographical explanation: The attempt to explain an artwork's meaning by the facts of the artist's life, temperament, or psychology.
  • Situated freedom: The view that freedom doesn't escape one's given situation but takes it up and gives it a meaning.

"Eye and Mind": The Painter's Body and Descartes's Optics

Introduction

"Eye and Mind" was the last essay Merleau-Ponty saw published. Written in 1960 and published in 1961, it returns to painting to state a view of vision that his earlier work had only begun.

This reading covers the essay's critique of scientific thinking, its account of the painter's body, and its critique of Descartes's theory of vision. Citations to Descartes's Optics give the discourse number, which holds across editions.

Operational Thought

The essay opens with a diagnosis of science (Merleau-Ponty [1961] 1993). Science, Merleau-Ponty writes, manipulates things and gives up living in them. He calls this operational thought: Merleau-Ponty's name for scientific thinking that manipulates things through models and operations and treats everything as an object in general, without inhabiting it.

Merleau-Ponty doesn't reject science. His worry is that operational thought forgets the perceived world it depends on. It treats models as if they were the things. What it needs, he argues, is to return to the world as it is seen and to the body through which it's seen. Painting keeps that world in view.

The Painter's Body

Merleau-Ponty borrows a remark from the poet Paul Valéry: the painter "takes his body with him" (Valéry [1936] 1960). A painter doesn't paint with a mind that surveys the world from outside. The painter paints by lending a body to the world, by looking, moving, and working with the hand.

This leads to what he calls the enigma of the body. My body sees, and it's visible. It's a thing among things, caught in the world it perceives, and it's also what sees them. The seer isn't outside the visible. Vision happens from within the visible: in Merleau-Ponty's late writing, the perceptible world as seen, which includes the seeing body itself.

Painting shows this. The painter, Merleau-Ponty says, gives visible existence to what ordinary vision takes to be invisible: depth, light, movement, and the way things come to be seen. A painting doesn't copy things. It makes seeing visible.

Descartes's Optics

Merleau-Ponty's foil is Descartes's Optics of 1637. He reads it as the charter of a thought that no longer wants to dwell in the visible.

Descartes explains vision without images that resemble things. He compares seeing to a blind man feeling his way with a stick: the eye receives actions transmitted by light, as the hand receives the stick's contacts (Descartes [1637] 1985, disc. 1). He then argues that the signs in the brain need not resemble their objects, any more than copperplate engravings resemble what they depict. An engraving shows a circle as an oval and a square as a rhombus, and it represents things well precisely because it doesn't resemble them (disc. 4). On this account, seeing is the mind reading signs. It's a form of thinking.

Merleau-Ponty objects that this is a theory of thinking about vision, not of vision. Descartes chose engravings because they reduce painting to line and outline, which the mind can decode. Color, depth, and the painter's body drop out. What drops out is lived perspective: the perspective of perception as lived by a moving, embodied perceiver, in which things appear with the deformations of an unfolding view rather than according to a geometric construction. The table sets the three views side by side.

Depth

The disagreement comes to a head over depth. For Descartes, depth is a third dimension, derived from the other two. For Merleau-Ponty, depth is the dimension in which things envelop and hide one another, lived by a body among them before it's measured as distance. Depth isn't a line seen from the side. It's the experience of being in a world where things are behind one another.

Conclusion

"Eye and Mind" sets painting against operational thought. The painter takes the body along, and vision happens from within the visible, where the seer is also seen. Descartes's Optics explains vision as the mind decoding signs, modeled on engravings. Merleau-Ponty answers that this theory loses color, depth, and the seeing body.

Key Terms

  • Operational thought: Merleau-Ponty's name for scientific thinking that manipulates things through models and operations and treats everything as an object in general, without inhabiting it.
  • The visible: In Merleau-Ponty's late writing, the perceptible world as seen, which includes the seeing body itself.
  • Lived perspective: The perspective of perception as lived by a moving, embodied perceiver, in which things appear with the deformations of an unfolding view rather than according to a geometric construction.
  • Depth: In Merleau-Ponty, the dimension in which things envelop and hide one another, lived by a body among them before it's measured as distance.

"Indirect Language": Painting, Speech, and Style as Expression

Introduction

Merleau-Ponty's account of speech and his essays on painting were written in parallel, and each clarifies the other. "Indirect Language and the Voices of Silence" states the parallel directly.

This reading covers the essay's account of indirect expression, its account of a painter's style, and the link between painting and creative speech. Citations give the essay's original date only.

The Setting

Merleau-Ponty published the essay in 1952 (Merleau-Ponty [1952] 1993). It responds to André Malraux's The Voices of Silence, a large study of the history of art (Malraux [1951] 1953). Malraux treated style as the means by which a painter bends the world to a personal vision. Merleau-Ponty argues that this makes style too subjective, and that it misses what painting shares with language.

Indirect Language

Merleau-Ponty's starting point is a claim about language. Words don't signify one by one, each pointing to a meaning set out in advance. They signify through their relations to one another and through what goes unsaid between them. This is indirect language: Merleau-Ponty's term for the way language and painting signify through the relations among their elements and what those relations leave unsaid, rather than by pointing directly to a meaning set out in advance.

The consequence is that meaning is never simply transferred. A speaker or writer relies on what the words imply as much as on what they state. The same is true, Merleau-Ponty argues, of painting. A painting's meaning lies in the relations among its colors, lines, and spaces, not in a message that could be stated in some other way. That's why he calls painting a tacit language: one that expresses without words.

Style

The essay's central concept is style. For Merleau-Ponty, a painterly style is a painter's way of inhabiting and rendering the world, a system of equivalences already at work in perception, not a set of techniques or a personal signature. The phrase "system of equivalences" is his. A painter's style is the consistent way that painter's work translates the look of things into paint.

Merleau-Ponty makes two claims about it.

  1. Style begins in perception. The painter doesn't first see neutrally and then add a style. Perceiving is already stylized. A woman walking past, he writes, isn't first seen as a contour. She's seen as a certain manner of moving, a way of being a body. The painter's work takes up and develops this.
  2. Style is not visible to the painter. A painter doesn't choose a style from outside. It emerges in the work, and others often recognize it before the painter does.

Painting and Speech

These claims make painting a form of creative expression parallel to speech. The painter takes up the available means of painting, as a speaker takes up available words, and makes them say something they haven't said before. Merleau-Ponty's distinction between two kinds of speech applies (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, pt. 1, ch. 6). Speaking speech (parole parlante), in Merleau-Ponty, is the originating act of expression that brings a new meaning into being. A new painting that changes how things can be seen is expression of this kind. Its achievement, once taken up, becomes part of the history of painting that later painters inherit, as new meanings settle into available language.

Conclusion

"Indirect Language" treats language and painting as indirect: both signify through relations and what goes unsaid, not by pointing to prior meanings. A painter's style is a system of equivalences that begins in perception, not a signature added to it. Painting that changes how things can be seen is creative expression, like speaking speech.

Guided Close Reading: Cézanne's Distortions and Lived Perspective in "Cézanne's Doubt"

Introduction

"Cézanne's Doubt" contains a short passage that does a lot of work. In it, Merleau-Ponty explains the "distortions" in Cézanne's paintings as fidelity to perception rather than departures from it. This reading works through the passage in three moves, pauses on what "true to perception" can mean, flags a common misreading, and ends with a described painting and a model paraphrase.

Locating the Passage

The passage comes in the middle of the essay, after Merleau-Ponty describes Cézanne's break with Impressionism and before he turns to Cézanne's life (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 1993). It's the paragraph that introduces lived perspective and discusses the tilted table and the bowls.

No free English translation is available. The free Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Merleau-Ponty summarizes his essays on painting (Toadvine 2023, §4). Key phrases below are given in French, with English renderings under Key Considerations. Public-domain reproductions of Cézanne's still lifes are held in many museum collections, and it helps to look at one as you read.

Before you start, note the setting. Merleau-Ponty has just argued that Cézanne refused the choice between sensation and composition. The passage now shows what that refusal looks like on the canvas.

Walking Through the Argument

Step 1: Geometric perspective gives a photograph's view

Merleau-Ponty begins by naming the standard Cézanne's paintings seem to break. Geometric perspective projects a scene onto a plane from a single, fixed point. A photograph does the same thing mechanically. By this standard, a painting is correct when its lines converge where the geometry says they should.

Notice the move. He doesn't deny that geometric perspective is accurate on its own terms. He asks what it's accurate to. It's accurate to a viewpoint no one occupies: an eye that doesn't move, sees with one eye, and takes in the scene in an instant. Studies of Cézanne's work, he reports, show that lived perspective (perspective vécue) isn't geometric or photographic perspective.

Step 2: The distortions track how things are lived as the eye moves

The second move examines the distortions. Merleau-Ponty's examples are specific. In the Portrait of Gustave Geffroy, the table stretches toward the bottom of the picture, more than geometry allows. In the still lifes, the edge of a table doesn't continue in a straight line behind the objects in front of it. And the opening of a bowl or cup, which geometry would render as a thin oval, is painted rounder, swollen at its ends.

His explanation is that each distortion records a way of looking. When the eye runs over a large surface, it takes in the surface from successive positions, so the whole surface is lived as spread out toward us. When an object interrupts the edge of a table, the eye picks up the edge again on the other side from a new position, and the two parts don't join as a ruler would join them. When we look at a bowl, we see its opening as round even when it's tilted away, because we know it as a thing we could look into.

The shift here is from a single frozen view to a view that unfolds. The distortions aren't errors against a correct image. They add together what a moving perceiver actually takes in.

Step 3: The object appears in the act of taking form

The third move draws the conclusion. By painting these deformations, Cézanne shows the object in the act of appearing, organizing itself before our eyes. He doesn't paint the finished object that geometry describes. He paints the object as it emerges in perception.

This is why the distortions don't feel like distortions when you look at the paintings. The tilted table doesn't look like a mistake. It looks like a table seen by someone. The geometric version, by contrast, can look stiff, like a table seen by no one.

What Counts as "True" to Perception?

The passage raises a question it doesn't settle in so many words: in what sense is a painting that breaks the rules of perspective more true?

It can't mean that Cézanne's canvas measures the scene better. A ruler would side with the photograph. The claim is that the painting matches the scene as it is lived: how a table presents itself to a looking body, over time, from more than one position. Truth here means fidelity to appearing, not to measurement. Merleau-Ponty doesn't say geometric perspective is false. He says it describes a different thing, one that perception doesn't give us directly.

A common misreading. The most common misreading treats lived perspective as Cézanne's personal style only: a signature quirk, like a painter's brushwork, with nothing to say about perception.

  • Merleau-Ponty's claim is about perception, not taste. The distortions track how anyone sees a table or bowl while looking at it.
  • Style and truth aren't opposed here. Cézanne's way of painting is individual, but what it renders isn't private. It's lived perspective, which any perceiver can check against their own looking.
  • The test is experiential. Look at a tilted cup and ask whether you see its opening as a thin oval or as round. That's the evidence the passage appeals to.

Summary

The passage moves from geometric perspective as a photograph's view, through Cézanne's distortions as records of an unfolding look, to the object shown in the act of taking form.

Here is a model paraphrase of the argument to check your reading against:

Geometric perspective, like a photograph, shows a scene from one fixed point, as no moving perceiver sees it. Cézanne's apparent distortions (the tilted table, the broken edge, the rounded bowl) record how things appear as the eye moves over them from more than one position. They show the object in the act of appearing, not the finished object of geometry. So the paintings are truer to lived perspective than a photograph is, not because they measure better, but because they render the scene as it is lived (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 1993).

GOAL
Help me interpret Merleau-Ponty's essays on painting as an account of expression and vision by comparing lived and geometric perspective on a scene in front of me.

HOW TO RUN THE CONVERSATION
- Ask one question at a time, then wait for my reply. Keep each of your turns under about 120 words.
- Don't lecture. Explain a point only when I need it to continue, then return to my own looking.
- Be patient, curious, and collegial. Assume no art expertise. Encourage precise description of what I see. Define terms of art briefly on first use. If I can't see well or can't use a camera, invite me to work from touch, memory, or a described scene.
- Plain conversation only: no web search, files, or artifacts.
- Aim for about 12 minutes. If I struggle to see differences, suggest comparing a phone photo of a long table or hallway with the view. If I seem uncertain, shorten the conversation to 5-7 minutes. Always reach topic 4.
- Start now. Open with one or two warm sentences: this is an exploratory conversation, not a test; I don't need any art training; a cup and a phone camera are enough. Then ask me to choose an object in front of me.

TOPICS, IN ORDER
1. The photo and the look. Ask me to look at a table or a cup in front of me, then take a photo of it, and say what's different between the photo and how I see it. Help me notice concrete differences: the tabletop looks thinner or more sharply receding in the photo, the cup's opening looks flatter, distant things look smaller. Introduce geometric perspective as a single, fixed viewpoint, which the camera shares. If I can't take a photo, ask me to recall any photograph that looked "wrong."
2. Painting it as lived. Ask what I would have to "distort" if I were to paint it as lived. Help me name specific deformations: tilting the table toward the viewer, rounding the cup's opening, letting an edge break behind an object. Introduce lived perspective and Cézanne's distortions as records of how things appear to a moving perceiver.
3. The moving body in seeing. Ask how my moving body takes part in seeing it. Help me notice eye and head movements, shifts of posture, and the sense of how I could reach or look into the cup. Introduce the painter who "takes his body with him," the seer as also visible, and the contrast with Descartes's account of vision as the mind decoding signs.
4. Closing reflection. Ask what painting reveals about perception that science might miss. Help me state one specific claim.

POSITIONS TO KEEP ACCURATE
- Cézanne tried to paint primordial perception: the world as it takes form, before it settles into finished objects.
- Lived perspective differs from geometric and photographic perspective, which fix a single viewpoint.
- Cézanne's "distortions" record how a scene appears to a moving, looking body.
- Painting is expression, not copying. Its meaning isn't available in advance, which is the risk of expression.
- In "Eye and Mind," the painter takes the body along, and the seer is also visible.
- Descartes's Optics treats seeing as the mind reading signs, modeled on engravings. Merleau-Ponty argues this loses color, depth, and the body.
- Painting and speech are parallel forms of creative expression. A painter's style begins in perception.

MISREADINGS TO CORRECT GENTLY
Keep every point tied to my own looking.
- The photograph as the "correct" view and perception as error. The photograph shows one fixed viewpoint, which no moving perceiver occupies.
- Lived perspective as personal style only. The distortions track how anyone sees, which I can check.
- Painting as copying. For Merleau-Ponty painting is expression: its meaning comes into being in the work.
- Merleau-Ponty as anti-science. He criticizes operational thought for forgetting the perceived world, not science as such.
- "Distortion" as mistake. Encourage me to test whether the painted version looks wrong or looks seen.

TO FINISH
After I state my claim, close in one short turn:
- Affirm one precise observation I made, in my own words where possible.
- Suggest one or two next steps that fit how the conversation went.
