Mapping a Controversy: Stating Each Side So Its Advocates Would Agree

Most arguments about AI reach you with a verdict already attached. A headline says AI is taking jobs, or that the fear is overblown, and the article describes the other side mainly in order to dismiss it. If a friend asks what to make of the story, repeating either version passes along a verdict without the reasoning.

A position map does a different job. It states the question in dispute, gives each side's answer in words that side would sign, and lists the evidence each side relies on. It then says where that evidence is stronger, weaker, or missing. The writer's own view comes last and is labeled as the writer's.

Fair statement comes before evaluation for a practical reason. An argument you've met only in its opponents' summary is usually missing its strongest part, so rejecting that summary leaves the real argument untested. A reader who holds the view you've described badly will also stop trusting the rest of what you wrote.

The International AI Safety Report 2026 shows the method at full scale. More than 100 independent experts contributed to it, with Yoshua Bengio, a computer scientist at the University of Montreal, as chair. The report "does not recommend any policies" (Bengio and others 2026, "About this document").

On whether AI systems could escape human control, the report declines to name a winner. It says researchers' views on the likelihood "vary widely." Some researchers and company leaders treat loss of control as a serious possibility, and "others consider such scenarios implausible." The report then says what the disagreement rests on: "different assumptions about what future AI systems will be able to do, how they will behave, and how they will be deployed" (Bengio and others 2026, sec. 2.2.2).

Those few sentences are a map in miniature. Each position appears in terms its holders would accept, and the reader learns that the dispute turns on assumptions about future systems.

The same section doesn't treat every question as open. It gives a plain assessment of the systems that existed when it was written: they show early signs of the relevant abilities, and not at a level that would allow loss of control (Bengio and others 2026, sec. 2.2.2). The report separates what the evidence supports from what experts dispute.

A reader of your map will bring three doubts, and the map has to answer each one.

  • Is each side stated fairly? The test is whether a person who holds the position would accept your statement of it.
  • Is the evidence what it's said to be? A forecast, a laboratory test, and a set of payroll records support different kinds of claim.
  • Has the writer hidden their own view or their uncertainty? A map that pretends to have no author is harder to trust than one that states a view and says how firmly it's held.

In this project you'll choose one AI controversy that is live when you write and map it in 700 to 1,000 words, ending with your own view and what would change it.

Project Requirements and Instructions

Deliverable Overview

You'll write a position map of 700 to 1,000 words on one AI controversy that is live when you write. Your reader is a friend or colleague who has asked what to make of a story in the news and expects a straight answer. If the map favors one side by how it describes the other, they'll notice. If it treats every claim as equally supported, it won't help them.

Nobody grades the map. You check it yourself against the success criteria listed here, and you keep it.

Detailed Requirements

The map has six parts, in this order.

The question

State the controversy in one sentence, as a question with more than one defensible answer. Keep it narrow enough that evidence could bear on it. "Is AI good or bad for workers?" is too broad to map. "Has AI reduced the hiring of young workers in the United States?" can be mapped.

The positions

Give at least two positions. Write each in three to five sentences that its advocates would accept. Attribute each to a named person or document, and give the person's role and affiliation.

Then state the advocate's stake: what they or their employer gain or lose depending on which answer people believe. A stake can be money, reputation, or an institution's mission. Having a stake doesn't make a position wrong. Your reader is entitled to know about it.

The evidence

For each position, give its best evidence, usually one or two items, and label each item by kind.

KindWhat it isWhat it can support
Laboratory testA study in which researchers build the conditions themselves, as opposed to observing what happens in ordinary useWhat a system does under those conditions
SurveyA study that asks a sample of people the same questions and reports the share giving each answerWhat people say they do or think
Field dataRecords of what happened in ordinary use, such as payroll, sales, or usage recordsA pattern in the real world, often without its cause
Court rulingA court's decision on one disputeWhat the law requires in that case and jurisdiction
ForecastA statement about what will or may happen, which can't be checked until the time arrivesWhat its author expects, and on what assumptions

If an item fits none of these, describe it in your own words.

Where the evidence stands

Separate three things: findings that both sides accept, interpretations of those findings that the sides dispute, and forecasts. Then say where the evidence is stronger, weaker, or absent. If it's lopsided, say so.

Your view

State your current answer to the question, your confidence in plain words, and one or two specific things that would change your mind.

The sources

List every source with its author, date, title, and a named link. A named link shows the name of the site or document as the link text in place of a web address. In-text citations are welcome, in the form (Author Year).

Success Criteria

Your map is complete when:

  • The controversy is stated as a question
  • At least two positions are stated as their advocates would accept
  • Each position names an advocate, with role and stake
  • Each piece of evidence is labeled by kind
  • You said where the evidence is stronger, weaker, or absent, without forcing a balance
  • You stated your view, your confidence, and what would change it
  • Every source is listed with a named link

Step-by-Step Guidance

The steps use one running example, a dispute over entry-level jobs, described as it stood in October 2026. The example shows the method. Choose a different controversy for your own map.

Step 1: Choose a controversy that's live now and state it as a question with more than one defensible answer

Start from a story you've seen in the past few weeks. Find the claim under dispute and turn it into a question. The example question: has AI reduced the hiring of young workers in the United States?

Step 2: Find a primary statement of each position by someone who holds it

A primary statement is a paper, essay, ruling, official statement, or transcript by the person or body that holds the position. A news article about a position can lead you to one. The example uses the published summary of a Stanford University study and a critique of that study published by the Economic Innovation Group, a policy organization.

Step 3: Write each position in three to five sentences, with the advocate's role and stake

Position A. Three researchers at Stanford University's Digital Economy Lab report a warning sign in payroll records. Employment of workers aged 22 to 25 in AI-exposed occupations stands 19 percent below where it would be had it kept pace with that of less-exposed peers, and experienced workers show no comparable gap. The authors call these "early, descriptive indicators" and offer them "rather than causal estimates" (Brynjolfsson, Chandar, and Chen 2026). Stake: professional reputation, since the finding carries their names.

Position B. Two economists at Google argue that interest rates explain the pattern. They attribute it to "the sharpest monetary policy tightening cycle in four decades," which began in March 2022. Job postings in the most AI-exposed occupations peaked that spring, months before the first widely used AI chatbot was released in November 2022 (Iscenko and Curto Millet 2026). Stake: their employer develops and sells AI systems and benefits if AI isn't blamed for job losses.

Position A includes its own caveat because its authors do. Leaving the caveat out would turn their claim into one they haven't made.

Step 4: List each position's best evidence and label its kind

Position A rests on field data: payroll records covering millions of United States workers through June 2026. Position B rests on field data too, in the form of job postings, and on an interpretation of their timing. A third source helps with both. The Budget Lab at Yale, a policy research center, analyzed a government survey of households and reported "no statistically or economically significant effects as of yet." It describes that data as "somewhat underpowered" for small groups such as recent graduates (Gimbel, Kendall, and Nunn 2026).

Step 5: Write where the evidence is stronger, weaker, or absent

In the example, the evidence is stronger on the pattern. Both sides accept that the gap appears in the payroll records, so presenting its existence as an open question would force a balance the evidence doesn't support. The evidence is weaker on the cause, which is the point in dispute. A causal estimate, meaning a result that measures how much one thing changed another with other explanations ruled out, is absent. Any statement about entry-level jobs in the coming years is a forecast and belongs under that label.

Step 6: State your view, your confidence, and what would change it; add sources with named links

A workable form is: "My current view is… My confidence is low, moderate, or high, because… I'd change my view if…" Make the last part specific. In the example, a study comparing similar firms that did and didn't adopt AI tools would bear on the cause. "More research" is too vague to count.

Resources

  • Primary sources are the base of the map: reports from governments and international bodies, court rulings, research papers, and essays or interview transcripts by the people who hold each position.
  • Your own notes or practice writing on any AI debate can supply a topic or a first draft of a position.
  • An AI assistant can help you find primary statements or test whether your statement of a position is fair. Open every source yourself. An assistant's summary isn't a source, and assistants sometimes misdescribe or invent references. Don't paste in anything confidential.

Common Pitfalls

  • Stating one side in its own words and the other in its critics'. Take each position from a person who holds it.
  • Citing a forecast as a finding. A prediction about the future is evidence of what its author expects.
  • Leaving out who benefits. State the stake for every advocate, including the ones you agree with.
  • Splitting the difference when the evidence is lopsided. Where one claim is far better supported, say so.
  • Hiding your own view. Your reader asked for a straight answer. Give one, with your confidence.

Writing Assignment 60 min

Map a Live Controversy

Overview

You'll write a position map of one AI controversy that is in the news now. Picture the reader as a friend or colleague who asked what to make of the story and expects a straight answer. The map is yours to keep, and you check it yourself with the list at the end.

Writing Prompt

Write a 700 to 1,000 word position map of one current AI controversy. Give each side in its strongest form, the evidence it rests on, where the evidence is stronger, and what would change your own view.

Use these six parts, in order:

  1. The controversy, stated as a question with more than one defensible answer
  2. At least two positions, each in three to five sentences its advocates would accept, with a named advocate, their role, and their stake
  3. Each position's best evidence, labeled by kind: laboratory test, survey, field data, court ruling, or forecast
  4. Where the evidence is stronger, weaker, or absent
  5. Your current view, your confidence, and what would change it
  6. Your sources, each with a named link

Tips

  • Pick a controversy narrow enough to cover in the word limit. One disputed claim maps better than a whole topic.
  • Work from statements by people who hold each position. Open every source you list.
  • Draft the position you disagree with first, while you have the most patience for it.
  • Keep each advocate's own caveats. Removing them makes a claim stronger than the one its authors made.
  • Spend about 10 minutes choosing and finding sources, 35 writing, and 15 checking.
  • You may draw on notes or practice writing of your own. Citations are welcome, in the form (Author Year).

Self-Check

When you're done, check that:

  • The controversy is stated as a question
  • At least two positions are stated as their advocates would accept
  • Each position names an advocate, with role and stake
  • Each piece of evidence is labeled by kind
  • You said where the evidence is stronger, weaker, or absent, without forcing a balance
  • You stated your view, your confidence, and what would change it
  • Every source is listed with a named link

Nothing is uploaded. Write in your own notebook or document and keep it.
