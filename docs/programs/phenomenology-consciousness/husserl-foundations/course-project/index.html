Course Project

Describe an Experience Phenomenologically

In this project, you'll write a phenomenological description of an everyday experience, such as listening to a piece of music, drinking a cup of coffee, or walking up a flight of stairs. You'll apply the epoché, analyze the experience's intentional structure, and describe its temporal and bodily dimensions in precise phenomenological terms. Writing a careful first-person description is a core research skill in phenomenology and in any consciousness research that takes experience seriously as data.

What you will be able to do

  • Produce a phenomenological description that suspends natural-attitude assumptions and attends to how an experience is given
  • Integrate intentional, temporal, and bodily structures into a single analysis of one experience

Rigorous First-Person Description as a Research Skill

Researchers trained in third-person methods often doubt first-person description, and with reason. People are frequently wrong about their own current experience, and confidently so (Schwitzgebel 2008). If a report of experience is only an unchecked impression, it can't be treated as data.

Professional Context

Imagine you're a graduate researcher preparing the methods section of a study of everyday experience. Your colleagues are trained in experiments, questionnaires, and physiological measures. They'll read your first-person method with skepticism. For them, "describe what it was like" sounds like an invitation to anecdote.

Your task is to show them otherwise. You'll write a rigorous phenomenological description of one ordinary experience that can serve as a model of first-person method. It needs to be disciplined, precise, and checkable: others who have the same kind of experience should be able to test the description against their own.

The stakes are real. If the description reads as anecdote, your colleagues will dismiss first-person data. If it shows method, it makes the case that careful description is a legitimate source of evidence. Researchers have argued for exactly this. Structured interview methods can guide people past their assumptions to what they actually experienced (Petitmengin 2006). And phenomenological insights can be built into the design of experiments rather than left to untrained reports (Gallagher 2003).

What You'll Create

You'll write a 1,000–1,500 word essay describing one everyday experience after performing the epoché. The essay has five parts:

  • what you bracketed
  • the experience's intentional structure
  • its temporal structure
  • the role of your lived body
  • at least two invariant features you found through free variation

Skills in Action

The project brings several methods together in one description. The epoché sets aside assumptions about existence and causes, so that you can attend to how the experience is given. Intentional analysis separates the act from the object as meant. Temporal analysis describes how the experience unfolds, with its now-phase, the just-past it retains, and the about-to-come it anticipates. Bodily analysis describes the lived body's role: its orientation, its felt movement, and its sensings. Free variation tests which features belong to the kind of experience, not just to this one occasion.

None of these works well alone. A temporal analysis without the epoché slides into causal explanation. An intentional analysis without the body loses the perceiver's position. The description is rigorous because the methods check one another.

Portfolio Value

A finished essay is a research writing sample: a worked demonstration that you can produce first-person data with method behind it. It's the kind of text that could anchor the methods section of a study, support a proposal for mixed-methods research, or show a reviewer that your first-person claims are disciplined rather than impressionistic.

Project Requirements and Instructions

Deliverable Overview

You'll write one essay: a phenomenological description of one everyday experience, 1,000–1,500 words long. The essay describes the experience after the epoché and has five required components:

  1. A brief statement of what you bracketed
  2. An analysis of the experience's intentional structure: the act, and the object as meant
  3. A description of its temporal structure: primal impression, retention, and protention
  4. A description of the role of your lived body
  5. At least two invariant features you identified through free variation

Use a section heading for each component so your analysis is easy to follow. If you cite sources, use Chicago author-date citations and include a References list. The References list doesn't count toward the word limit. Citations are optional.

Detailed Requirements

1. What You Bracketed

In a short opening section, state what you suspended through the epoché. This means your assumptions about what the object is made of and what causes the experience, together with any scientific account of it. The epoché doesn't doubt or deny these things. It sets them aside so that you can attend to how the experience is given. A few sentences are enough, but they should be specific to your experience.

2. Intentional Structure

Identify the act quality, meaning the kind of act: perceiving, tasting, listening, or climbing. Then describe the object as meant, in "as" phrases: the coffee as tasted from the first sip, the stairs as rising ahead. Describe how the object is given, not what it is. Include its perspective, its partial givenness, and its place in a surrounding field.

3. Temporal Structure

Describe how the experience unfolds. Name the now-phase, which is the primal impression. Name what is still held as just past, which is retention. Name what is anticipated as about to come, which is protention. Ground each term in a specific moment of the experience, and describe how the phases shift as the experience continues.

4. The Lived Body

Describe the role of your body as lived, not as a physical object. Consider three aspects:

  • the body as the "here" from which everything is oriented
  • the felt sense of your own movement (kinaesthesis), and how it shapes what you perceive
  • sensings localized in the body

Include kinaesthesis where movement is part of the experience.

5. Invariant Features

Vary the experience in imagination: change the object's features, your position, the setting, or the pace. Report at least two features that stayed constant across every variation, along with a brief account of the variations that revealed them. Invariants belong to the kind of experience, such as perspectival givenness or a retained just-past. They aren't accidental properties of this occasion.

Success Criteria

Your essay meets the requirements when it:

  • States what was bracketed and keeps the epoché throughout, with no causal or mechanistic claims in the description
  • Identifies the act quality correctly and describes the object as meant
  • Describes the temporal structure using primal impression, retention, and protention, grounded in the experience
  • Describes the lived body's role, including kinaesthesis or bodily orientation where relevant
  • Reports at least two invariant features with a brief account of the variation that revealed them
  • Uses concrete, first-person descriptive detail throughout rather than general theory

Quality Indicators

Adequate work includes all five components, uses the technical terms correctly, and stays free of causal claims.

Excellent work does more. It describes the manner of givenness throughout, including perspective, horizon, temporal flow, and felt movement. It keeps every technical term tied to a concrete moment of the experience. Its components connect: the temporal analysis shows how the object as meant unfolds, and the bodily analysis shows how movement shapes what's given. Its invariants are features of the kind of experience, found through variation that is actually reported.

Step-by-Step Guidance

Step 1: Choose a short, repeatable experience. Pick something that takes seconds to a minute and that you can repeat: a sip, a short melody, a flight of stairs, opening a door. A short experience keeps the description focused. A repeatable one lets you check it.

Step 2: Live through it two or three times, taking notes. Attend to how it's given each time. Jot down perspective, what stands out and what recedes, what you anticipate, what lingers, and what you feel in your body. Don't theorize yet.

Step 3: Write down what you're bracketing. List the assumptions you'll set aside: what the object is made of, how your senses work, what causes the experience. Keep the list at hand as a check.

Step 4: Draft the intentional, temporal, and bodily analyses. Write each as a section. Use your notes. Ground every technical term in a specific moment.

Step 5: Perform free variation and record the invariants. Vary the experience in imagination: a different object, speed, position, or setting. Note what can't change without the experience ceasing to be the kind it is. Record at least two invariants, and say how you found them.

Step 6: Revise to remove causal or mechanistic language. Reread for words such as "brain," "signal," "receptor," "caused," and "processed." Rewrite each as a description of how things appeared.

Resources

  • Component checklist. Before you finish, confirm that your essay has these headed sections:
    1. What I bracketed
    2. Intentional structure
    3. Temporal structure
    4. The lived body
    5. Invariant features
  • Annotated exemplar excerpt. The excerpt below shows a strong temporal analysis of a different experience from yours: a kettle coming to the boil. Use it as a model of grounding each term in a moment, not as a template to copy.

The kettle's sound is given now as a low hiss, and the hiss is what I hear in primal impression. But it isn't heard alone. The quieter hiss of a moment ago is still there, retained as just past, so the present sound is heard as louder than it was, not simply as loud. As the hiss rises to a rumble, the earlier hiss sinks further back without vanishing. All the while, I'm leaning toward the click of the switch. That's a protention, open and unspecific as to exactly when, but definite enough that when the click comes, it arrives as expected.

Annotations: The excerpt grounds each term in a moment: the now-hiss, the retained quieter hiss, the anticipated click. It shows retention shaping how the present sounds ("louder than it was"). It uses no causal vocabulary about water, heat, or hearing.

  • Two refreshers on method.
    • The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Husserl covers the epoché, reduction, time-consciousness, and the lived body (Zahavi 2025, §§3–5).
    • Robert Sokolowski's introduction to phenomenology treats perception and description in accessible terms (Sokolowski 2000).
  • Your own notes. You may draw on any notes or journal entries you've written as you worked through these ideas.

Tips and Common Pitfalls

  • Choosing an experience that's too complex. A dinner party or a commute has too many parts. Pick one short, repeatable act.
  • Slipping into causal explanation. "My taste buds detected bitterness" is causal. "The bitterness arrived after the warmth, at the back of the tongue" is descriptive.
  • Listing theory without describing. Defining retention in general does little. Showing retention at work in your experience does a lot.
  • Treating the epoché as doubt. You aren't questioning whether the coffee exists. You're setting that question aside to attend to how the coffee is given.
