Philosophical Roots: Brentano's Intentionality

This lesson introduces Franz Brentano's thesis that every mental act is directed toward an object, which he called intentional inexistence, and his proposal that directedness is the mark of the mental. You'll be able to explain the thesis, apply it to perceiving, judging, and desiring, and describe how his students reworked it. Intentionality remains a central concept in philosophy of mind, so knowing its origin helps you read current debates about mental representation with more precision.

  • Describe Brentano's thesis of intentionality and its role as phenomenology's starting point

Brentano's Intentionality and the Puzzle of Aboutness in Current Research

Arguments about whether AI systems understand anything often turn on a question much older than computing: what makes a state about something? A sentence in a book is about the weather because readers take it that way. Your fear of a storm seems to be about the storm on its own, whether or not anyone interprets it.

Philosophers call this aboutness intentionality. The word has nothing to do with intending to do something. It names the way a mental state is directed at an object.

Ordinary cases show how odd this is. You can fear a storm that never comes. You can think of a friend on another continent, or of a character who never existed. In each case, your mental state is directed at something that is either absent or nowhere at all.

Compare an ordinary physical relation. A rock can't hit a window that doesn't exist, because both ends of the relation have to be there. Aboutness seems to work with one end missing. Any theory that treats minds as physical systems has to say what kind of relation this is, or explain why it only looks like one. Current work on mental representation spends much of its effort on exactly this problem (Jacob 2023).

The puzzle sharpens with judgment. The belief that there are no unicorns is about unicorns, and it's true precisely because nothing answers to it. A theory of mind has to explain how a true thought can be directed at nothing at all.

The modern form of the problem starts with Franz Brentano. In 1874, he proposed that directedness toward an object is what all mental phenomena share and what no physical phenomenon has (Brentano [1874] 2015, bk. II, ch. 1, §5; Jacob 2023). That claim gave the problem a name and put it at the center of the study of mind. It also gave phenomenology its starting point: the task of describing how consciousness is directed at its objects.

A precise grasp of intentionality pays off across research on the mind:

  • You can separate what a state is about from the state itself, a distinction that debates about representation often blur.
  • You can spot a theory that handles thoughts about existing things but quietly ignores thoughts about things that don't exist.
  • You can read claims about machine "understanding" with a clear sense of which feature of mind is at issue.

Most questions about consciousness run into aboutness sooner or later. The concept's history shows which parts of the problem have been answered and which are still open.

Brentano's Intentional Inexistence and His Three Classes of Mental Acts

Introduction

Nearly every account of intentionality in philosophy of mind starts from the same short passage in Franz Brentano's Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint (1874). The passage is famous partly because its wording is hard to pin down.

This reading explains what the passage claims, how its key terms work, and how Brentano sorted mental acts into three basic classes. Citations give the book, chapter, and section of the Psychology, which stay stable across editions.

The Thesis

Brentano wanted to mark off mental phenomena from physical ones. By "physical phenomena" he meant what sensation presents, such as a color, a sound, warmth, or a shape. "Mental phenomena" are acts such as seeing a color, hearing a sound, judging, or wishing (Brentano [1874] 2015, bk. II, ch. 1; Huemer 2019).

His proposal is that every mental phenomenon has what medieval Scholastics called the intentional inexistence of an object: the object exists "in" the act as its object. Brentano offers other names for the same feature, including direction toward an object and "immanent objectivity." Every mental phenomenon includes something as its object, though not all in the same way (bk. II, ch. 1, §5).

Two words need care. "Intentional" comes from the scholastic vocabulary of intentio, the mind's aiming at something. It has nothing to do with doing something on purpose. "Inexistence" means existence in, not nonexistence. Brentano himself notes that his phrasings aren't free of ambiguity.

The general feature is intentionality: the directedness of a mental act toward an object. Each case has two sides. The act is a mental occurrence such as seeing, judging, or loving. The object is what the act is directed at.

Act and Object

The thesis applies whether or not the object exists outside the act.

ActDirected atDoes the object exist?
Seeing a colorThe colorYes
Hearing a melodyThe melodyYes
Fearing a storm that never comesThe stormNo
Thinking of a unicornA unicornNo
Judging that it will rain tomorrowRain tomorrowNot yet settled

Each row has an act and an object, and the act is directed in the same way in every row. That's why Brentano speaks of the object as existing "in" the act. It's also where the trouble starts. If the object exists in the act, is the unicorn somehow in the mind? The 1874 wording leaves the question open (Huemer 2019).

Three Classes of Mental Acts

Brentano sorts mental acts by the way they are directed at their objects, not by what their objects are (bk. II, ch. 6):

  • A presentation (Vorstellung) is having something before the mind at all, as in seeing a color or imagining a shape. Every other act either is a presentation or is based on one.
  • A judgment accepts or rejects a presented object: it affirms or denies that the object exists. For Brentano, a judgment needn't join a subject to a predicate. "There's a storm" simply affirms a storm.
  • Phenomena of love and hate take a positive or negative stance toward a presented object. This class covers emotions, desire, and will.

The classes are layered. Judging that a storm is coming presupposes presenting a storm, and fearing the storm presupposes presenting it too. Presentation is the base on which the rest of mental life is built.

Conclusion

Brentano's thesis says every mental act has an object, and that the act has it whether or not the object exists. His three classes sort acts by how they're directed: presenting, accepting or rejecting, and loving or hating. The thesis leaves one question unsettled: in what sense the object is "in" the act.

Key Terms

  • Intentional inexistence: Brentano's scholastic term for the way every mental act contains an object: the object exists "in" the act as its object, whether or not it exists outside it.
  • Intentionality: The directedness of a mental act toward an object.
  • Act: A mental occurrence such as seeing, judging, or loving.
  • Object: What a mental act is directed at.
  • Presentation (Vorstellung): In Brentano, the basic class of mental acts: having something before the mind at all. Every other act either is a presentation or is based on one.
  • Judgment: In Brentano, the class of acts that accept or reject a presented object as existing.
  • Phenomena of love and hate: In Brentano, the class of acts that take a positive or negative stance toward a presented object, including emotion, desire, and will.

Intentionality as the Mark of the Mental: Brentano's Thesis and Its Challenges

Introduction

Philosophers of mind still debate whether any single feature separates the mental from the physical. The best-known candidate comes from Franz Brentano: directedness toward an object (Jacob 2023).

This reading explains Brentano's mark-of-the-mental thesis, the hard cases that challenge it, and his descriptive psychology, the method that made him a precursor of phenomenology. Citations to Brentano's Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint (1874) give the book, chapter, and section.

The Thesis

Brentano draws the line between two kinds of phenomena. Mental phenomena are acts such as seeing, hearing, judging, and wishing. Physical phenomena, in his sense, are the contents that sensation presents, such as a color, a sound, or warmth. They are not physical objects in the modern sense.

He then proposes that intentionality, the directedness of a mental act toward an object, is exclusive to mental phenomena. Physical phenomena show nothing like it. Mental phenomena can therefore be defined as those that contain an object intentionally (Brentano [1874] 2015, bk. II, ch. 1, §5). This is the mark-of-the-mental thesis.

The thesis makes two claims at once:

  • Necessity: whatever is mental is directed at an object.
  • Sufficiency: whatever is directed at an object is mental.

Brentano offered other criteria in the same chapter, such as the claim that mental phenomena are the only objects of inner perception. Directedness is the criterion that lasted (Huemer 2019).

Hard Cases

Most challenges target the necessity claim (Jacob 2023):

  • Moods. Diffuse anxiety or elation doesn't seem to be about anything in particular. You can be anxious without being anxious about anything you could name.
  • Bodily sensations. A dull ache or an itch seems to be a felt state, not a state directed at something.

Defenders of the thesis have proposed reinterpretations. A sensation might be directed at a bodily location or condition: an ache in the knee presents something going on in the knee. A mood might be directed at the world as a whole, rather than at any one thing in it. Critics reply that these readings stretch "directedness" until it no longer marks anything. Others concede that some mental states lack an object, give up the necessity claim, and keep intentionality as a central feature of mind rather than a defining one. The debate remains open (Jacob 2023).

The sufficiency claim faces pressure from the other side. If whatever is directed at an object counts as mental, then deciding whether a machine's states are genuinely about anything amounts to deciding whether it has mental states. That consequence is one reason the thesis keeps returning in debates about artificial intelligence.

Descriptive Psychology

Brentano's method matters as much as his thesis. He held that psychology should first describe and classify mental phenomena as they're given in inner perception, before trying to explain them. In lectures of the late 1880s, he called this descriptive psychology and set it apart from genetic psychology, which explains how mental phenomena arise, including from physiological causes (Brentano [1982] 1995; Huemer 2019).

Descriptive psychology asks what kinds of mental acts there are, what their parts are, and how those parts combine. Brentano thought these questions could be answered with a kind of exactness, not merely as statistical generalizations. The idea that consciousness can be described rigorously before it is explained, and that its basic structure is directedness, passed to students who attended his Vienna lectures, including Edmund Husserl (Huemer 2019).

Conclusion

Brentano proposed that directedness toward an object belongs to all and only mental phenomena. Moods and bodily sensations press on the "all" side. Questions about machines press on the "only" side. Neither challenge is settled. His descriptive psychology supplied the method phenomenology inherited: describe and classify mental acts before explaining them.

Key Terms

  • Mental phenomena: In Brentano, acts such as seeing, hearing, judging, and wishing.
  • Physical phenomena: In Brentano, the contents that sensation presents, such as a color, a sound, or warmth, rather than physical objects in the modern sense.
  • Intentionality: The directedness of a mental act toward an object.
  • Mark-of-the-mental thesis: Brentano's proposal that intentionality belongs to all and only mental phenomena.
  • Descriptive psychology: Brentano's program of describing and classifying mental phenomena as given in inner perception, before explaining them.
  • Genetic psychology: In Brentano, the branch of psychology that explains how mental phenomena arise, including from physiological causes.

Twardowski's Content–Object Distinction and Meinong's Theory of Objects

Introduction

Franz Brentano held that every mental act is directed at an object that exists "in" the act (Brentano [1874] 2015, bk. II, ch. 1, §5). Within twenty years, two of his students had pulled that claim apart. The question they pressed was this: when you think of something that doesn't exist, what exactly are you thinking of?

This reading explains Kazimierz Twardowski's distinction between the content and the object of a presentation, and Alexius Meinong's theory of objects that don't exist.

The Problem Brentano Left

For Brentano, every act, a mental occurrence such as seeing, judging, or loving, has an object: what the act is directed at. He said the object exists "in" the act.

That phrasing merges two things. Think of Vienna. Your thought is about a city on the Danube, not about something in your mind. Yet whatever is "in" your mind can't be a city on the Danube. Something has to give.

Twardowski: Content and Object

Twardowski's On the Content and Object of Presentations (1894) resolves the problem by splitting Brentano's single notion in two. Every presentation involves an act, a content, and an object (Twardowski [1894] 1977).

  • The content exists in the mind, alongside the act. It is the mental item through which the act is directed at its object, and it exists as long as the act does.
  • The object is what the presentation is of. It may or may not exist. It also has properties the content lacks. A presented mountain is large and made of rock, and the content of your presentation is neither.

Two different contents can present one object. "The capital of Austria" and "the city where Brentano lectured in the 1880s" present the same city through different contents.

Twardowski held that every presentation has an object, even a presentation of something impossible, such as a round square. The round square doesn't exist and couldn't exist, but it is presented. On this view, what is "in" the mind is the content, never the object.

Meinong: Objects Without Being

Alexius Meinong, another of Brentano's students, took the object side further. In "The Theory of Objects" (1904), he argued that the range of objects is wider than the range of things that exist (Meinong [1904] 1960). The golden mountain and the round square are objects. They can be thought about, and they have the properties they're thought to have: the golden mountain is golden. But the golden mountain doesn't exist, and the round square couldn't.

Meinong's theory of objects rests on the independence of being-so from being. What an object is like (its Sosein, or being-so) doesn't depend on whether it exists (its Sein, or being). A golden mountain can be golden without being anywhere at all. Meinong accepted the consequence that some objects have no being of any kind.

Critics found this ontology crowded: it fills the world with objects that have no being. Current theories of intentionality still debate whether thoughts about things that don't exist require nonexistent objects, or whether the same work can be done some other way (Jacob 2023).

Two Answers Compared

TwardowskiMeinong
What is "in" the mindThe contentThe content (Meinong keeps a content–object distinction)
What the act is directed atThe object, which may not existThe object, which may lack being altogether
Status of a round squarePresented, but doesn't existAn object with properties but no being

Conclusion

Twardowski located the mental item in the content and freed the object to exist or not. Meinong accepted the consequence: if objects needn't exist, the range of objects includes things with no being at all. Both keep Brentano's claim that every act is directed at an object. They differ over what kind of thing that object is.

Key Terms

  • Act: A mental occurrence such as seeing, judging, or loving.
  • Object: What a mental act is directed at.
  • Content: In Twardowski, the mental item that exists in the mind with the act, and through which the act is directed at its object.
  • Theory of objects: Meinong's account of objects in general, including objects that don't or can't exist.
  • Independence of being-so from being: Meinong's principle that what an object is like (Sosein) doesn't depend on whether it exists (Sein).

Husserl's Objection to Immanent Objects and Four Positions on the Intentional Object

Introduction

Brentano's students agreed that every mental act is directed at an object. They disagreed about what that object is. The disagreement still shapes theories of mental representation (Jacob 2023).

This reading explains Edmund Husserl's early objection to treating the object as something inside the mind, compares four positions on the intentional object, and notes why the problem persists. Citations to Husserl's Logical Investigations give the Investigation and section ("Inv. V, §11"), which stay stable across editions.

Husserl's Objection

In the Fifth Logical Investigation (1901), Husserl rejects the idea that the object of an act exists inside consciousness. His example is presenting the god Jupiter. When you present Jupiter, you present the god of Roman myth, not a mental item. Your experience is directed at Jupiter whether or not Jupiter exists, and no component of the experience is a mental Jupiter (Husserl [1900–1901] 2001, Inv. V, §11).

If Jupiter doesn't exist, nothing further is needed. The experience is simply an experience of this kind, directed in this way. If the object does exist, as when you present Berlin, the object you intend and the actual city are one and the same.

This yields a clean account of the intentional object: the object as intended, which is the object itself, not a mental copy. It also denies that intentionality is a relation between two existing items. Being directed is a feature of the experience itself, and it holds whether or not anything answers to it.

Husserl applies the same point against the image theory, the view that we perceive or think of things by having images of them in the mind. An image depicts something only for a consciousness that already intends what's depicted. Images therefore presuppose intentionality and can't explain it (Husserl [1900–1901] 2001, Inv. V, §11 and appendix to §§11 and 20).

The target of both objections is the immanent object: an object taken to exist inside the mental act, as one reading of Brentano's term intentional inexistence suggests. That term names Brentano's claim that the object exists "in" the act as its object, whether or not it exists outside it (Brentano [1874] 2015, bk. II, ch. 1, §5).

Four Positions Compared

Where the object isObjects that don't existMain difficulty
Brentano ([1874] 2015)"In" the act, as its intentional objectExist in the act as objectsUnclear whether the object is a mental item or the thing itself
Twardowski ([1894] 1977)Outside the mind, reached through a mental contentAre presented but don't existNeeds objects that don't exist
Meinong ([1904] 1960)In a range of objects wider than what existsHave properties but no beingFills the world with objects that lack being
Husserl ([1900–1901] 2001)Nowhere inside the act; the act is directed at the object itselfNeed nothing further; the act is directed as it isMust explain directedness when nothing answers to it

Why the Problem Persists

Current theories of mental representation still face the same choice (Jacob 2023). If intentionality is a relation, what stands at the far end when the object doesn't exist? Some theories posit abstract contents, such as propositions. Some posit nonexistent objects. Some deny that intentionality is a relation at all. Each option echoes one of the four positions above, and none has won general agreement.

Conclusion

Brentano's formula held together two things his successors separated: the mental act and what it's about. Twardowski put a content in the mind and let the object go free. Meinong gave objects that don't exist a standing of their own. Husserl denied that anything object-like sits inside the act. What a thought is related to, when its object doesn't exist, is still an open question.

Key Terms

  • Intentional object: For Husserl, the object as intended by an act. It is the object itself, not a mental copy of it.
  • Image theory: The view that we perceive or think of things by having images of them in the mind. Husserl argued that images presuppose intentionality.
  • Immanent object: An object taken to exist inside the mental act.
  • Intentional inexistence: Brentano's scholastic term for the way every mental act contains an object: the object exists "in" the act as its object, whether or not it exists outside it.

Guided Close Reading: Brentano's Intentional Inexistence Passage in the Psychology

Introduction

Brentano's passage on intentional inexistence is a single paragraph. Every major theory of intentionality since has had to decide what it means. This reading walks through it phrase by phrase: its scholastic vocabulary, its central term, and its list of examples. It then looks at the ambiguity that divided Brentano's readers, and closes with a two-sentence paraphrase you can check your own reading against.

Locating the Passage

The passage is in Franz Brentano's Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint (1874), Book II, Chapter 1, Section 5 (Brentano [1874] 2015, bk. II, ch. 1, §5). Short phrases below come from the English translation by Rancurello, Terrell, and McAlister. Everything else is paraphrase.

If you don't have the book, the free Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Brentano quotes the passage in full and discusses it (Huemer 2019). The German original is also free online, where the key phrase reads intentionale (auch wohl mentale) Inexistenz.

Before you start, note where the passage sits. The chapter's task is to find a feature that separates mental phenomena, such as seeing or judging, from physical phenomena, such as the colors and sounds that sensation presents. Brentano has considered several candidate features. This paragraph gives the one he treats as decisive.

Walking Through the Passage

Step 1: Trace the scholastic source of "intentional"

The passage opens by crediting its key term to "the Scholastics of the Middle Ages." Brentano signals that he is reviving an old term, not inventing a new one.

The Latin intentio comes from intendere, "to stretch toward" or "to aim at." In medieval philosophy, the vocabulary of intentio was used for the way things exist in the mind as understood, as opposed to the way they exist in nature. Brentano adds a note tracing the idea back through medieval authors to Aristotle. For Brentano, the term names the mind's being directed at, or containing, an object.

Keep one thing in view from the start. Nothing in this history connects "intentional" with purpose or deliberate action.

Step 2: Read "inexistence" as existence in

The central phrase is "intentional (or mental) inexistence of an object." The prefix in- here means "in," not "not." Inexistence is existence in something, the way the Latin inesse means "to be in." The object in-exists in the mental act as its object.

Brentano then offers alternative expressions for the same feature: "reference to a content," "direction toward an object," and "immanent objectivity." He adds two qualifications. First, "object" here shouldn't be understood as meaning a real thing. Second, none of these expressions is wholly free of ambiguity.

The alternatives don't all say the same thing. "Direction toward an object" describes the act as pointing beyond itself. "Immanent objectivity" describes the object as lying within the act. Brentano treats them as equivalent, and that's where the trouble starts.

The qualification about "object" matters too. Brentano isn't saying that every mental act is directed at a physical thing, or even at something real. The object is whatever the act is directed at, taken just as the act takes it. A dreaded exam, a remembered friend, and an imagined dragon all count.

Step 3: Read the list of examples

Brentano then makes the claim concrete. Every mental phenomenon includes something as object within itself, though not all in the same way. In presentation, something is presented. In judgment, something is affirmed or denied. In love, something is loved. In hate, something is hated. In desire, something is desired.

Two features of the list matter. First, it runs through the kinds of mental act Brentano recognizes: presenting, judging, and the emotional and volitional acts he groups under love and hate. Second, the list is grammatical as much as psychological. Each verb takes an object: presenting something, loving something. That slot is filled whether or not the thing exists. You can love a fictional character, and you can desire a future that never arrives.

The paragraph ends by drawing the conclusion. This intentional inexistence belongs exclusively to mental phenomena, and no physical phenomenon shows anything like it. Mental phenomena can therefore be defined as those that contain an object intentionally.

This last sentence turns a description into a definition. Up to this point, Brentano has said what every mental phenomenon has. Now he says that having it is what makes a phenomenon mental. That move from "all" to "all and only" is the mark-of-the-mental thesis, and it's where most later criticism aims.

Key Considerations

The central ambiguity. Is the object inside the act, or does the act point beyond itself to the object?

  • The immanent-object reading. On this reading, the object is a mental entity that exists in the mind, just as "in-existence" and "immanent objectivity" suggest. Thinking of Vienna is then a relation to something mental.
  • The directedness reading. On this reading, the passage describes directedness toward the object itself, and "in" is a manner of speaking. Thinking of Vienna is then directed at Vienna.

The passage supports both readings, and scholars dispute which Brentano meant in 1874. His later work rejected immanent objects altogether (Huemer 2019). The choice matters because it decides what a thought about something nonexistent is related to: a mental item, or nothing at all.

Terms and translation.

  • "Inexistence" renders the German Inexistenz. English readers often hear "nonexistence." Some commentators prefer "in-existence," with a hyphen, to block that reading.
  • "Presentation" renders Vorstellung, which older English translations sometimes render as "idea." It means having something before the mind, not a picture or a claim.
  • "Mental" in "intentional (or mental) inexistence" signals that the object exists in the mental act. It doesn't mean the object is imaginary.

A common misreading. It's natural to take "intentional" to mean "deliberate" or "on purpose." In this passage it means directed. An unwanted memory, a sudden fright, and an idle daydream are all intentional in Brentano's sense, because each is directed at something. None of them is intended in the everyday sense.

Summary

The passage revives a scholastic term, treats inexistence as existence in the act, and illustrates the thesis with each kind of mental act. It also leaves open whether the object is inside the act or merely aimed at by it. Here is a model paraphrase to check your reading against:

Every mental phenomenon is directed at an object: in presenting, something is presented; in judging, something is affirmed or denied; in loving or hating, something is loved or hated. This directedness, which Brentano calls the intentional inexistence of an object, belongs to all mental phenomena and to no physical phenomenon (Brentano [1874] 2015, bk. II, ch. 1, §5).

1. Three acts and their objects. Ask me to name three things I thought, perceived, or wanted in the past hour, and what each one was about. Briefly restate the classes if needed: a presentation is having something before the mind; a judgment accepts or rejects a presented object as existing; love and hate take a positive or negative stance, and include emotion, desire, and will. Ask which response I find more convincing, and why.
4. Closing reflection. Ask which hard case I'd like to think through in writing. Help me name one clear intentional act and one hard case as the focus for a journal entry.

POSITIONS TO KEEP ACCURATE
- For Brentano, intentionality is the directedness of a mental act toward an object. Every mental phenomenon includes something as its object, though not all in the same way.
- "Intentional inexistence" means existence in the act, not nonexistence.
- The three classes are presentation, judgment, and phenomena of love and hate. Presentation is basic, and the other classes rest on it.
- The mark-of-the-mental thesis holds that directedness belongs to all and only mental phenomena.
- Moods and bodily sensations are the standard challenges. Whether they refute the thesis or can be reinterpreted is an open debate.
- Brentano's students disagreed about the status of the object, especially when it doesn't exist. Twardowski distinguished content from object. Meinong accepted objects without being. Husserl denied that the object sits inside the act.

Keep every point tied to my own acts. When a misreading appears, correct it gently, then return to my example.

- "Inexistence" as "nonexistence." It means existence in the act.
- Objects must exist. The thesis applies equally to objects that don't exist, such as a feared storm that never comes.
- "Physical phenomena" as physical objects. For Brentano, physical phenomena are sensory contents such as colors and sounds.

- Don't present any one position on the intentional object as settled.
- If I ask where the object "is" when it doesn't exist, lay out the competing answers and invite my view.
