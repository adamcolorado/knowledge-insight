Husserl's Method: Against Psychologism

This lesson covers Husserl's argument that logic can't be reduced to the psychology of thinking, and his call to go back "to the things themselves." You'll be able to reconstruct the core of the anti-psychologism argument and explain the principle that what is given in intuition is the source of evidence. The distinction between the content of a thought and the psychological process of thinking it remains basic to any rigorous theory of mind.

  • Explain Husserl's critique of psychologism and his aim of describing experience as it is given

Husserl's Critique of Psychologism and Current Debates About Naturalizing Logic

A familiar move in naturalistic philosophy of mind is to explain a norm by the mechanism that implements it. On this approach, valid reasoning is what well-functioning brains do. Meaning is a pattern of use. Truth is what our cognitive systems are built to track. Each claim tries to ground something normative in facts about how humans happen to work.

Edmund Husserl attacked an earlier version of this move, which he called psychologism (Husserl [1900–1901] 2001, Prolegomena, chs. 3–7). In its classic form, psychologism holds that logical laws are laws of human thinking, so logic is a branch of psychology (Kusch 2024). The view was influential in late 19th-century German philosophy. Husserl had held a version of it himself before he turned against it.

Here is a claim that looks harmless: "2 + 2 \= 4 is true because we're built to think it." It says something plausible about how people form arithmetic beliefs. But it also implies that if we'd been built differently, 2 + 2 might have made 5. Most people balk at that. The balk is the start of Husserl's argument. What you believe when you believe that 2 + 2 \= 4 seems to be one thing. The process by which you came to believe it seems to be another. Only the first is true or false.

Husserl's point isn't that psychology is useless. Psychology can explain why people reason as they do, why they make errors, and how they learn. What it can't do, on his view, is make a valid inference valid.

Debates about naturalizing logic, meaning, and normativity still turn on that distinction (Kusch 2024). Some philosophers hold that normative facts can be reduced to natural ones. Others argue that no description of cognitive processes, however complete, could show why a valid inference is valid. The question is live, and it's not settled.

The skill this raises is one you can use in any field that studies thinking:

  • You can separate what a claim says from the process that produced the belief in it.
  • You can spot arguments that slide from "people reason this way" to "this way of reasoning is correct."
  • You can ask of any theory of mind whether it accounts for the content of thought, or only for the events of thinking.

Psychologism isn't a museum piece. Versions of it appear whenever a theory explains norms by the mechanisms that implement them. Recognizing the pattern lets you ask whether the explanation reaches as far as it claims.

Psychologism and Husserl's Arguments Against It in the Prolegomena

Introduction

The first volume of Edmund Husserl's Logical Investigations (1900) is mostly an attack on a single view. That view, psychologism, was widely held in the German-speaking philosophy of his time. His attack is still the standard reference point for anyone who argues that logic can't be reduced to psychology.

This reading defines psychologism, reconstructs Husserl's two main arguments against it, and explains the distinction his arguments rest on: the ideal content of a judgment versus the real act of judging. Citations to the Logical Investigations name the Prolegomena to Pure Logic and its chapter.

What Psychologism Claims

Psychologism is the view that logical laws, or the contents of thought more generally, are grounded in facts about human psychology (Kusch 2024). On this view, the law of non-contradiction describes how human minds work: we can't believe a proposition and its denial at the same time. Logic becomes a branch of the empirical science of thinking.

The view has an obvious appeal. Logic is about reasoning, reasoning is something minds do, and psychology studies what minds do. Husserl's Prolegomena argues that this chain breaks at the last link (Husserl [1900–1901] 2001, Prolegomena, chs. 3–7).

Argument 1: Exact and A Priori vs. Vague and Empirical

Psychological laws are empirical. They're discovered by observation and induction, and they're vague: they hold roughly, for the most part, and with exceptions. If logical laws were psychological laws, they'd share these features. They'd be approximate, and we'd know them only with probability, pending further observation.

But logical laws aren't like that. They're exact, and we know them a priori, independently of observation. No one confirms the law of non-contradiction by surveying reasoners. So logical laws can't be psychological laws (Husserl [1900–1901] 2001, Prolegomena, ch. 4).

A related point concerns what the laws are about. A psychological law makes claims about mental events. A logical law, such as "if all A are B and all B are C, then all A are C," says nothing about any mind. It would hold even if no one ever thought it.

Argument 2: Psychologism Leads to Relativism

If logical laws were laws of human thinking, their validity would depend on the constitution of the human species. A being built differently could have a different logic, and what's true for us might be false for it. Husserl calls this view specific relativism: the view that truth is relative to the constitution of a species. He calls its human form "anthropologism" (Husserl [1900–1901] 2001, Prolegomena, ch. 7).

Husserl argues that this relativism undermines itself. The claim "truth is relative to the species" is put forward as true, and not merely as true for humans. So the relativist relies on the very notion of truth that the view denies.

The Distinction Behind Both Arguments

Both arguments rest on one distinction. A real act of judging is a mental event: it happens at a particular time, in a particular person, and it has causes and effects. The ideal content of the judgment is what is judged: it stays the same across different acts and different people, and it isn't an event in time.

Real act of judgingIdeal content judged

Psychology studies the left column. Logic studies the right. Psychologism's mistake, on Husserl's account, is to treat the right column as if it were part of the left.

Conclusion

Husserl's case against psychologism has two arguments. Logical laws are exact and known a priori, while psychological laws are vague and empirical. And psychologism collapses into a self-undermining relativism. Both arguments depend on separating the ideal content of a judgment from the real act of judging, a distinction every rigorous theory of mind still needs.

Key Terms

  • Psychologism: The view that logical laws, or the contents of thought more generally, are grounded in facts about human psychology.
  • A priori: Knowable independently of any particular experience.
  • Specific relativism: Husserl's name for the view that truth is relative to the constitution of a species. He calls its human form anthropologism.
  • Real act: A mental event of judging or meaning, which occurs at a particular time in a particular person.
  • Ideal content: What is judged or meant, which stays the same across different acts and people and isn't an event in time.

Husserl's Philosophy of Arithmetic, Frege's Review, and the Return to the Things Themselves

Introduction

Husserl's attack on psychologism has an awkward backstory. The view he demolished in 1900 was close to the one he'd defended in his own first book. How he got from one position to the other, and how much credit Gottlob Frege deserves for the change, is still debated.

This reading covers Husserl's Philosophy of Arithmetic (1891), Frege's 1894 review of it, the dispute over Frege's influence, and the methodological slogan Husserl announced in the second volume of the Logical Investigations.

The Philosophy of Arithmetic

Husserl trained as a mathematician before turning to philosophy. His first book, the Philosophy of Arithmetic (1891), set out to clarify the concept of number by tracing it to its origin in mental acts (Husserl [1891] 2003).

His account ran roughly as follows. When you take several things together as a group, you perform a mental act of collective combination: taking the things together as one group. Reflect on that act, abstract from what the things are, and you arrive at the concept of a multiplicity, and from there at particular numbers. On this account, number concepts get their content from psychological acts of combining.

The approach was typical of the philosophy Husserl had learned from his teacher Franz Brentano (Brentano [1874] 2015). The book grounded a logical and mathematical concept in a description of the mental acts that produce it.

Frege's Review

In 1894, Gottlob Frege published a sharply critical review of the book (Frege [1894] 1972). His central charge was that Husserl confused the subjective with the objective. On Frege's account, Husserl treated ideas, meaning the mental images and acts in a particular mind, as if they were concepts and numbers, which are objective and the same for everyone. For Frege, this was psychologism, the view that logical laws, or the contents of thought more generally, are grounded in facts about human psychology. It made arithmetic depend on the contents of individual minds.

The review has often been read as the event that turned Husserl against psychologism.

The Influence Debate

How far Frege changed Husserl's mind is disputed.

  • The Fregean-influence reading treats the review as decisive. Husserl, stung by Frege's criticism, abandoned psychologism and wrote the Prolegomena partly in response.
  • The independent-development reading argues that Husserl was already moving away from psychologism before 1894. On this reading, he reached the distinction between ideal content and real act largely through his own work. J. N. Mohanty is the best-known defender of this view (Mohanty 1982).

The debate turns on dating Husserl's unpublished and minor writings of the early 1890s. It remains open.

"To the Things Themselves"

The second volume of the Logical Investigations (1901) opens with a statement of method. Husserl says that logical concepts can't rest on mere words, on vague meanings that are only half understood. Their clarification has to go back to the things themselves: auf die "Sachen selbst" zurückgehen (Husserl [1900–1901] 2001, Introduction to vol. 2, §2).

Husserl's slogan, "to the things themselves", makes a specific methodological demand. To clarify a concept, you have to go back from the word to the intuition that fulfills it: an experience in which the thing meant is itself given, as in perceiving an object rather than merely thinking of it. That means bringing before your mind, in an actual experience, the thing the concept is about. You check the concept against what is given, not against a theory about what must lie behind it.

Two features of the demand matter.

  • It isn't a turn to psychology. The "things" aren't mental states. They're whatever the concept is about, as it is given: a number, a relation, a perceived object.
  • It is a turn to description. The demand makes careful description of what is given the basis of philosophical clarification. That is the program phenomenology took up.

Conclusion

Husserl began with a psychological account of number and ended by refuting psychologism. Frege's 1894 review is part of that story, but how large a part is still disputed. The slogan "to the things themselves" names the method that replaced psychologism: clarifying concepts by returning to what is given in intuition.

Key Terms

  • Collective combination: In Husserl's Philosophy of Arithmetic, the mental act of taking several things together as one group, from which he derived the concept of number.
  • Psychologism: The view that logical laws, or the contents of thought more generally, are grounded in facts about human psychology.
  • "To the things themselves": Husserl's methodological demand to clarify concepts by going back from words to the things they're about, as those things are given in intuition.
  • Intuition: In Husserl, an experience in which the thing meant is itself given, as in perceiving an object rather than merely thinking of it.

Empty and Fulfilled Intentions: Husserl on Evidence as Fulfillment

Introduction

Philosophers often treat evidence as a relation between propositions: one claim supports another. Husserl's Logical Investigations locates it somewhere else, in experience. The model case is the difference between thinking of something and having it before you.

This reading explains the distinction between act and ideal content, the difference between empty and fulfilled intentions, and Husserl's account of evidence as the experience of fulfillment. Citations to the Logical Investigations give the Investigation and section ("Inv. VI, §8"), which stay stable across editions.

Act and Content, Briefly

A real act is a mental event of judging or meaning, which occurs at a particular time in a particular person. Its ideal content is what is judged or meant, which stays the same across different acts and people and isn't an event in time. When you and a colleague both think that the meeting starts at noon, there are two acts and one content.

The distinction tells you what is meant. It doesn't yet tell you when a meaning counts as known. For that, Husserl looks at how a meaning relates to what is actually given.

Empty and Fulfilled Intentions

Consider a simple case. You think of a friend's face while they're out of town. Your thought is directed at the face, and it means that face and no other. But the face isn't there. You intend it without seeing it.

Husserl calls this an empty intention: an act that means its object without the object being given in intuition. Most of our thinking is like this. We speak of places we aren't in and people we aren't seeing, and our words intend them emptily.

Now your friend walks in. You see the face you were thinking of. The same object that you meant emptily is now given in person. Husserl calls this fulfillment: an empty intention meets an intuition that gives its object (Husserl [1900–1901] 2001, Inv. VI, §§8–16).

Fulfillment has a distinctive structure. You don't just have two experiences, a thought and a perception. You experience an identity between them: this is the face I was thinking of. The meaning and the given coincide (Zahavi 2025, §2).

Fulfillment can also fail. If the person who walks in isn't your friend, the intention is disappointed. You experience a conflict rather than a coincidence, because what's given isn't what you meant.

Evidence as Fulfillment

This gives Husserl an account of evidence (Evidenz): the experience of fulfillment, in which what was meant is given as it was meant (Zahavi 2025, §2). Evidence, on this view, isn't a feeling of certainty added to a belief. It's the experienced coincidence between an intention and the intuition that fulfills it.

The account has three features worth noting.

  • Evidence comes in degrees. A glimpse of a face across a crowded room fulfills your intention only partly. A long look in good light fulfills it more completely.
  • Evidence applies beyond perception. Grasping that a mathematical proof works is also a fulfillment: what you meant by the theorem is given, in this case in an intellectual intuition rather than a sensory one.
  • Evidence is about the object, not the self. The focus is on what is given and how, not on an inner report of your mental states.

Conclusion

Husserl separates meaning from knowing. An empty intention means its object without having it. Fulfillment brings the object to intuitive givenness and is experienced as a coincidence between what was meant and what is given. Evidence is that experience, and it comes in degrees.

Key Terms

  • Real act: A mental event of judging or meaning, which occurs at a particular time in a particular person.
  • Ideal content: What is judged or meant, which stays the same across different acts and people and isn't an event in time.
  • Empty intention: An act that means its object without the object being given in intuition.
  • Fulfillment: In Husserl, the experienced coincidence of an empty intention with an intuition that gives its object.
  • Evidence (Evidenz): In Husserl, the experience of fulfillment, in which what was meant is given as it was meant.

Husserl's Principle of All Principles and the Limits of Givenness

Introduction

Every method needs a standard for what counts as a legitimate claim. In Ideas I (1913), Husserl states phenomenology's standard in a single sentence. He calls it the "principle of all principles," and critics and defenders have argued over it ever since.

This reading explains what the principle claims, how it grounds phenomenological method, and how it differs from a claim that introspection is infallible. Citations to Ideas I give the section number (§), which stays stable across translations.

What the Principle Says

The principle of all principles, paraphrased, runs as follows. Every intuition that gives its object originally is a legitimate source of knowledge. Whatever presents itself to us in intuition in this original way, in its "bodily" presence as it were, is to be accepted simply as what it presents itself as being. But it is to be accepted only within the limits in which it presents itself (Husserl [1913] 2014, §24).

Three terms carry the sentence:

  • Intuition, in Husserl's sense, is an experience in which the thing meant is itself given, as in perceiving an object rather than merely thinking of it.
  • Originary giving intuition is intuition that gives its object itself, "in person," rather than through a sign, an image, or a memory. Seeing a tree is originary. Recalling it isn't.
  • Givenness is the way something shows up in experience: from a perspective, against a background, and over time.

Why the Principle Grounds the Method

The principle makes what is given in originary intuition the final court of appeal. No theory, however well established, can override what is actually given. And no claim can be accepted if nothing is given to back it.

This is the demand Husserl had announced in the Logical Investigations (1901) with the slogan "to the things themselves" (Husserl [1900–1901] 2001, Introduction to vol. 2, §2), now stated as a principle of evidence. A concept is clarified, and a claim is justified, by returning to the intuition in which its object is given.

The Limit Clause

The principle's last clause matters as much as its first. What is given is to be accepted only within the limits in which it is given.

A perceived house is given from one side. The principle licenses what is given: this side, from here, in this light. It doesn't license claims about the back of the house, which isn't given, however confident you are about it. What is given partially can ground only partial claims.

The limit clause works as a guard against overclaiming. It explains why phenomenological description has to track the manner of givenness, including perspective, partiality, and what's merely co-intended, rather than reporting what the object is "really" like.

How This Differs From Introspection

The principle is sometimes read as a claim that introspection is infallible: whatever I seem to experience, I must be right about. That reading is mistaken on two counts.

  • The principle concerns what is given, not inner reports. It is about objects as they present themselves, such as a house, a number, or a melody, and not about the introspector's verdicts about their own mental states.
  • The limit clause builds fallibility in. Givenness is often partial, and further intuition can correct earlier claims. A glimpse can be revised by a closer look. The principle ties authority to givenness, and givenness comes in degrees.

Conclusion

The principle of all principles makes originary intuition the source of legitimate knowledge, and it restricts that knowledge to the limits of what is given. It grounds phenomenological method in a standard of evidence, not in introspective authority. The limit clause is what keeps the principle from overclaiming.

Key Terms

  • Principle of all principles: Husserl's principle that every originary giving intuition is a legitimate source of knowledge, and that what it gives is to be accepted as it is given, but only within the limits in which it is given.
  • Intuition: In Husserl, an experience in which the thing meant is itself given, as in perceiving an object rather than merely thinking of it.
  • Originary giving intuition: Intuition that gives its object itself, in person, rather than through a sign, an image, or a memory.
  • Givenness: The way something shows up in experience: from a perspective, against a background, and over time.

Guided Close Reading: The Principle of All Principles in Ideas I §24

Introduction

Husserl's principle of all principles is one long sentence in Ideas I. It is quoted often and read carefully less often. This reading walks through the sentence clause by clause. It shows how Husserl makes originary intuition the standard of evidence, and how the same sentence limits what that standard can license. It ends with a model reconstruction you can check your reading against.

Locating the Passage

The principle is in §24 of Ideas I (1913), in the chapter that defends phenomenology against the charge of naturalism (Husserl [1913] 2014, §24). Section numbers are the same in every translation, so you can follow along in any edition.

This reading paraphrases the principle and gives its key terms in Husserl's German, because the two English translations render them differently. The recommended translation is Daniel Dahlstrom's (Hackett, 2014). F. Kersten's earlier translation (Springer) is widely cited, and its wording differs at every key phrase (Husserl [1913] 1982). Whichever translation you use, match the German terms below to its wording. No free English translation is available. The free Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Husserl summarizes the method the principle grounds (Zahavi 2025, §3).

Before you start, note the setting. Husserl introduces the principle while answering critics who say that only natural science yields knowledge. He sets it against any theory that would override what is actually given.

Walking Through the Principle

Step 1: Originary giving intuition

The principle begins with its subject: every originary giving intuition (originär gebende Anschauung).

Intuition (Anschauung) here doesn't mean a hunch. It means an experience in which the thing meant is itself given, as opposed to being merely thought of or referred to.

Originary (originär) narrows the class further. An originary intuition gives its object itself, in person, not through a substitute. Perceiving the tree outside your window is originary. Remembering the tree, or looking at a photograph of it, gives the tree too, but only by way of a re-presentation.

Giving (gebend) says what the intuition does: it presents, or gives, its object. The phrase as a whole picks out acts in which the object is present in the fullest sense available.

Step 2: A legitimizing source of knowledge

The principle's main claim is that every such intuition is a legitimate source, or legitimizing source, of knowledge: eine Rechtsquelle der Erkenntnis.

Rechtsquelle is a legal term. It means "source of right," the kind of thing that confers legitimacy, as a statute or a constitution does. Husserl isn't claiming only that intuition causes beliefs. He's claiming that it grants them their right to be held.

The sentence goes on to spell out what follows. Whatever presents itself to us originally in intuition, in its bodily actuality, as it were (in seiner leibhaften Wirklichkeit), is to be accepted simply as what it presents itself as being. "Bodily" here is a metaphor for full presence: the object is there in person, not in effigy.

Step 3: Only within the limits in which it is given

The sentence then adds its qualification: what is given is to be accepted, but only within the limits in which it is given there (in den Schranken, in denen es sich da gibt).

This clause restricts the license the first part grants. Suppose you see a house from the street. The front is given originally. The back is intended as part of the same house, but it isn't given. The principle licenses claims about what you see from here. It doesn't license claims about the back, the plumbing, or what the house is "really" made of.

The clause also explains why the principle doesn't end inquiry. Givenness can be enlarged: walk around the house, and more is given. A claim made within the limits of a glimpse can be corrected by a fuller look.

Key Considerations

Terms and translations. Because Dahlstrom's and Kersten's renderings differ, three terms are worth tracking in whichever translation you use:

GermanWhat it meansWhat to look for in your translation
originär gebende AnschauungIntuition that gives its object in person"originary," "originarily presentive," or "originally giving" intuition
Rechtsquelle der ErkenntnisA source that confers legitimacy on knowledge"legitimizing source," "source of legitimacy," or "legitimate source" of cognition or knowledge
in den Schranken, in denen es sich da gibtWithin the limits of its givenness"only within the limits in which it is presented" or "gives itself"

How the qualification guards against overclaiming. Without the limit clause, the principle would license every confident perceptual belief. With it, the principle licenses only what is actually given, as it is given. Partial givenness grants partial authority. This is why phenomenological description tracks perspective and incompleteness rather than reporting objects wholesale.

A common misreading. The principle is easy to read as a claim that introspection is infallible, as if whatever I seem to experience, I must be right about. That misreads it twice.

  • First, the principle is about objects given in intuition, such as the house, the tree, or the number. It isn't about the introspector's verdicts on their own mental states.
  • Second, the limit clause makes the principle's authority depend on the extent of givenness, which is often partial and can be corrected by further intuition.

A principle of evidence that builds in its own limits isn't a doctrine of infallibility.

Summary

The principle names its subject (originary giving intuition), grants it authority (a legitimizing source of knowledge), and restricts that authority (only within the limits of givenness). Here is a model reconstruction to check your reading against:

Every intuition that gives its object in person is a legitimate source of knowledge. Whatever is given in such an intuition is to be accepted as what it is given as, and only within the limits in which it is given. So what is given confers the right to hold a claim about it, but only as far as the givenness extends, and fuller givenness can correct a claim made on partial givenness (Husserl [1913] 2014, §24).

- Ask one question at a time, then wait for my reply. Keep each of your turns under about 120 words.

TOPICS, IN ORDER
1. The claim and the process. Ask what the claim itself is, and what the mental process was by which I came to believe it. Help me state the claim precisely, then describe the process: reading, calculating, being told, or seeing. Keep the two separate.

POSITIONS TO KEEP ACCURATE
- Psychologism is the view that logical laws, or the contents of thought, are grounded in facts about human psychology.
- Husserl argues that logical laws are exact and known a priori, while psychological laws are vague and empirical. He also argues that psychologism leads to a self-undermining relativism.
- The real act of judging is a mental event. The ideal content is what is judged, the same across acts and people.
- An empty intention means its object without having it given. Fulfillment is the experienced coincidence of that intention with an intuition that gives the object.
- Evidence, for Husserl, is the experience of fulfillment, and it comes in degrees.
- The principle of all principles makes originary intuition a legitimate source of knowledge, but only within the limits of what is given.

MISREADINGS TO CORRECT GENTLY
Keep every point tied to my own claim and example.
- Psychologism as any empirical psychology. Psychologism is a thesis about grounding logic in psychology, not the study of psychology as such. Husserl didn't object to psychology.
- Content as private. If I treat "my belief" as something only in my head, point out that someone else can believe the very same thing.
- Fulfillment as a feeling of certainty. Fulfillment is the coincidence between what was meant and what is given, not a confident feeling.
- The principle as introspective infallibility. The principle concerns what is given, and only within its limits.
- Anti-psychologism as anti-science. Husserl's point is about what grounds validity, not a rejection of empirical study of the mind.
