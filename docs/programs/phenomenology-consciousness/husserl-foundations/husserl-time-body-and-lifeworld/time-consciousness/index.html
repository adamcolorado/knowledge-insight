Time, Body, Lifeworld: Time-Consciousness

This lesson presents Husserl's account of how we experience time: every present moment holds the just-past (retention) and anticipates the about-to-come (protention). You'll be able to use the melody example to explain retention, protention, and primal impression, and tell retention apart from recollection. Time-consciousness is one of the most studied phenomenological structures in current cognitive science, so this vocabulary is essential for reading work on the temporality of experience.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain Husserl's analysis of time-consciousness in terms of primal impression, retention, and protention

The Melody Puzzle and Husserl's Analysis of Time-Consciousness

When you hear a melody, you hear a melody. You don't hear a string of separate tones that you later assemble. Yet at any instant, only one tone is sounding. The same holds for a spoken sentence, heard one word at a time, and for a ball crossing a room, seen at one position at a time. Somehow the experience of the present contains more than the present instant.

The obvious answer is memory. You remember the earlier notes, and the memory combines with the current note to give you the phrase. But this answer seems wrong on reflection. You don't remember the note you heard half a second ago in the way you remember what you ate for breakfast. Remembering breakfast is a new act: you call up something that had dropped out of experience. The just-heard note hasn't dropped out. It's still part of what you're hearing, sounding as just past, and it shapes how the current note sounds.

The puzzle has practical consequences. Research on speech perception, motion perception, and music cognition studies experiences that exist only across time. How researchers describe those experiences shapes what they look for in the data, and a description that starts from frozen instants will look for the wrong thing.

Psychology gave this problem a name more than a century ago. William James called the short stretch of time we experience as present the "specious present." In his phrase, it's not a knife-edge but a stretch with some breadth to it (James 1890, ch. 15). Philosophers still debate how to model it. Some hold that each experience contains a short span of content. Others hold that experiences themselves are extended in time. Still others propose different structures again (Dainton 2023).

Edmund Husserl offered one of the most detailed answers (Husserl [1928] 1991). His analysis describes the living present as having three parts:

  • a phase of what's happening now
  • a retaining of what just happened
  • an anticipating of what's about to happen

Researchers working on time perception, neurophenomenology, and the structure of consciousness still use and argue with this analysis.

For anyone studying consciousness, the question isn't optional. Every experience takes time: a perception, a thought, a feeling, a sentence understood. An account of consciousness that treats experience as a series of frozen instants leaves out something basic. Knowing Husserl's analysis gives you a precise vocabulary for describing that temporal structure, and a clear view of what current models of temporal experience are trying to explain.

Primal Impression, Retention, and Protention in Husserl's Time Lectures

Introduction

If the present were an instant, you could never hear a melody, only one tone at a time. Husserl's lectures on the consciousness of internal time answer this puzzle by describing the present as a structure with three parts.

This reading explains primal impression, retention, and protention using Husserl's melody example. It then walks through his diagram of time-consciousness in words and a table. Citations to the lectures, first given in 1905 and published in 1928, use section numbers (§), which stay stable across editions.

The Melody Example

Husserl builds his analysis around hearing a melody or a single enduring tone (Husserl [1928] 1991, §§7–11). As you listen, one tone sounds now. The tone just before it hasn't vanished from your experience. It's still there, as just past. And you hear the current tone as leading somewhere, toward what comes next.

Each moment of hearing therefore has three parts.

  • Primal impression, the now-phase: the tone as sounding now (§11).
  • Retention, the just-past held in the present: the tone that just sounded, still given, but as just past. As the melody goes on, the retained tone sinks further back, given as "further past," without dropping out at once (§11).
  • Protention, the open anticipation of what's about to come: the next tone, expected but not yet given (Zahavi 2025, §4).

These aren't three separate acts that happen one after another. They're three phases of a single experience, which Husserl's successors often call the living present: the present as a flowing structure, not an instant.

The Comet's Tail

Husserl describes the retained phases as trailing behind each now like a comet's tail. As each new tone arrives in primal impression, the previous one slides into retention. The one before that slides further back, retained as further past. The present experience carries a continuously fading tail of the just-past, and that tail is what lets you hear a phrase rather than a single note.

Husserl's Diagram, in Words

Husserl represents this structure with a diagram (§10). It can be described in three lines.

  1. A horizontal line runs from left to right. It represents the series of now-points, the moments at which each tone is given in primal impression.
  2. Slanting lines run downward from each now-point. They represent how each now-phase sinks back into retention as time goes on. The further down the line, the further past.
  3. A vertical line drops from the current now-point. It represents everything given together at that moment: the tone sounding now, plus the whole continuum of retained earlier tones, each at its own depth of pastness.

The same structure can be shown in a table. Here, a listener hears three notes, C, D, and E, in order:

MomentPrimal impression (now)Retention (just past)Protention (about to come)
C soundsCNothing yetA next note, not yet specified
D soundsDC, as just pastA next note, shaped by C–D
E soundsED, as just past; C, as further pastA next note, or the phrase's end

The last row corresponds to the vertical line: at the moment E sounds, all three notes are given together, each in its own temporal mode.

Conclusion

For Husserl, the present isn't an instant. It has three parts: primal impression of the now, retention of the just-past, and protention of the about-to-come. Retained phases trail behind each now like a comet's tail. That structure, described in his diagram, is what makes it possible to hear a melody as a whole.

Key Terms

  • Primal impression: In Husserl, the now-phase of experience: what is given as happening now.
  • Retention: In Husserl, the just-past held in the present experience and given as just past. It's part of the current experience, not a separate act of memory.
  • Protention: In Husserl, the open anticipation, within the present experience, of what is about to come.
  • Living present: The present as a flowing three-part structure of primal impression, retention, and protention, not an instant.

Retention vs. Recollection: Primary and Secondary Memory in Husserl

Introduction

"Retention" is often glossed as short-term memory, and the gloss is misleading. Husserl's time lectures distinguish sharply between two ways the past is given, and much of his analysis depends on keeping them apart.

This reading explains the difference between retention and recollection, which Husserl also calls primary and secondary memory, and why the difference matters. Citations to the lectures use section numbers (§).

Two Ways the Past Is Given

When you hear a melody, the tone that just sounded is given in retention: the just-past held in the present experience and given as just past. Husserl sometimes calls this primary memory (Husserl [1928] 1991, §§12–14).

Later, after the concert, you might call the melody to mind again. You run through it in imagination, note by note, as it sounded then. Husserl calls this recollection, or secondary memory: a new act that re-presents a past experience (§§14, 19).

The two differ in structure, not just in timing.

Retention (primary memory)Recollection (secondary memory)
Kind of actPart of the present perceptionA new act, separate from any present perception
How the past is givenPerceived as just past, still givenRe-presented, as if lived through again
ContinuityContinuous with the now: sinks back graduallyDiscontinuous: the past is called back after a gap
Voluntary?No: it happens with every perceptionOften yes: you can choose to recall
ExampleThe note that just sounded, still heardThe melody recalled the next day

Why Retention Isn't Short Memory

Three features show that retention isn't a very short memory.

  • It belongs to perception. Retention is part of perceiving the melody. Without it, you'd perceive a series of instantaneous tones, never a phrase. A memory is a different act, directed at something no longer perceived.
  • It isn't a re-presentation. In recollection, you live through the past again, as if it were present, with the awareness that it isn't. In retention, nothing is lived through again. The just-past is simply still there, given as past.
  • It's continuous. Recollection calls back a past event after a gap. Retention has no gap. Each now-phase slides smoothly into retention as the next arrives.

Husserl draws a further contrast. In retention, the past is perceived: the just-past is given as itself, in its pastness. In recollection, the past is imaged or reproduced. The difference is between a presentation and a re-presentation (§19).

Why It Matters

The distinction matters for any account of temporal experience. A theory that explains hearing a melody by storing each note in memory and replaying it has confused retention with recollection. It would need a separate act of memory at every instant, and it couldn't explain why the retained note is heard as part of the present phrase rather than recalled as a past event.

Conclusion

Retention and recollection are two different ways the past is given. Retention, or primary memory, is part of the present perception, continuous with the now, and gives the just-past as itself. Recollection, or secondary memory, is a new act that re-presents a past experience after a gap. Treating retention as a short memory erases the difference, and with it Husserl's answer to the melody puzzle.

Key Terms

  • Retention: In Husserl, the just-past held in the present experience and given as just past. It's part of the current experience, not a separate act of memory.
  • Primary memory: Husserl's alternative name for retention.
  • Recollection: In Husserl, a new act that re-presents a past experience, as if lived through again. It's also called secondary memory.
  • Secondary memory: Husserl's alternative name for recollection.

Protention, Surprise, and the Regress Problem in Husserl's Account of Time

Introduction

Protention is the least discussed of Husserl's three phases of the present, partly because it's hard to notice. It usually shows itself only when it fails. This reading explains how protention reveals itself in surprise. It then takes up a deeper problem Husserl faced: if experience is temporal, what is aware of its flow? Citations to Husserl's time lectures use section numbers (§).

Protention and Surprise

Protention is the open anticipation, within the present experience, of what is about to come. It's not a prediction you make. It's built into perceiving. As you hear a melody, the next note is anticipated in a loose, open way, shaped by what came before.

The clearest evidence for protention is surprise. Suppose you're walking down a flight of stairs in the dark and there's one step fewer than you expected. Your foot comes down on nothing. The jolt is immediate, and it's not a conclusion drawn from evidence. It shows that your experience already contained an anticipation of the next step, which the world failed to fulfill.

The same is true when a melody resolves unexpectedly, or when a sentence ends with a word you didn't expect. You didn't consciously predict the right note or word. But the surprise shows that the experience was already leaning toward something, and that something didn't come.

Protention also explains why experience feels continuous forward, not just backward. Each now is heard as leading on, not as a stopping point.

The Regress Problem

Husserl's analysis raises a hard question. The phases of the present, primal impression, retention, and protention, are themselves experienced. They flow: each now slides into retention as the next arrives. So what is aware of this flow?

If the answer is "another consciousness, which experiences the flow in time," the problem repeats. That consciousness is temporal too, and something must be aware of its flow in turn. The result is an infinite regress, with a new consciousness needed at every level (Dainton 2023).

Husserl's Response, in Overview

Husserl's answer, developed in the later sections of the lectures, is that the regress stops at an absolute time-constituting flow (Husserl [1928] 1991, §§34–39). This is the deepest level of consciousness, the flow in which temporal objects and experiences are constituted. Husserl holds that it doesn't need another consciousness to be aware of it, because it is self-manifesting: the flow appears to itself as it flows, without a further act of awareness.

Husserl also denies that this flow is itself in time in the way its contents are. It is what makes temporal order possible, so it can't simply be one more item in that order. That's why he called it absolute.

This response is among the most difficult parts of Husserl's philosophy, and its interpretation remains disputed (Dainton 2023). Its overview-level point is that the structure of time-consciousness, on Husserl's view, bottoms out in a consciousness that is aware of itself without a further act.

Conclusion

Protention is the anticipation built into perception, and it's most visible when it fails, as in the missing step. Husserl's analysis of the flowing present raises a regress: what is aware of the flow itself? He answers with a self-manifesting, absolute time-constituting flow. The answer is hard and still debated.

Key Terms

  • Protention: In Husserl, the open anticipation, within the present experience, of what is about to come.
  • Regress: In this context, the problem that if time-consciousness must itself be experienced in time, a further consciousness is needed at every level, without end.
  • Absolute time-constituting flow: In Husserl, the deepest level of consciousness, in which temporal experience is constituted, and which is self-manifesting rather than known by a further act.
  • Self-manifesting: In Husserl, appearing to itself without a further act of awareness, as the absolute time-constituting flow does.

Husserl, James's Specious Present, and Time-Consciousness in Current Research

Introduction

Husserl wasn't the only one to notice that the experienced present has breadth. William James, working in psychology at the same time, reached a related idea from a different direction. Current research on temporal experience draws on both.

This reading gathers the core concepts of time-consciousness in one table, compares Husserl's analysis with James's "specious present," and points to two current lines of work that build on Husserl.

The Core Concepts at a Glance

TermWhat it isExample
Primal impressionThe now-phase of experience: what is given as happening nowThe note sounding now
RetentionThe just-past held in the present experience and given as just pastThe note that just sounded, still heard
ProtentionThe open anticipation, within the present experience, of what is about to comeThe next note, expected before it sounds
RecollectionA new act that re-presents a past experience, as if lived through againRecalling the melody the next day

The first three are phases of one present experience. The fourth is a separate act.

James's Specious Present

In The Principles of Psychology (1890), William James argued that the present we actually experience isn't a durationless instant. It's a short stretch of time, which he called the specious present. Within that stretch, we directly experience succession and change, such as a tone rising or an object moving (James 1890, ch. 15).

James and Husserl agree that the experienced present has breadth. They differ in how they describe it.

  • James treats the specious present as a span of time, with a rough duration, within which a sequence is given at once. His approach is psychological, and he treats the span as something that could in principle be measured.
  • Husserl describes a structure rather than a span. The present consists of a now-phase, a continuum of retentions, and protention, each with its own temporal mode (Husserl [1928] 1991). His approach is phenomenological, and it asks how each phase is given.

Philosophers still debate which kind of account is better. Some models hold that each experience contains a short span of content. Others hold that experiences are themselves extended over time (Dainton 2023). Husserl's retention-based analysis is one of the main reference points in this debate.

Current Uptake

Two lines of current work build on Husserl's analysis.

  • Neurophenomenology. Francisco Varela proposed pairing Husserl's structure of retention and protention with the dynamics of large-scale brain activity. He linked the experienced present to timescales at which neural activity is integrated into coherent patterns (Varela 1999).
  • Models of temporal experience. Philosophers comparing models of temporal consciousness treat Husserl's analysis as a leading example of a retentional model. They test it against rival models and against experimental findings on how succession and change are perceived (Dainton 2023).

In both, Husserl's vocabulary supplies the description of the experience that a model or a neural account is meant to explain.

Conclusion

Primal impression, retention, and protention are phases of one present experience, and recollection is a separate act. James's specious present and Husserl's living present agree that the experienced present has breadth. They differ over whether it's a span or a structure. Current work in neurophenomenology and in models of temporal experience still uses Husserl's analysis as its point of departure.

Key Terms

  • Primal impression: In Husserl, the now-phase of experience: what is given as happening now.
  • Retention: In Husserl, the just-past held in the present experience and given as just past. It's part of the current experience, not a separate act of memory.
  • Protention: In Husserl, the open anticipation, within the present experience, of what is about to come.
  • Recollection: In Husserl, a new act that re-presents a past experience, as if lived through again. It's also called secondary memory.
  • Specious present: William James's term for the short stretch of time experienced as present, within which succession and change are directly experienced.

Guided Close Reading: Husserl's Melody Analysis in the Time Lectures, §§7–11

Introduction

Husserl's analysis of hearing a melody runs over a few short sections of his time lectures. It's compact, dense, and easy to misread. This reading walks through its argument move by move, from hearing a single tone to distinguishing retention from recollection. It ends with a model paraphrase and a small table you can check your reading against.

Locating the Passage

The analysis is in §§7–11 of On the Phenomenology of the Consciousness of Internal Time (Husserl [1928] 1991, §§7–11). Section numbers are the same in every edition. The recommended English translation is John Barnett Brough's. An older translation by James S. Churchill, published as The Phenomenology of Internal Time-Consciousness (Husserl [1928] 1964), is still widely cited. Its wording differs at several key terms, so the key phrases below are given in Husserl's German.

No free English translation is available. The free Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on temporal consciousness summarizes Husserl's analysis, including the melody example (Dainton 2023, §4.2).

Before you start, note the setting. In the preceding sections, Husserl has set aside the question of objective time, meaning clock time and the time of physics. He asks only how temporal objects, such as a tone or a melody, are given in experience.

Walking Through the Argument

Step 1: Hearing a tone

Husserl starts with the simplest temporal object: a single tone that begins, lasts, and stops. The tone is a temporal object in a precise sense. It isn't just in time. It has duration as part of what it is.

As the tone begins, it's given as now. Husserl calls this phase the primal impression (Urimpression): the tone as sounding now, given with a freshness nothing else has. But the tone doesn't stay in that phase. As it continues, each new phase of it arrives as now.

Step 2: The tone's sinking back

What happens to the phase that was just now? It doesn't vanish. Husserl says it sinks back: it's still given, but modified, as just past, or soeben gewesen ("just having been"). As further phases arrive, it sinks further back and is given as further past.

Husserl's name for these continuous modifications is running-off modes (Ablaufsmodi). Each phase of the tone runs off from now, through just-past, into further past. The whole continuum is given at once, in a single present experience.

Step 3: The retained tone as still "there"

This is the crux. The just-past phase is given in retention, and Husserl insists that it's still genuinely given. It isn't pictured, imagined, or inferred. You're still hearing it, as just past.

This is why he uses the image of a comet's tail. Each now drags behind it a tail of retained phases, fading as they recede, and that tail is part of what you hear now. Without it, you'd hear only a knife-edge now, and never a tone lasting or a melody unfolding. The same account extends from one tone to a melody: each new note sounds against the retained notes before it.

Step 4: The contrast with recalling a melody later

Now compare a different experience. The next day, you call the melody back to mind. This is recollection, and Husserl argues that it differs in kind from retention.

In recollection, the melody is re-presented: it's run through again, as if heard again, but with the awareness that it isn't heard now. Retention re-presents nothing. The just-past is simply still there. Recollection is a new act. Retention is part of the present perception. Husserl calls retention primary memory and recollection secondary memory, and he warns against running them together.

Key Considerations

Terms and translations. Brough and Churchill render several key terms differently, so it's useful to track the German.

GermanMeaningWhat to look for in your translation

RetentionThe just-past held in the present"retention," or "primary memory"
soeben gewesenJust having been"just past," "just-having-been"
AblaufsmodiThe modes in which each phase runs off into the past"running-off modes," "modes of running-off," "running-off phenomena"
WiedererinnerungRecollection, a re-presenting act"recollection," "secondary memory"

A common misreading. Retention is easy to read as a very short memory, as if you stored the last half-second of sound and replayed it. That reading gets Husserl backward in two ways.

  • First, memory is a separate act, and retention isn't. Retention is a phase of the present perception.
  • Second, memory re-presents, and retention doesn't. In retention, the just-past is given as itself, in its pastness.

A theory that treats retention as short-term memory needs a new act of remembering at every instant, and it can't explain why the retained note is heard as part of the present phrase.

Summary

The passage moves from a single tone's primal impression, through its sinking back in retention, to the claim that the retained tone is still given. It then contrasts that with recalling a melody later. Here is a model paraphrase to check your reading against:

Hearing a melody, I hear each tone as now in a primal impression. As each tone passes, it isn't lost but retained, still given as just past and then as further past, trailing behind the now like a comet's tail. That retained continuum is part of my present hearing, which is why I hear a phrase and not an isolated tone. Recalling the melody later is different: it's a new act that re-presents the melody as past, whereas retention is part of perceiving (Husserl [1928] 1991, §§7–11).

Hands-on Activity: Listen to Time (about 12 minutes)

Note to the learner: press send to start. Everything below is facilitator guidance for Claude. It lists misreadings to watch for, so skip it if you'd rather come to the conversation fresh.

Claude, please facilitate a reflective dialogue with me. I'm a graduate-level learner studying Husserl's analysis of time-consciousness. Follow this guidance for the whole conversation.

GOAL
Help me explain Husserl's analysis of time-consciousness by describing my own experience of a short melody or phrase in terms of primal impression, retention, and protention, and by distinguishing retention from recollection.

HOW TO RUN THE CONVERSATION
- Ask one question at a time, then wait for my reply. Keep each of your turns under about 120 words.
- Don't lecture. Explain a point only when I need it to continue, then return to what I'm hearing.
- Be patient, curious, and collegial. Encourage me to listen again rather than theorize. Define terms of art briefly on first use.
- Plain conversation only: no web search, files, or artifacts.
- Aim for about 12 minutes. If I can't play music, a spoken phrase or a clock's ticking works. If I struggle with retention, have me say a sentence slowly and notice the words just said. If I seem uncertain, shorten the conversation to 5-7 minutes. Always reach topic 4.
- Start now. Open with one or two warm sentences: this is an exploratory conversation, not a quiz; time is hard to describe, so tentative answers are welcome; I can ask you to clarify any term. Then ask me to play or hum a short melody I know, or say a familiar phrase aloud.

TOPICS, IN ORDER
1. The just-past note. Ask me to listen to a short melody or phrase I know, and to say what's happening, as one note sounds, to the note before it in my experience. Help me notice three things about the previous note: it hasn't vanished; it's still present, but as just past; it shapes how the current note sounds. Introduce primal impression and retention as the names for the now-phase and the just-past held in the present.
2. Anticipation. Ask what I was expecting just before the next note, and how I'd describe that anticipation. Help me see that the anticipation was open rather than specific, and that it was built into hearing rather than being a conscious prediction. Introduce protention. If the melody is familiar, ask how the anticipation differed from hearing an unfamiliar one.
3. Retention vs. recollection. Ask me to compare hearing the just-past note with recalling the whole piece later, and to say how they differ. Guide me toward the differences: retention is part of the present hearing, continuous, and gives the note as itself; recollection is a new act that re-presents the melody after a gap.
4. Closing reflection. Ask where else in my day I noticed protention, such as a surprise or a moment of expectation, and have me note it for a journal entry. Help me name one concrete moment. The missing-step case is a good prompt.

POSITIONS TO KEEP ACCURATE
- For Husserl, the present isn't an instant. It has three phases: primal impression, retention, and protention.
- Retention holds the just-past in the present experience, given as just past. It's part of perception, not a separate memory.
- Protention is an open anticipation built into experience, most visible in surprise.
- Recollection, or secondary memory, is a new act that re-presents the past. Retention, or primary memory, isn't.
- Retained phases sink back like a comet's tail, and together they make it possible to hear a melody as a whole.
- Husserl's analysis raises a regress about what is aware of the flow. He answers it with a self-manifesting, absolute flow; the answer is still debated.

MISREADINGS TO CORRECT GENTLY
Keep every point tied to what I'm hearing.
- Retention as short-term memory. Retention is part of the present perception, not a separate act of memory.
- Protention as conscious prediction. Protention is built into perception and usually unnoticed until it fails.
- Three separate acts. Primal impression, retention, and protention are phases of one experience, not a sequence of acts.
- The present as an instant with memory added. On Husserl's view the present itself has breadth.
- James and Husserl as saying the same thing. If James comes up, note that they agree the present has breadth but describe it differently: a span for James, a structure for Husserl.

TO FINISH
After I name my moment, close in one short turn:
- Affirm one precise observation I made, in my own words where possible.
- Suggest one or two next steps that fit how the conversation went. Possible steps: write the journal entry, applying all three phases and the contrast between retention and recollection to one experience; review the definitions of primal impression, retention, and protention; review the distinction between retention and recollection, focusing on "part of perception" versus "new act"; go back over how a melody is heard note by note and paraphrase it; repeat this conversation, listening to a very short phrase several times; explore how neurophenomenology pairs Husserl's analysis with neural dynamics; consider how the regress problem challenges the analysis.
- Restate my moment on its own line, labeled "Journal note," so I can copy it as the focus for a journal entry.

Journal Entry on Time-Consciousness

Overview

If you don't keep a phenomenology journal yet, start one now; any notebook or document works. This activity is optional, and your entry is for your own development.

You'll write a 300–500 word entry. The entry applies Husserl's analysis of time-consciousness to a short experience of your own.

Writing Prompt

In 300–500 words, describe the temporal structure of one short experience using primal impression, retention, and protention, contrast retention with a later recollection of the same event, and describe one moment when your anticipation was confirmed or broken.

Steps

  1. Choose a short experience that unfolds in time. A melody, a snatch of conversation, or catching a ball would work. Pick something that lasted a few seconds.
  2. Describe its three phases. Describe the now-phase, what was retained as just past, and what was anticipated.
  3. Contrast retention with a later recollection of the same event. Recall the event now, and describe how recalling it differs from how the just-past was given at the time.
  4. Note a confirmed or broken anticipation. Describe one moment where your anticipation was confirmed or broken.

Self-Check

Before you finish, check that your entry:

  • Describes one specific, temporally extended experience
  • Applies primal impression, retention, and protention to it
  • Explains that retention is part of the present experience, while recollection is a new act
  • Describes one confirmed or disappointed anticipation
