Being-in-the-World: The Question of Being

This lesson explains why Heidegger made the question of being, rather than consciousness, the starting point for phenomenology, and why he called the human being "Dasein." You'll be able to explain his definition of phenomenology in Being and Time §7 and his objection that Husserl's transcendental subject overlooks our prior involvement in the world. This shift from mind to world-involved existence underlies today's embodied and situated approaches to cognition.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain Heidegger's reorientation of phenomenology from consciousness to the question of being

Heidegger's Question of Being and the Default Ontology of Mind Research

Every research program on the mind assumes something about what kind of thing a mind is, and most of those assumptions go unstated. Martin Heidegger's Being and Time (1927) starts from a puzzle that brings them into view.

The puzzle is easy to state. You constantly understand what it is for things to be. You deal with a hammer as something to use, a number as something to calculate with, and a person as someone to talk to. You don't confuse these ways of being with one another. Yet if someone asks what "being" means, you can't say. Heidegger argues that this isn't a curiosity. We always already move within some understanding of being, while the meaning of being stays obscure (Heidegger [1927] 2008, §1, H. 2–4). The philosophical tradition, he claims, treated the word as too general, too obvious, or too basic to define, and stopped asking.

Heidegger's shift is from the question of what a mind knows to the question of how the one who knows exists. That shift has research stakes. Many theories treat a human being as one more object in the world, marked out by its inner states: beliefs, representations, experiences. The framing is so familiar that it doesn't look like a framing. But it's already an answer to the question of being. It says that to be a person is to be a thing with certain internal properties. Heidegger's objection is that this answer misses how we actually exist: as already involved with tools, tasks, and other people, in a world that matters to us before we reflect on it.

Current research has taken up versions of that objection. Situated and embodied approaches to cognition start from agents engaged with an environment, not from an inner processor representing an outer world (Spaulding 2021). Not all of these approaches draw on Heidegger, and some reject much of his framework. What they share is the suspicion that the picture of the mind as an inner container is an assumption, not a result.

The default ontology behind a research program shapes what counts as data, what counts as an explanation, and what drops out of view. If you can ask what a theory takes it to mean for a mind to be, you can see which options it closed off before any experiment was run. Heidegger's question, and the vocabulary he built to pursue it, gives that challenge a precise form.

The Question of Being and the Ontological Difference in Being and Time

Introduction

Heidegger opens Being and Time with the charge that philosophy has forgotten its most basic question. The charge sounds grand, but it rests on a precise distinction.

This reading explains the question of being, the distinction between being and beings, and why Heidegger thinks the inquiry must begin with the kind of being we ourselves are. Citations to Being and Time give the section (§) and the German page (H.), which both English translations print in the margin.

A Question That Seems Empty

Heidegger's first move is to explain why the question looks unnecessary (Heidegger [1927] 2008, §1, H. 2–4). The tradition, he says, rests on three assumptions about being:

  1. It's the most universal concept, so there's nothing more to say about it.
  2. It's indefinable, because it can't be derived from anything higher.
  3. It's self-evident: everyone understands "the sky is blue" or "I am happy."

Heidegger turns each assumption into evidence for his question. Universality doesn't make a concept clear. Indefinability shows only that being isn't the kind of thing that can be defined like an object. And self-evidence shows that we already understand being in some way, while being unable to say what we understand. That combination is the puzzle.

Being and Beings

The question depends on a distinction. Beings are anything that is: stones, hammers, numbers, people, events. Heidegger's German is Seiendes, which Macquarrie and Robinson translate as "entities." Being (Sein) is what determines beings as beings, the basis on which they're already understood (§2, H. 6). Being isn't itself a being. You can't find it by listing more things, and you can't explain it by tracing beings back to some first being, as a creation story does.

Heidegger soon gave this distinction a name, the ontological difference: the difference between being and beings (Heidegger [1975] 1982). Keeping it in view is the whole discipline of his inquiry. Whenever you answer "what does it mean to be?" by pointing to a particular kind of thing, such as matter, mind, or God, you have answered a question about beings, not about being.

The distinction also sorts kinds of inquiry (§3, H. 11). An ontic inquiry is about beings and their properties. An ontological inquiry is about the being of beings: what it is for something to be at all, or to be a certain kind of thing.

Ontic inquiryOntological inquiry
Asks aboutParticular beings and their propertiesThe being of beings
Example questionWhat is the melting point of iron?What is it to be a material thing?
Typical practitionerThe special sciencesPhilosophy

Heidegger argues that every science rests on ontological assumptions about its domain. Physics assumes something about what it is to be a material thing, and biology about what it is to be alive. He reads a science's foundational crises as moments when those assumptions come into question (§3, H. 9–10).

Why Start with Us

If the meaning of being is obscure, where should an inquiry begin? Heidegger's answer is that we already have a clue. We live in a pre-ontological understanding of being: an everyday understanding of what it is for things to be, which we have without any explicit concept of it. Heidegger calls it a "vague average understanding" of being (§2, H. 5).

So the inquiry has to start with the being who has that understanding and who can ask the question: the kind of being we ourselves are. Heidegger marks this being out by one feature: in its very being, its being is an issue for it (§4, H. 12). A stone doesn't relate to its own being. We do, whether or not we ever reflect on it. Heidegger's name for this being is "Dasein."

Heidegger calls the analysis of Dasein fundamental ontology: the groundwork on which the question of the meaning of being in general can be raised (§4, H. 13). This is the decisive shift. Where Husserl began with consciousness and its acts (Husserl [1913] 2014), Heidegger begins with a being defined by its relation to its own being.

Conclusion

Heidegger's question of being rests on the ontological difference between being and beings. It sorts ontic inquiries from ontological ones, and it treats every science as resting on ontological assumptions. Because we already understand being pre-ontologically, the inquiry starts with the being that has that understanding: Dasein.

Key Terms

  • Beings: Anything that is: things, tools, numbers, people, events. Heidegger's German is Seiendes, translated "entities" by Macquarrie and Robinson.
  • Being: In Heidegger, what determines beings as beings, the basis on which they are already understood. It is not itself a being.
  • Ontological difference: The difference between being and beings.
  • Ontic: Concerning particular beings and their properties.
  • Ontological: Concerning the being of beings: what it is for something to be at all, or to be a certain kind of thing.
  • Pre-ontological understanding: The everyday understanding of being that we have without any explicit concept of it.
  • Fundamental ontology: Heidegger's name for the analysis of Dasein, the groundwork for raising the question of the meaning of being in general.

Dasein, Being-in-the-World, and Heidegger's Break with Husserl

Introduction

Heidegger was Husserl's assistant and dedicated Being and Time to him. Yet the book's central term was chosen to avoid Husserl's vocabulary of consciousness and subject (Husserl [1913] 2014).

This reading explains why Heidegger calls us "Dasein," what he means by existence, being-in-the-world, and facticity, and how these ideas ground his critique of Husserl. Citations to Being and Time give the section (§) and the German page (H.). Citations to Heidegger's 1925 lectures give the section (§).

Teacher and Student

Heidegger worked closely with Husserl in Freiburg after the First World War, as his assistant, and he learned phenomenology in Husserl's seminars. Being and Time appeared in 1927 in the yearbook Husserl edited, dedicated to him. In 1928 Heidegger succeeded Husserl in his Freiburg chair. The relationship soon strained. Reading the book closely, Husserl concluded that Heidegger had abandoned phenomenology for a philosophy of human existence (Wrathall 2025, §1).

Why "Dasein"

Dasein is Heidegger's term for the kind of being we ourselves are: the being whose own being is an issue for it, and which always has some understanding of being. In ordinary German, Dasein means "existence." Heidegger uses it for its literal sense, "being-there." Both English translations leave it untranslated.

He avoids the familiar terms on purpose (Heidegger [1927] 2008, §10, H. 45–50). "Subject," "soul," "consciousness," "mind," and "person" all carry an ontology with them. They treat the self as a thing that is present with properties, like a stone with its weight, only inner. Descartes, Heidegger says, secured the "I think" (Descartes [1641] 2017, AT 7:25) but left the being of the "I am" undetermined (§10, H. 46). "Dasein" is meant to name us without that assumption built in.

Two features mark Dasein's being (§9, H. 42).

  • Existence, in Heidegger's sense, is Dasein's own kind of being: having to be its being as possibilities it takes up, not as a fixed set of properties. In Macquarrie and Robinson's rendering, the "essence" of Dasein lies in its existence.
  • Mineness (Jemeinigkeit) is the feature that Dasein's being is in each case one's own. You can't be Dasein in general.

Being-in-the-World

Dasein's basic state is being-in-the-world: a single, unified way of being, involved with things, other people, and tasks. It isn't a subject located inside a container, and it isn't a mind facing objects (§12, H. 53).

Heidegger clarifies the "in" by contrast. Water is in a glass, and a garment is in a cupboard. That's spatial inclusion, one present thing inside another. Dasein is "in" the world in a different sense: the way you're in a job or at home in a city. It means dwelling alongside and being familiar with (§12, H. 54).

Facticity is the fact that Dasein always already finds itself in a world and a situation it didn't choose, bound up with the beings it meets there (§12, H. 56). A stone's being-there is a mere fact. Dasein's facticity is lived: you find yourself already speaking a language, living in a body, with a history.

The Break with Husserl

These concepts carry Heidegger's critique of Husserl. He made it explicit in his 1925 Marburg lectures (Heidegger [1979] 1985, §§10–13). Husserl, he argues, determined pure consciousness as a region of absolute being without asking what kind of being intentional acts and the one who performs them actually have. He calls this a neglect of the question of the being of the intentional.

Being and Time draws the consequence. Intentionality is real, but it's not the starting point. Before any act is directed at an object, Dasein is already in a world where things matter and make sense. On this view, the transcendental subject Husserl reaches by the reduction (Husserl [1913] 2014) is an abstraction from being-in-the-world.

The contrast can be set out in a table.

Husserl: consciousness directed at objectsHeidegger: Dasein already absorbed in a world
Starting pointThe intentional act and its objectBeing-in-the-world
What comes firstA subject and its actsInvolvement with things, others, and tasks
A pen on the deskPerceived from one side, meant as a penReached for in the middle of signing a form, barely noticed
The subjectA transcendental ego, reached by the reductionAn abstraction from Dasein's being-in-the-world

Conclusion

"Dasein" names us without the ontology of a thing with inner properties. Its being is existence, it's in each case mine, and its basic state is being-in-the-world, marked by facticity. From here, Heidegger argues that intentionality presupposes being-in-the-world, and that Husserl's transcendental subject abstracts from it.

Key Terms

  • Dasein: Heidegger's term for the kind of being we ourselves are: the being whose own being is an issue for it, and which always has some understanding of being.
  • Existence: In Heidegger, Dasein's own kind of being: having to be its being as possibilities it takes up, not as a fixed set of properties.
  • Mineness (Jemeinigkeit): The feature that Dasein's being is in each case one's own.
  • Being-in-the-world: In Heidegger, Dasein's basic state: a single, unified way of being, involved with things, other people, and tasks, not a subject inside a container or a mind facing objects.
  • Facticity: In Heidegger, the fact that Dasein always already finds itself in a world and a situation it didn't choose, bound up with the beings it meets there.

Hermeneutic Phenomenology: Heidegger's Method Compared with Husserl's

Introduction

Husserl wanted phenomenology to begin free of presuppositions (Husserl [1900–1901] 2001, Introduction to vol. 2, §7). Heidegger holds that no inquiry can, because every interpretation starts from an understanding already in place.

This reading explains why Heidegger calls his phenomenology hermeneutic, what he means by the fore-structure and the hermeneutic circle, and how his method compares with Husserl's. Citations to Being and Time give the section (§) and the German page (H.).

Phenomenology as the Method of Ontology

Heidegger defines phenomenology in §7 of Being and Time. It's a concept of method, not a subject matter. It lets what shows itself be seen from itself, as it shows itself (Heidegger [1927] 2008, §7C, H. 34). What phenomenology must bring to light, though, is exactly what doesn't show itself at first: the being of beings, which is for the most part hidden (§7C, H. 35). That's why Heidegger can say that ontology is possible only as phenomenology.

The consequence for method follows. If being is hidden, and the being who asks about it is Dasein, then phenomenology has to interpret Dasein's own understanding of being. Heidegger therefore calls the phenomenology of Dasein hermeneutic, meaning concerned with interpretation (§7C, H. 37). A hermeneutic phenomenology interprets from an understanding already in place. It doesn't describe from a presuppositionless standpoint.

The Fore-Structure

Heidegger develops the point in his account of interpretation (§32, H. 148–53). Interpretation (Auslegung), for Heidegger, is making explicit what is already understood: working it out as something. When you see a hammer as a hammer, you're interpreting, even if you never put it into words.

Every interpretation draws on a fore-structure: the prior understanding that any interpretation already works from (§32, H. 150). Heidegger divides it into three parts.

PartGermanWhat it suppliesExample: reading a patient interview
Fore-havingVorhabeA prior grasp of the whole in which the thing belongsYou already take this to be a clinical encounter
Fore-sightVorsichtA point of view from which the thing is approachedYou read for signs of distress
Fore-conceptionVorgriffA set of concepts ready to be appliedYou have diagnostic categories at hand

So interpretation is never a presuppositionless grasping of something simply present (§32, H. 150).

The Hermeneutic Circle

This looks circular, and it is. Understanding is needed for interpretation, and interpretation develops understanding. Heidegger calls this the hermeneutic circle: interpretation starts from and develops an understanding it already has.

He denies that the circle is vicious (§32, H. 153). A vicious circle assumes what it sets out to prove. The hermeneutic circle is the structure of all understanding. The task isn't to escape it, which is impossible, but to enter it the right way. The inquirer has to test the fore-structure against the things themselves, rather than letting habits or popular opinion supply it. That's why, for Heidegger, phenomenological method still answers to how things show themselves.

Husserl and Heidegger Compared

The two methods differ at every level (Zahavi 2025; Wrathall 2025).

HusserlHeidegger
Starting pointPure consciousness and its intentional actsDasein's everyday being-in-the-world
MethodEpoché, reduction, and eidetic descriptionHermeneutic interpretation of the understanding already in place
Status of the subjectA transcendental subject that constitutes meaningNo worldless subject; the "subject" is Dasein
GoalPhenomenology as a rigorous science, grounding all knowledgeFundamental ontology: raising the question of the meaning of being

How deep the break goes is disputed. Heidegger presented his work as radicalizing phenomenology, not leaving it (Heidegger [1927] 2008, §7). Commentators sympathetic to Husserl argue that his later analyses of the lifeworld (Husserl [1936] 1970) and of passive, pre-reflective experience already describe much of what Heidegger calls being-in-the-world (Zahavi 2025). Others see a genuine break over whether a transcendental subject is the right place to start. Both readings have serious defenders.

Conclusion

Because the being of beings is hidden and must be drawn out of Dasein's own understanding, Heidegger's phenomenology is hermeneutic. Interpretation works from a fore-structure and moves within a circle that isn't vicious. Set against Husserl's, his method starts from being-in-the-world rather than pure consciousness, and aims at fundamental ontology rather than a rigorous science of consciousness.

Key Terms

  • Hermeneutic: Concerned with interpretation. In Heidegger, a phenomenology is hermeneutic because it interprets from an understanding already in place rather than describing from a presuppositionless standpoint.
  • Interpretation (Auslegung): In Heidegger, making explicit what is already understood: working it out as something.
  • Fore-structure: In Heidegger, the prior understanding that every interpretation already works from, made up of fore-having, fore-sight, and fore-conception.
  • Hermeneutic circle: The circularity by which interpretation starts from and develops an understanding it already has. Heidegger holds that it isn't vicious.

A Map of Being and Time, Its Translations, and Heidegger's Politics

Introduction

Being and Time is an unfinished book, available in two English translations that disagree about its key words, by an author whose politics cast a long shadow. Readers need a map, a translation key, and the facts.

This reading lays out the book's structure, pairs its core terms across the two translations, and gives the historical and political context briefly. Citations to Being and Time give the section (§) and the German page (H.).

A Map of the Book

Heidegger planned Being and Time in two parts of three divisions each (Heidegger [1927] 2008, §8, H. 39–40). Only the first two divisions of Part One were published.

SectionSections (§)Main themes
Introduction§§1–8The question of being; the method of phenomenology (§7)
Division I: The analytic of Dasein§§9–44Being-in-the-world; worldhood and equipment; others and the "they"; attunement, understanding, and discourse; care
Division II: Dasein and temporality§§45–83Being-toward-death; conscience and authenticity; temporality as the meaning of care; historicality

Division I describes Dasein in its everyday being. Division II returns to the same structures to show that time is their deeper meaning. Its themes are treated here at overview level only.

Core Terms in Two Translations

Two English translations are standard. John Macquarrie and Edward Robinson's (1962) is used throughout most English secondary literature. Joan Stambaugh's, revised by Dennis Schmidt (2010), is closer to current usage (Heidegger [1927] 2008; Heidegger [1927] 2010). Both print the German page numbers, so the H. citations work in either.

Later terms diverge more sharply. Macquarrie and Robinson's "ready-to-hand" and "present-at-hand," for example, become "handiness" and "objective presence" in Stambaugh and Schmidt.

Heidegger's Politics

Heidegger's political record belongs in any serious reading of him, stated briefly and factually. In April 1933 he became rector of the University of Freiburg, and in May he joined the Nazi Party. He resigned the rectorship in April 1934, but he remained a party member until the end of the Second World War (Wrathall 2025, §3).

From 2014, Heidegger's private notebooks, the Black Notebooks, began to be published in his collected works. They include antisemitic passages (Heidegger [2014] 2016). Their publication reopened a debate that had run since the 1980s.

Scholars disagree about how far this bears on the philosophy. Some argue that his thought and politics are intertwined, so the published work must be reread in the notebooks' light. Others hold that the analyses of Being and Time, published in 1927, can be assessed on their own terms. Many take an intermediate position: the analyses can be evaluated independently, but readers should ask whether concepts such as the "they" or authenticity lent themselves to his political choices. It remains a live scholarly question.

Heidegger's later work, including the "turn" and the essays on technology, lies beyond the scope here (Wrathall 2025, §4).

Conclusion

Being and Time is the published two-thirds of one part: an introduction, an analytic of Dasein, and a turn to temporality. Its core terms appear under different English names in the two standard translations. Heidegger's Nazi involvement and the Black Notebooks are part of the record, and how they bear on the philosophy is still debated.

  • Ontological difference: The difference between being and beings.
  • Dasein: Heidegger's term for the kind of being we ourselves are: the being whose own being is an issue for it, and which always has some understanding of being.
  • Existence: In Heidegger, Dasein's own kind of being: having to be its being as possibilities it takes up, not as a fixed set of properties.
  • Facticity: In Heidegger, the fact that Dasein always already finds itself in a world and a situation it didn't choose, bound up with the beings it meets there.
  • Hermeneutic: Concerned with interpretation. In Heidegger, a phenomenology is hermeneutic because it interprets from an understanding already in place rather than describing from a presuppositionless standpoint.

Guided Close Reading: Phenomenon and Logos in Heidegger's Being and Time §7

Introduction

Section 7 of Being and Time is Heidegger's definition of phenomenology. He builds it word by word from the two Greek terms that make up the name, phainomenon and logos. The argument is short and careful, and a single misreading of the first term throws the rest off.

This reading follows the section's three moves. It ends with a model paraphrase you can check your reading against.

Locating the Passage

The section runs from H. 27 to H. 39 (Heidegger [1927] 2008, §7, H. 27–39). It has three subsections: A, on the concept of phenomenon; B, on the concept of logos; and C, on the preliminary conception of phenomenology. Both English translations print the German page numbers (H.) in the margin, so you can follow along in either. Key phrases below are quoted from Macquarrie and Robinson.

No free English translation is available. The free Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Heidegger summarizes his method (Wrathall 2025, §2).

Before you start, note Heidegger's aim. He has just argued that the question of being must be raised through an analysis of Dasein. He now needs a method for that analysis. He takes over the name "phenomenology" and its maxim, "To the things themselves!," but he redefines what the method is for.

Walking Through the Argument

Step 1: "Phenomenon" as what shows itself in itself (§7A)

Heidegger starts from the Greek verb phainesthai, "to show itself." A phenomenon, he concludes, is "that which shows itself in itself, the manifest" (§7A, H. 28).

He then separates this from two nearby ideas.

  • Semblance (Schein). A thing can show itself as something it isn't: a stick that looks bent in water, or a false friend who seems loyal. Semblance is still a kind of self-showing. It's a privative one, showing itself falsely.
  • Appearance (Erscheinung). Here something announces itself through something else that does show itself, while it doesn't show itself at all. Heidegger's example is the symptoms of an illness (H. 29). The symptoms show themselves. The illness doesn't. It is indicated by them.

The order of dependence matters. Appearance depends on phenomena, because something must show itself for anything to be announced through it. Semblance depends on phenomena too, because only what can show itself can show itself falsely. The phenomenon, in the strict sense, comes first.

Step 2: "Logos" as letting something be seen (§7B)

Heidegger now turns to logos. It's usually translated as "reason," "judgment," or "definition." He argues that its basic sense is discourse that makes manifest what the discourse is about. The logos is a way of "letting something be seen" (§7B, H. 32–33).

Heidegger ties this to his account of truth. Discourse is true when it takes what it talks about out of hiding and lets it be seen as uncovered. He glosses the Greek word for truth, aletheia, in these terms, as unconcealment. Truth, on this view, isn't first of all agreement between a judgment and a fact. It's the uncovering that such agreement presupposes.

Step 3: The combined definition (§7C)

Putting the two terms together, Heidegger renders the Greek as a formula: phenomenology means "to let that which shows itself be seen from itself" in the very way it shows itself from itself (§7C, H. 34). This, he says, is just the maxim "To the things themselves!" stated formally.

So the name says nothing about the subject matter. It names a how: a way of letting things be seen, without imposing constructions on them.

Why the Definition Makes Phenomenology the Method of Ontology

The next step turns the formal definition into a program. What is it that most needs to be brought into view by such a method? Heidegger's answer: what doesn't show itself at first, what lies hidden behind what does show itself, yet belongs to it essentially. That is the being of beings (§7C, H. 35).

Beings show themselves all the time. Their being usually doesn't. It's covered over, forgotten, or disguised by received concepts. Phenomenology is needed precisely because the phenomena are not simply given. Heidegger concludes that "only as phenomenology, is ontology possible" (§7C, H. 35). And since the being who asks the question is Dasein, the method takes the form of an interpretation of Dasein: it's hermeneutic (§7C, H. 37).

A common misreading. The most common misreading treats Heidegger's "phenomenon" as a mental appearance or a sense datum: an item in consciousness that stands in for an outer thing. That reading gets §7 backward.

  • It confuses phenomenon with appearance. On the misreading, a phenomenon is a representation that points to something beyond it. That's exactly Heidegger's definition of appearance, which announces something that doesn't itself show.
  • It relocates the phenomenon in the mind. For Heidegger, the phenomenon is the thing itself as it shows itself: the hammer, the landscape, and ultimately being. It's not an inner proxy.
  • It misses the target. The phenomenon phenomenology most needs to uncover is the being of beings, which is not a datum at all.

Summary

Section 7 defines phenomenology from its Greek parts. A phenomenon is what shows itself in itself, distinct from semblance and appearance. Logos lets something be seen. Together they define phenomenology as letting what shows itself be seen from itself. Because being is what mostly doesn't show itself, phenomenology becomes the method of ontology. Here is a model paraphrase to check your reading against:

For Heidegger, a phenomenon is whatever shows itself as it is, in itself. It isn't a representation in the mind, which he'd call an appearance, and it isn't a false show, which he calls semblance. Logos is discourse that lets something be seen. So phenomenology is a method, not a subject matter: it lets things be seen as they show themselves. What most needs this method is the being of beings, which is usually hidden. That's why, for Heidegger, ontology is possible only as phenomenology, and why the phenomenology of Dasein must be interpretive (Heidegger [1927] 2008, §7).

HOW TO RUN THE CONVERSATION
- Ask one question at a time, then wait for my reply. Keep each of your turns under about 120 words.
- Don't lecture. Explain a point only when I need it to continue, then return to my hour.
- Be patient, curious, and collegial. Encourage me to stay with concrete moments rather than theorize. Define terms of art briefly on first use.
- Plain conversation only: no web search, files, or artifacts.
- Aim for about 12 minutes. If I struggle to recall the last hour, ask about my morning routine. If I seem uncertain, shorten the conversation to 5-7 minutes. Always reach topic 4.
- Start now. Open with one or two warm sentences: this is an exploratory conversation, not a test; Heidegger's vocabulary is unusual, so tentative answers are welcome; I can ask you to explain any term. Then ask me to bring to mind the last hour, before I opened this activity.

TOPICS, IN ORDER
1. The last hour. Ask whether, in the last hour, I was mostly aware of "my consciousness" or engaged with things, tasks, and people, and have me describe it. Help me notice how much of the hour was absorbed involvement: reading, typing, talking, moving through a space. Ask how often I was aware of my own mental states as such. Introduce Dasein and being-in-the-world as Heidegger's names for this way of being.
2. Understanding being without defining it. Ask what I understand about what it is for a tool, a number, or a person to "be," even without defining it. Help me see that I treat each differently without hesitation: a tool is for using, a number for calculating, a person for addressing. Draw out that this is a pre-ontological understanding of being. Introduce the ontological difference: my examples are beings, and the question is about their being.
3. Does intentionality presuppose being-in-the-world? Tell me Heidegger claims that it does, and ask whether my experience supports or resists that claim. Help me test the claim against an example from my hour: could I have been directed at the object without already being in a situation where it made sense? Welcome resistance. If I defend the view that directed acts come first, press gently on where the sense of the situation came from.
4. Closing reflection. Ask which assumption about minds in my own field Heidegger would challenge. Help me name one concrete assumption, such as treating people as information processors, treating experience as a sequence of inner states, or treating perception as the starting point of cognition.

POSITIONS TO KEEP ACCURATE
- Heidegger replaces consciousness with the question of being as phenomenology's starting point.
- The ontological difference: being is what determines beings as beings, and it isn't itself a being.
- We have a pre-ontological understanding of being, which is why the inquiry starts with Dasein.
- Dasein is the being whose own being is an issue for it. Its basic state is being-in-the-world.
- Facticity: Dasein always already finds itself in a world and situation it didn't choose.
- Heidegger's objection to Husserl: intentionality presupposes being-in-the-world, and the transcendental subject is an abstraction from it. Whether this is fair to Husserl is disputed.
- Phenomenology, for Heidegger, lets what shows itself be seen from itself, and it's hermeneutic.

MISREADINGS TO CORRECT GENTLY
Keep every point tied to my own hour. When one of these appears, name the accurate position briefly, then return to my experience.
- Dasein as "the self," the individual, or consciousness under a new name. Dasein names a way of being, defined by being-in-the-world and by having its being as an issue, not an inner subject.
- Being as a thing, a supreme being, or the sum of everything. Being isn't a being. It's what determines beings as beings.
- Being-in-the-world as spatial location. "In" means dwelling and familiarity, not being contained like water in a glass.
- Heidegger as rejecting intentionality. He grounds it in being-in-the-world, rather than denying it.
- Facticity as "facts about me," such as height or age. Facticity is lived: finding oneself already thrown into a world and situation.
- Hermeneutic as merely subjective. Interpretation still answers to how things show themselves.
