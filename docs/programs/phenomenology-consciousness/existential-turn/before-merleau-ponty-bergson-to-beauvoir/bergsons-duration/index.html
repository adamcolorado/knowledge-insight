Bergson to Beauvoir: Bergson's Duration

This lesson introduces Henri Bergson's distinction between clock time, which lays moments side by side like points in space, and duration (durée), the lived flow in which moments interpenetrate. You'll be able to explain Bergson's critique of spatialized time using his own examples, and outline his account of perception and memory in Matter and Memory. Bergson's ideas remain a reference point in current debates about the experience of time and the limits of measuring consciousness.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain Bergson's concept of duration and his critique of spatialized time

Lived Time and Clock Time: Bergson, Einstein, and Measuring Experience

An hour spent waiting for test results and an hour absorbed in good work take the same time on a clock. They aren't the same as lived. The waiting hour drags, each minute separately felt. The working hour passes as one movement. Most people treat the difference as a distortion: the clock gives the real duration, and experience gets it wrong.

Henri Bergson argued the reverse, or close to it. For him, the clock's time is a useful abstraction, and lived time, which he called duration, is the more basic reality (Bergson [1889] 1913, ch. 2). A melody shows what he meant. You hear it as one flowing whole, not as a row of separate notes that you then add up. Laying the notes out as points on a line, one after another, already changes what you're describing.

Bergson's view made him one of the most famous intellectuals in the world in the early 20th century. His lectures in Paris drew overflow crowds, and his books were read far beyond philosophy (Moulard-Leonard 2025, §1). In April 1922 he debated Albert Einstein in Paris on the nature of time. Bergson argued that relativity's time, measured by clocks, couldn't replace the time of lived experience. Einstein answered that there was no separate time of the philosophers, only physical time and psychological time (Canales 2015). Many at the time judged that Einstein had won, and Bergson's reputation suffered (Canales 2015). The historian Jimena Canales argues that the debate was more even than that verdict suggests, and that its questions about what clocks measure remained open.

Those questions bear directly on research. Timing experiments, reaction-time studies, and many methods in cognitive science treat time as a line of measurable points. Questionnaires that ask how long an episode felt inherit the same picture. That's a powerful approach, and much of what's known about perception depends on it. But it builds in a choice. It assumes the time of experience can be represented as positions on a line without losing anything that matters. If Bergson is right, some features of experience, such as the way earlier moments color later ones, don't survive that representation.

Knowing Bergson's argument gives you a clear account of what measuring experience may leave out. It also gives you a historical case in which physics and philosophy disagreed about time, and in which the question of who was right turned out to be less settled than it first appeared.

Quantitative and Qualitative Multiplicity in Bergson's Time and Free Will

Introduction

Before Bergson can say what lived time is, he asks what it means to count. His answer, that counting always smuggles in space, is the base of his whole critique of clock time.

This reading explains his distinction between two kinds of multiplicity and why he thinks counting requires space. Citations to Time and Free Will give the chapter, which stays stable across editions. The Pogson translation is free on Project Gutenberg, so you can check each passage.

What Counting Requires

Bergson starts with an ordinary example: counting sheep in a flock (Bergson [1889] 1913, ch. 2). To count them, you have to do two things.

  1. Ignore their differences. For counting, each sheep has to be treated as a unit identical to the others. Their individual features don't matter.
  2. Keep them distinct. The units have to stay separate, or you couldn't tell the fifth from the sixth.

What lets identical units remain distinct? Bergson's answer is position. The sheep occupy different places. Even when you count sheep in imagination, or count abstract units, you set them out side by side in an ideal space: you picture them, or picture the points that stand for them. On Bergson's analysis, every act of counting involves this imagined spatial layout, whether you notice it or not.

Two Kinds of Multiplicity

This leads Bergson to distinguish two ways in which something can be many.

A quantitative multiplicity, in Bergson's sense, is a multiplicity of distinct, identical units set side by side, which can be counted. It presupposes space. The sheep, the coins in a pile, and the ticks marked on a ruler are quantitative multiplicities. Their parts relate by juxtaposition: in Bergson's usage, the setting of distinct items side by side, as in space.

A qualitative multiplicity is a multiplicity of states that interpenetrate and can't be counted, because each colors the others. Bergson's examples come from inner life. A deepening feeling of joy isn't a heap of smaller joys. It's a single state that changes in quality as it grows, coloring more and more of one's other states. Its phases relate by interpenetration: the way conscious states permeate and color one another in duration, without clear boundaries.

The contrast can be set out in a table.

Quantitative multiplicityQualitative multiplicity
PartsDistinct, identical unitsInterpenetrating states
How parts relateJuxtaposition, side by sideInterpenetration, each coloring the others
Can it be counted?Yes, by setting units out in spaceNo, not without distorting it
ChangeAdding or removing unitsChange in the quality of the whole
ExampleSheep in a flockA growing feeling of joy

Why the Distinction Matters

Bergson's target is a habit that runs through psychology and philosophy: treating conscious states as if they formed a quantitative multiplicity. On that habit, a mental life is a series of separate states, each distinct from the next, which can be counted, ordered on a line, and measured. Bergson's earlier chapter on the intensity of feelings attacks one version of the habit, the attempt to measure sensations as magnitudes (ch. 1).

His claim is that the habit carries a concept from space into a domain where it doesn't belong. When we count our states, we've already laid them out side by side. The states themselves, as lived, form a qualitative multiplicity. They're many, but not in a way that allows counting.

The same point is about to apply to time. If moments of lived time are counted, placed on a line, and measured, they're treated as a quantitative multiplicity, and so as space.

Conclusion

Counting requires treating units as identical and keeping them distinct, and Bergson argues that only space makes that possible. A quantitative multiplicity is countable, made of units juxtaposed side by side. A qualitative multiplicity is made of interpenetrating states that color one another and can't be counted without distortion. Bergson holds that conscious life is a qualitative multiplicity.

Key Terms

  • Quantitative multiplicity: In Bergson, a multiplicity of distinct, identical units set side by side, which can be counted. It presupposes space.
  • Juxtaposition: In Bergson, the setting of distinct items side by side, as in space.
  • Qualitative multiplicity: In Bergson, a multiplicity of states that interpenetrate and can't be counted, because each colors the others.
  • Interpenetration: In Bergson, the way conscious states permeate and color one another in duration, without clear boundaries.

Duration, Spatialized Time, and Free Acts in Bergson's Time and Free Will

Introduction

Clock time looks like the most neutral thing there is: a line of equal moments, one after another. Bergson argues that this picture of time is space in disguise, and that lived time is something else.

This reading explains how Bergson thinks we spatialize time, what he means by duration, and what follows for freedom. Citations to Time and Free Will give the chapter.

How Time Gets Spatialized

Earlier in the same chapter, Bergson argues that counting requires space. He now applies that argument to time (Bergson [1889] 1913, ch. 2).

When you measure time, you count: seconds on a clock, swings of a pendulum, frames of a film. Each counted moment is treated as a unit identical to the others and distinct from them. That makes time a quantitative multiplicity: a multiplicity of distinct, identical units set side by side, which can be counted. It presupposes space. So the time we measure is time laid out as a line: a series of positions, each outside the others.

Spatialized time, for Bergson, is time represented as a line of distinct, homogeneous moments laid side by side, as in clock time. He argues that homogeneous time of this kind is really space in disguise, a fourth dimension of space smuggled into our idea of succession.

Bergson's analysis of the clock shows how this happens. When you watch a pendulum, each swing you see is present only as a single position. The past swings are gone. What you actually perceive is a series of simultaneities: the pendulum's position at each moment. Only consciousness holds the swings together, and when it counts them, it lays them out side by side in an imagined space.

Duration

What is time, then, if not a line? Bergson's answer is duration (durée): lived time as a qualitative flow in which moments interpenetrate, a succession without distinction. It has the structure of a qualitative multiplicity: a multiplicity of states that interpenetrate and can't be counted, because each colors the others.

Bergson describes duration as what the succession of conscious states becomes when the self lets itself live, without separating its present state from its former states. In duration, earlier moments aren't left behind as separate points. They carry into and color the present.

His central image is a melody (ch. 2). You hear its notes in succession, yet you perceive them in one another, as a single whole. The whole is heard, not assembled. The same holds for the strokes of a clock heard while you're absorbed in something else: you can grasp their rhythm as a whole before you ever count them.

The contrast can be set out in a table.

Spatialized timeDuration
PictureBeads on a lineAn unbroken, shaded flow
MomentsDistinct, identical, side by sideInterpenetrating, each coloring the next
Past momentsLeft behind, at earlier positionsCarried into the present
A melodyA sequence of separate notesOne phrase, heard as a whole
Known byCounting and measuringLiving through it

Free Acts

The same distinction yields Bergson's account of freedom, which is the goal of the book (ch. 3). Debates about free will usually picture the self as passing through a series of separate states, with the question being whether each state causes the next. Bergson thinks that picture already spatializes the self. A free act, for Bergson, is an act that springs from and expresses the whole self in duration. It isn't caused by one prior state, and it isn't uncaused. It flows from the whole personality, the way a mature work expresses its author.

Conclusion

Measured time is a quantitative multiplicity, so for Bergson it's spatialized: time laid out as a line of homogeneous moments, space in disguise. Lived time is duration, a qualitative flow in which moments interpenetrate, as in a melody. Free acts express the whole self in duration.

Key Terms

  • Quantitative multiplicity: In Bergson, a multiplicity of distinct, identical units set side by side, which can be counted. It presupposes space.
  • Spatialized time: In Bergson, time represented as a line of distinct, homogeneous moments laid side by side, as in clock time. He calls it space in disguise.
  • Duration (durée): In Bergson, lived time as a qualitative flow in which moments interpenetrate: a succession without distinction.
  • Qualitative multiplicity: In Bergson, a multiplicity of states that interpenetrate and can't be counted, because each colors the others.
  • Free act: In Bergson, an act that springs from and expresses the whole self in duration.

Perception, Action, and Two Forms of Memory in Bergson's Matter and Memory

Introduction

Most theories of perception ask how the brain produces an inner picture of the world. In Matter and Memory (1896), Bergson rejects the question (Bergson [1896] 1911, ch. 1). For him, perception doesn't add a picture. It subtracts everything that doesn't matter for action.

This reading gives an overview of Bergson's account of perception, the body, and two forms of memory in the book's first two chapters. Citations to Matter and Memory give the chapter.

Perception as Selection

Bergson opens with a deliberately neutral term. He calls everything that exists an image: in his usage, anything that exists, taken as it appears, more than a representation in the mind and less than a thing hidden behind its appearance (Bergson [1896] 1911, ch. 1). Matter is the whole of images, acting and reacting on one another.

Among these images, one is special: my body. Other images act on it, and it acts back. Bergson calls the body a center of action: a place where influences are received and actions are initiated, not a producer of representations.

What, then, is perception? Bergson's answer is that it's selection. Out of the whole field of images, perception picks out those that bear on the body's possible actions and lets the rest pass unnoticed. Perception doesn't create an inner copy of the world. It measures the body's possible action on things. That's why what you perceive depends on what you can do: the ledge you could step onto stands out, and the texture of the wall beyond reach fades.

The Brain as Switchboard

This view changes the role of the brain. Bergson denies that the brain stores or produces representations. It's more like a telephone exchange: it receives movements and either relays them directly or delays them, opening a range of possible responses (ch. 1). The more complex the nervous system, the wider the range of choice between stimulus and response. On this view, the brain is an organ of action, not of representation.

Two Forms of Memory

In chapter 2, Bergson distinguishes two forms of memory, using the example of learning a lesson by heart (ch. 2).

Suppose you learn a poem by reading it aloud many times. Two different things are retained.

  • Habit memory, for Bergson, is memory stored in the body as acquired motor mechanisms, repeated rather than represented, like a lesson learned by heart. Once you've learned the poem, you can recite it without recalling any particular reading. The memory is in the performance.
  • Pure memory is the memory that records each past event as a unique, dated image, recalled rather than repeated. You can also recall the third time you read the poem, where you were, and how the light fell. That reading happened once and can't be repeated.

The contrast can be set out in a table.

Habit memoryPure memory
What's retainedAn acquired motor mechanismA unique past event, as an image
How it's usedRepeated, performedRecalled, imagined
Where it livesIn the bodyNot in the body; in the past itself
ExampleReciting the poemRemembering the third reading

The two forms cooperate in ordinary life. Habit memory lets us act. Pure memory supplies images that help interpret the present, and the body's current action selects which past images become relevant.

Conclusion

In Matter and Memory, Bergson treats perception as selection: out of the field of images, the body, as a center of action, picks out what bears on its possible actions. The brain relays and delays movement rather than producing representations. Memory takes two forms: habit memory, stored in the body and repeated, and pure memory, which records unique past events and is recalled.

Key Terms

  • Image: In Bergson's Matter and Memory, anything that exists, taken as it appears: more than a representation in the mind, less than a thing hidden behind its appearance.
  • Center of action: In Bergson, the body's role as a place where influences are received and actions are initiated, not a producer of representations.
  • Habit memory: In Bergson, memory stored in the body as acquired motor mechanisms, repeated rather than represented, like a lesson learned by heart.
  • Pure memory: In Bergson, the memory that records each past event as a unique, dated image, recalled rather than repeated.

Bergson and Husserl on Time, and Bergson's Eclipse and Return

Introduction

Bergson and Husserl both argued, around 1900, that the present we live through isn't an instant. Yet French philosophy soon set Bergson aside for Husserl. The comparison shows why they're often paired, and why they came apart.

This reading gathers Bergson's core concepts (Bergson [1889] 1913; Bergson [1896] 1911), compares his account of time with Husserl's, and traces Bergson's eclipse and later reappraisal.

Bergson and Husserl on Time

Both thinkers reject the idea that experience is a series of instants. They differ in method and in how they describe the present (Husserl [1928] 1991; Moulard-Leonard 2025; Zahavi 2025, §4).

BergsonHusserl
MethodIntuition: placing oneself within duration, against the analytic habits of the intellectReflective description of how temporal objects are given, after bracketing objective time
Key conceptDuration, a qualitative flowThe living present: primal impression, retention, and protention
Status of the presentNot a separable point; the past carries into it without breakA structured present with phases, each with its own temporal mode
Attitude to analysisAnalysis spatializes; duration is grasped by intuitionAnalysis of structure is the method itself

The deepest difference is about analysis. Husserl's phenomenology describes the present as a structure with distinguishable phases. Bergson would suspect that distinguishing phases already begins to lay time out in space. Husserl, for his part, would ask how Bergson's intuition could be communicated or checked without some articulated description. The two accounts are often read as complementary, but each exposes a risk in the other.

Eclipse and Return

Bergson's reputation fell sharply from the 1920s on (Moulard-Leonard 2025, §7). A new generation of French philosophers, including the young Sartre and Merleau-Ponty, turned to Husserl and Heidegger. They found in phenomenology a rigorous method and in Bergson a vocabulary that seemed vague, even mystical. Bergson's defeat in public opinion after his 1922 debate with Einstein didn't help.

Merleau-Ponty later reconsidered. In his lectures and essays of the 1950s, he treated Bergson as a thinker whose account of the body, perception, and time anticipated central themes of his own phenomenology (Merleau-Ponty [1960] 1964). That reappraisal is a useful contrast to the earlier dismissal: the same ideas that seemed vague to one generation looked prescient to the next.

Bergson's later work goes beyond the scope here. His Creative Evolution (1907) extended the idea of duration to life as a whole, driven by a creative impulse he called the élan vital (Bergson [1907] 1911).

Conclusion

Bergson's core concepts are duration, qualitative multiplicity, spatialized time, the body as center of action, and two forms of memory. He and Husserl agree that the present isn't an instant, but differ over whether analyzing its structure spatializes it. Bergson was eclipsed as French philosophy turned to phenomenology, and later reappraised by Merleau-Ponty.

Key Terms

  • Duration (durée): In Bergson, lived time as a qualitative flow in which moments interpenetrate: a succession without distinction.
  • Qualitative multiplicity: In Bergson, a multiplicity of states that interpenetrate and can't be counted, because each colors the others.
  • Spatialized time: In Bergson, time represented as a line of distinct, homogeneous moments laid side by side, as in clock time. He calls it space in disguise.
  • Center of action: In Bergson, the body's role as a place where influences are received and actions are initiated, not a producer of representations.
  • Habit memory: In Bergson, memory stored in the body as acquired motor mechanisms, repeated rather than represented, like a lesson learned by heart.
  • Pure memory: In Bergson, the memory that records each past event as a unique, dated image, recalled rather than repeated.

Guided Close Reading: Bergson's Melody and Clock Passages in Time and Free Will, Chapter 2

Introduction

Bergson's argument for duration rests on a handful of short passages in the second chapter of Time and Free Will. The best known concern a melody and the striking of a clock. They're brief and vivid, and they're easy to read as mere impressions. This reading shows how they work as arguments. It ends with a model paraphrase contrasting the two kinds of multiplicity.

Locating the Passage

The passages are in chapter 2, "The Multiplicity of Conscious States: The Idea of Duration" (Bergson [1889] 1913, ch. 2). F. L. Pogson's translation is in the public domain and free on Project Gutenberg, so you can read the whole chapter and check every phrase quoted here. Chapter numbers are the same in every edition, and the French original, Essai sur les données immédiates de la conscience, is also freely available (Bergson 1889).

Before you start, note the setting. Bergson has just argued that counting requires space: to count, you must set identical units side by side in an ideal space. He now asks what happens when we apply number to time.

Walking Through the Argument

Step 1: Notes melt into one another in a melody

Bergson asks us to recall the notes of a tune. The notes succeed one another, yet we don't hear them as separate items in a row. In Pogson's rendering, they come to us "melting, so to speak, into one another." We perceive them in one another, and their totality is like a living being, whose parts are distinct but permeate one another because they're so closely connected.

Notice what's being claimed. It's not that the notes are blurred or indistinct. Each note is heard. The claim is about how they're related: not side by side, like marks on a page, but each carrying the others into it. That's a qualitative multiplicity.

Step 2: Changing one note alters the whole

The second move is a test. Suppose a musician lingers too long on one note. How do we notice the mistake?

Bergson's answer is that we don't notice it by measuring the note's excess length, as length. We notice it because the whole phrase has changed in quality. The error shows up as a change in the melody as a whole, not as an extra unit added to a sum.

This is the argument's hinge. If the melody were a quantitative multiplicity, a longer note would simply add a little more of the same. Instead, the change affects the whole. So the whole isn't built from independent parts. In Bergson's terms, the notes interpenetrate.

Step 3: Counting the clock's strokes requires laying them out in space

The clock passage adds a further step. Bergson describes writing while a neighboring clock strikes. His attention is elsewhere, so he doesn't notice the first few strokes. Yet when he turns his attention back, he can count the strokes already sounded and add them to those he's now hearing.

How is that possible? Bergson's answer has two parts.

  • Before counting, the strokes were grasped as a whole: a rhythm, a qualitative impression of four strokes, not four separate items. He can recognize the count because the whole has a character, the way a phrase does.
  • To count them, he has to lay them out, one beside another, in an imagined space. Only then do they become distinct, identical units that can be numbered.

The two ways of grasping the strokes are the two multiplicities. Lived, the strokes form a qualitative whole. Counted, they form a quantitative series. Counting doesn't reveal what was there all along. It converts one kind of multiplicity into the other.

Terms and translations. Bergson's key terms are simple words used precisely.

A common misreading. The most common misreading treats duration as a subjective illusion laid over "real" clock time. On this reading, the clock tells the truth, and the melting melody is how time feels to a distracted mind. That reverses Bergson's argument.

  • For Bergson, duration is the real. Spatialized time is a symbol, abstracted from duration for the purposes of counting and action.
  • Clock time isn't false. It's useful, indeed indispensable, for coordinating action and doing physics. But it's derivative: it depends on lived duration to be read at all, since consciousness must hold the clock's positions together.
  • Duration isn't about speed. The waiting hour that drags and the absorbed hour that flies both have duration. What makes duration distinctive is how its moments are related, not how fast they seem to pass.

Summary

The melody passage shows notes interpenetrating. The test of the lingering note shows that a change in one part changes the whole. The clock passage shows that counting requires converting a lived whole into units laid out in space. Here is a model paraphrase to check your reading against:

A quantitative multiplicity is made of distinct, identical units set side by side, and it can be counted, but only by laying its units out in space. A qualitative multiplicity is made of states that interpenetrate, each coloring the others, so it can't be counted without distortion. Lived time is a qualitative multiplicity. The notes of a melody melt into one another, so that lingering on one changes the whole phrase. The strokes of a clock are first grasped as a whole, and they become countable only when laid out in an imagined space. Clock time is that laying-out: useful, but derived from duration (Bergson [1889] 1913, ch. 2).

Claude, please facilitate a reflective dialogue with me. I'm a graduate-level learner studying Bergson's account of duration and spatialized time. Follow this guidance for the whole conversation.

GOAL
Help me explain Bergson's concept of duration and his critique of spatialized time by contrasting how two stretches of my own experience were lived with how they would be measured.

HOW TO RUN THE CONVERSATION
- Ask one question at a time, then wait for my reply. Keep each of your turns under about 120 words.
- Don't lecture. Explain a point only when I need it to continue, then return to my experience.
- Be patient, curious, and collegial. Encourage concrete description before theory. Define terms of art briefly on first use.
- Plain conversation only: no web search, files, or artifacts.
- Aim for about 12 minutes. If I struggle with the melody question, suggest a short song I know well or a familiar voice. If I seem uncertain, shorten the conversation to 5-7 minutes. Always reach topic 4.
- Start now. Open with one or two warm sentences: this is an exploratory conversation, not a test; time is hard to describe, so tentative answers are welcome; I can ask you to explain any term. Then ask me to think of two stretches of time from my recent days.

TOPICS, IN ORDER
1. Two periods, same length. Ask me to think of two periods of the same clock length, one that dragged and one that flew, and to describe how each was lived. Draw out concrete detail. Then gently steer past speed: ask how the moments in each period related to one another. Did earlier moments carry into later ones, or did each stand apart?
2. A melody or a conversation. Ask me to choose a piece of music or a conversation, and ask whether I experienced it as separate units or as an interpenetrating whole. Help me notice that the whole was heard, not assembled, and that an earlier phrase or remark colored what followed. Introduce duration and qualitative multiplicity. If I know music, ask what happens to a phrase when one note is held too long.
3. Counting and timing. Ask what happens to the experience when I start counting or timing it. Help me see that counting lays the moments out as distinct units, side by side. Introduce spatialized time and quantitative multiplicity. Emphasize that Bergson doesn't call clock time false, only derivative.
4. Closing reflection. Ask where in my own research or reading time is treated as spatialized, and tell me to note it for a journal entry. Help me name one concrete case, such as reaction-time measures, time-stamped logs, or survey scales.

POSITIONS TO KEEP ACCURATE
- A quantitative multiplicity is made of distinct, identical units side by side, and counting it requires space.
- A qualitative multiplicity is made of interpenetrating states that can't be counted without distortion.
- Spatialized time is time represented as a line of homogeneous moments: for Bergson, space in disguise.
- Duration is lived time as a qualitative flow in which moments interpenetrate, as in a melody.
- Clock time is useful and legitimate, but derived from duration.
- Free acts express the whole self in duration.
- In Matter and Memory, perception is selection for action, and memory takes two forms: habit memory and pure memory.

MISREADINGS TO CORRECT GENTLY
Keep every point tied to my own experience. When one of these appears, name the accurate position briefly, then return to my experience.
- Duration as time passing faster or slower. Duration concerns how moments relate, interpenetrating rather than side by side, not how fast time seems to pass.
- Duration as a subjective illusion over real clock time. For Bergson, duration is the real, and clock time is a useful symbol derived from it.
- Bergson as anti-science. He accepts measurement as indispensable for action and physics. His claim is about what measurement leaves out.
- Qualitative multiplicity as vague or blurred. Each note is heard. The point is that they interpenetrate.
- Duration as identical to Husserl's retention. If Husserl comes up, note that both reject the instantaneous present, but Bergson would suspect that analyzing the present into phases begins to spatialize it.

TO FINISH
After I name my case, close in one short turn:
- Affirm one precise observation I made, in my own words where possible.
- Suggest one or two next steps that fit how the conversation went. Possible steps: write up one experience using the two kinds of multiplicity; review the two kinds of multiplicity; go back over the terms duration, qualitative multiplicity, and spatialized time; work through the contrast between the melody and the clock again; compare Bergson's duration with Husserl's analysis of the living present; consider what Bergson's critique implies for reaction-time research; try the conversation again with a short melody, listening before counting.
- Restate my case of spatialized time on its own line, labeled "Journal note," so I can copy it as the focus for a journal entry.
