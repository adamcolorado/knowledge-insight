Others, Language, Art: Critical Phenomenology

This lesson examines how Frantz Fanon, Iris Marion Young, and Sara Ahmed extend and challenge Merleau-Ponty's body-subject to show how race, gender, and orientation shape lived experience. You'll be able to explain Fanon's "historico-racial schema," Young's "inhibited intentionality," and Ahmed's account of orientation, and evaluate what they reveal about the limits of a general account of the body. Critical phenomenology is an active research field that informs current work on social identity, embodiment, and consciousness.

What you will be able to do

  • Evaluate critical phenomenology's extensions of the body-subject to gender and race

Critical Phenomenology and the Generality of the Lived Body

Phenomenology describes experience in general terms. It speaks of "the" lived body, "the" perceiver, and "the" body schema. These descriptions are meant to hold for anyone. A description of how a body reaches for a cup, fits through a doorway, or understands another person's gesture isn't offered as a report about one person. It's offered as a description of a structure of embodied experience.

That generality is a strength. It's also a claim that can be tested. The question is whether descriptions of "the" body quietly assume one kind of body and one social position: a body that moves without hesitation, that isn't singled out by others' looks, and whose way of being in the world is treated as the norm. If they do, the description isn't false for everyone. But it isn't general either. It generalizes from a particular situation without saying so.

This question isn't unique to philosophy. Any research that builds on first-person accounts faces it. Psychology has long drawn its participants from a narrow range of populations and reported the results as findings about people in general. A phenomenologist who describes "the" lived body from their own experience runs the same risk. The question is always whose experience a general claim was built from, and who has been left out of it.

Critical phenomenology is the field that takes this question as its starting point. It uses phenomenological description, but it also asks how social structures, such as racism, sexism, and heteronormativity, shape experience at the level of the body and perception (Guenther 2019). A reference collection published in 2019 maps the field's central concepts and shows how widely they're now used (Weiss, Murphy, and Salamon 2019). The field draws on earlier work that tested Merleau-Ponty's body-subject (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012) against race, gender, and sexual orientation. Frantz Fanon described how being fixed by a racializing look disrupts the body schema (Fanon [1952] 2008, ch. 5). Iris Marion Young described how feminine bodily comportment is shaped by social situation (Young [1980] 2005). Sara Ahmed described how bodies are oriented by the lines they're expected to follow (Ahmed 2006a, 2006b).

For a researcher, this approach gives you a way to test any account of experience, including your own. You can ask whether a description holds across differences in social situation, and what it misses if it doesn't. You can also ask what would need to change for it to include the cases it left out. That's a question about method, not only about fairness. A general account of experience earns its generality only by surviving that test.

Fanon's Historico-Racial Schema and Epidermal Racial Schema

Introduction

Frantz Fanon's Black Skin, White Masks (1952) contains one of the most influential uses of Merleau-Ponty's body schema. Fanon, a psychiatrist born in Martinique, accepts the schema as a description of bodily life. He then shows what happens to it under a racializing look.

This reading explains the historico-racial schema and the epidermal racial schema from chapter 5, "The Lived Experience of the Black Man" in Richard Philcox's translation. The chapter is paraphrased throughout, and its racial slurs aren't reproduced. Citations give the chapter number, which holds across translations.

The Body Schema Accepted

Fanon begins where Merleau-Ponty does (Fanon [1952] 2008, ch. 5). The body schema (schéma corporel), in Merleau-Ponty, is a dynamic, unified sense of the body's position and possibilities for action, not an inventory of parts or a mental image of the body (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, pt. 1, ch. 3). Fanon gives an example of it at work. If he wants to smoke, he knows without looking that he must reach across the table for the cigarettes and that the matches are in the drawer to his left. He doesn't calculate these movements. His body knows its way around its world.

Fanon describes this schema as a slow composition of the self as a body in a world of space and time. It isn't imposed on him. It forms through his own living.

The Historico-Racial Schema

Fanon's next move is to find something beneath it. He calls it the historico-racial schema: in Fanon, a layer beneath the body schema, woven from the stories, images, and looks of a racializing society, that shapes how a racialized person lives their body.

The difference lies in where each schema's elements come from. The body schema is built from his own sensations and movements. The historico-racial schema is built by others. Fanon writes that its elements were supplied by the white world, which had woven him out of a thousand details, anecdotes, and stories. He lives his body through images he didn't make.

Body schemaHistorico-racial schema
Where its elements come fromOne's own sensations and movementsOthers' stories, images, and looks
How it formsThrough one's own livingThrough a racializing history and society
What it givesA body that knows its way around its worldA body lived through images imposed from outside
Its effect on actionFluent, unreflectiveSelf-conscious, hesitant, fixed

The Epidermal Racial Schema

Fanon then describes an encounter. In the street, a white child points him out to its mother by his race and says it's frightened. Fanon describes his body schema collapsing under the look. Its place is taken by an epidermal racial schema: in Fanon, the schema that displaces the body schema when a person is fixed by a racializing look, so that they live their body as its skin color seen by others.

The shift here is from a body that acts to a body that is looked at. Fanon writes that he was no longer aware of his body in the third person but in a triple person. He was responsible at once for his body, his race, and his ancestors. His body was given back to him distorted, as an object among objects.

Fanon names the structure of this experience directly. He is overdetermined from without: Fanon's phrase for being defined in advance by others' images, so that one's appearance is taken to reveal what one is. It isn't the idea others have of him that binds him, he says, but his appearance. He is seen before he can act, and what he is has already been decided by how he looks.

What Fanon Adds

Fanon doesn't reject Merleau-Ponty's body schema. He uses it. But he shows that the schema's fluency depends on conditions that not every body enjoys. For a racialized body, the world doesn't simply answer the body's "I can." It returns a look that interrupts it. The body schema, on Fanon's account, isn't socially neutral. It can be disrupted, and replaced, by a schema that others impose.

Conclusion

Fanon accepts the body schema as the composition of the self as a body in a world. Beneath it, he finds a historico-racial schema woven by others. Under a racializing look, the body schema collapses and an epidermal racial schema replaces it, and the person is overdetermined from without. His account shows that the lived body is shaped by social history, not only by its own movements.

Key Terms

  • Body schema (schéma corporel): In Merleau-Ponty, a dynamic, unified sense of the body's position and possibilities for action, not an inventory of parts or a mental image of the body.
  • Historico-racial schema: In Fanon, a layer beneath the body schema, woven from the stories, images, and looks of a racializing society, that shapes how a racialized person lives their body.
  • Epidermal racial schema: In Fanon, the schema that displaces the body schema when a person is fixed by a racializing look, so that they live their body as its skin color seen by others.
  • Overdetermined from without: Fanon's phrase for being defined in advance by others' images, so that one's appearance is taken to reveal what one is.

Young's Phenomenology of Feminine Motility and Its Challenge to Merleau-Ponty

Introduction

Iris Marion Young's "Throwing Like a Girl" (1980) is one of the most widely read essays in feminist phenomenology. It takes a familiar insult as a phenomenological problem: why do many girls and women move in ways that don't use the body's full capacities?

This reading explains Young's three modalities of feminine motility and her account of their source, then sets her work beside Fanon's. The essay has no standard section numbers, so citations give its original date only.

The Problem

Young starts from an observation by the psychologist Erwin Straus (Young [1980] 2005). Straus noted that girls of a certain age tend to throw differently from boys. They don't bring the whole body into the motion, twist the torso, or step into the throw. Straus explained the difference by a biological "feminine attitude" (Straus [1952] 1966). Young accepts the observation and rejects the explanation.

Her framework combines two sources. From Simone de Beauvoir, she takes the idea that a woman in a sexist society lives her body both as a subject and as an object: as capacity and as a thing looked at (Beauvoir [1949] 2010). From Merleau-Ponty, she takes the body as the subject of perception and action, directed at the world through movement (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, pt. 1, ch. 3).

Young limits her claim. She describes typical patterns among women in contemporary Western industrial societies, not all women, and not every woman's every movement (Lennon and Fischer 2024, §8.1).

Three Modalities of Feminine Motility

Young names three modalities.

  1. Ambiguous transcendence: in Young, the way feminine bodily existence reaches toward the world while also living the body as a thing, so that movement never fully leaves the body behind for its task. The body is both a capacity and a burden to be protected.
  2. Inhibited intentionality: in Young, a bodily directedness toward a task that is undercut by a simultaneous sense of not being able, so that the body reaches toward its aim while holding back from full commitment. Where Merleau-Ponty's body says "I can," the body Young describes says "I can" and "I cannot" at once.
  3. Discontinuous unity: in Young, a split between the body's aim and its movement, and between the parts of the body, so that some parts move while others stay inert. In a throw, the arm moves while the rest of the body holds still.

Each modality is a variation on motor intentionality: in Merleau-Ponty, the body's directedness toward things and goals through movement itself, aimed at things rather than coordinates, and not mediated by explicit representations. Young's claim is that this directedness is itself shaped, and sometimes held back, by social situation.

Space

Young also describes feminine spatiality. Many women, she observes, live the space around them as enclosed: a limited region they move within, rather than a space they reach out into. And they often experience themselves as positioned in space, as objects located somewhere, rather than as the center from which space is organized.

The Source: Situation, Not Anatomy

Young's central claim concerns cause. These modalities don't come from anatomy. They come from a situation. Girls are taught to be careful, to avoid getting hurt, and not to take up space. They get less practice at using the whole body in sport and physical tasks. And they live under the look of others, which makes them aware of their bodies as objects to be seen. The result is bodily self-consciousness that interrupts action.

What Fanon and Young Show

Fanon and Young both use Merleau-Ponty's account, and both show its limit. The body schema and motor intentionality are real structures of embodied life. But they aren't socially neutral. A racializing look can displace the body schema (Fanon [1952] 2008, ch. 5), and a sexist situation can inhibit motor intentionality. A general account of "the" body-subject that doesn't register these differences describes one situation as if it were all of them.

Conclusion

Young explains feminine motility through three modalities: ambiguous transcendence, inhibited intentionality, and discontinuous unity. She traces them to social situation, not biology. With Fanon, she shows that the body-subject Merleau-Ponty described is shaped by the social world it lives in.

Key Terms

  • Ambiguous transcendence: In Young, the way feminine bodily existence reaches toward the world while also living the body as a thing, so that movement never fully leaves the body behind for its task.
  • Inhibited intentionality: In Young, a bodily directedness toward a task that is undercut by a simultaneous sense of not being able, so that the body reaches toward its aim while holding back from full commitment.
  • Discontinuous unity: In Young, a split between the body's aim and its movement, and between the parts of the body, so that some parts move while others stay inert.
  • Motor intentionality: In Merleau-Ponty, the body's directedness toward things and goals through movement itself, aimed at things rather than coordinates, and not mediated by explicit representations.

Ahmed's Queer Phenomenology: Orientation, Lines, and Disorientation

Introduction

Phenomenology has always been concerned with direction. Consciousness is directed at objects, and the body is directed at tasks. Sara Ahmed's Queer Phenomenology (2006) asks a further question: what directs the directedness? Why are some objects in front of us and others behind?

This reading explains Ahmed's account of orientation, lines, queerness, and disorientation. Citations give the book (2006a) or the free article version of its argument (2006b).

Orientation

Ahmed's central concept is orientation: in Ahmed, the way bodies are directed toward some objects and away from others, which shapes what is within their reach (Ahmed 2006a, 2006b). To be oriented is to face certain things, to have them within reach, and to be able to act on them. What we're oriented toward isn't simply chosen. It depends on where we're placed, what we've inherited, and what we've repeatedly done.

Ahmed makes the point through the classic phenomenologist's example: the writing table, which Husserl describes as he writes (Husserl [1913] 2014, §35; Ahmed 2006a). Phenomenologists often describe the table in front of them as they write. Ahmed asks what this description leaves out. The philosopher can turn to the table because other things are behind him: a household and the domestic labor, often done by others, that frees him to write. What's in front depends on what's behind. Orientation has a history and a background, even when the description leaves them out.

Lines

Orientation is sustained by lines. A line, in Ahmed, is a path that bodies follow, often repeated and inherited, that directs them toward some objects and makes others harder to reach. A family line, a career path, and a social expectation are all lines in this sense.

Ahmed's claim is that lines are made by being followed. Each time a body follows a line, it makes the line clearer and easier to follow for the next. Bodies, in turn, take shape by following lines. They acquire the habits, skills, and tendencies that the lines require. Heterosexuality, on this account, functions as a straight line: a path that many bodies are expected to follow, which directs them toward some objects of desire and away from others.

Queer and Disoriented

Ahmed uses the word "queer" in two senses. In the narrower sense, it names non-heterosexual desire and life. In the broader sense, queer in Ahmed means what is oblique or off line: out of alignment with the lines one is expected to follow. Ahmed keeps both senses in play. Sexual orientation, on her account, is a matter of orientation in the phenomenological sense: of which objects a body is directed toward.

This leads to disorientation: in Ahmed, the experience of the world failing to line up with one's body, which can unsettle and reveal the lines one had been following. A body that doesn't follow the expected line may find that the world isn't arranged for it. Things aren't within reach, or the path isn't clear. Ahmed doesn't treat disorientation as simply negative. It can make visible the lines that orientation normally hides.

What Ahmed Adds

Ahmed extends Merleau-Ponty's account of the body-subject in a specific direction. For Merleau-Ponty, the body is oriented toward its tasks and possibilities (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, pt. 1, ch. 3). For Ahmed, those possibilities are themselves distributed along social lines. What a body "can" do depends on what's been placed within its reach. The same analysis, she argues, applies to race: whiteness can function as an orientation that puts certain things within reach of some bodies and not others.

Conclusion

For Ahmed, orientation is how bodies are directed toward some objects and away from others. It's sustained by lines, which are made by being followed and which shape the bodies that follow them. Queerness is both a sexual orientation and being off line, and disorientation can reveal the lines that orientation hides.

Key Terms

  • Orientation: In Ahmed, the way bodies are directed toward some objects and away from others, which shapes what is within their reach.
  • Line: In Ahmed, a path that bodies follow, often repeated and inherited, that directs them toward some objects and makes others harder to reach.
  • Queer: In Ahmed, both non-heterosexual desire and life and, more broadly, what is oblique or off line: out of alignment with the lines one is expected to follow.
  • Disorientation: In Ahmed, the experience of the world failing to line up with one's body, which can unsettle and reveal the lines one had been following.

Critical Phenomenology: Quasi-Transcendental Structures and Their Debates

Introduction

Fanon, Young, and Ahmed wrote decades apart and in different fields. Since the 2010s, their work has been gathered under a single name: critical phenomenology. The name marks a research program, and it raises a question about phenomenology itself.

This reading explains what critical phenomenology attends to, compares the three thinkers, and sets out the debate over whether the field completes or breaks with classical phenomenology.

What Critical Phenomenology Attends To

Classical phenomenology looks for the structures that make experience possible: intentionality, time-consciousness, the lived body, the lifeworld. Husserl called these transcendental structures, because they are conditions of any experience at all (Husserl [1913] 2014).

Critical phenomenology adds another layer. Lisa Guenther describes it as attending to quasi-transcendental structures: in critical phenomenology, social structures such as patriarchy, white supremacy, and heteronormativity that are not conditions of all possible experience but that shape experience so deeply they function like its conditions (Guenther 2019). They're "quasi" because they're historical and contingent. They could be otherwise. But for those who live within them, they structure perception and action from the start.

On this basis, critical phenomenology is a phenomenological method that describes lived experience while also examining how contingent social structures shape it, with a view to changing those structures. Guenther stresses the last clause. Critical phenomenology isn't only descriptive. It's also a practice aimed at restructuring the world, so that new possibilities for experience become available.

Completion or Break?

The field's relation to classical phenomenology is debated (Weiss, Murphy, and Salamon 2019).

  1. Critical phenomenology as completion. On one view, it carries out commitments phenomenology already had. Phenomenology has always suspended taken-for-granted assumptions to see how experience is structured. Critical phenomenology applies that suspension to assumptions about race, gender, and sexuality. Merleau-Ponty's own insistence that the body is situated, and that reflection can't fully escape its situation, points in this direction (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, Preface).
  2. Critical phenomenology as break. On another view, it breaks with the classical project. Classical phenomenology aimed at invariant, universal structures, described from the first-person singular. Critical phenomenology denies that such a starting point is neutral, and it treats contingent social structures as belonging to what phenomenology must describe. Some critics hold that this changes the method fundamentally.

The debate isn't settled. Many critical phenomenologists hold both positions at once: the field inherits phenomenology's methods while contesting its claims to universality.

Conclusion

Critical phenomenology attends to quasi-transcendental structures: contingent social structures that shape experience as deeply as its conditions. Fanon, Young, and Ahmed each use and extend Merleau-Ponty's body-subject. Whether the field completes classical phenomenology or breaks with it remains debated.

Key Terms

  • Quasi-transcendental structures: In critical phenomenology, social structures such as patriarchy, white supremacy, and heteronormativity that are not conditions of all possible experience but that shape experience so deeply they function like its conditions.
  • Critical phenomenology: A phenomenological method that describes lived experience while also examining how contingent social structures shape it, with a view to changing those structures.

Guided Close Reading: Inhibited Intentionality in Young's "Throwing Like a Girl"

Introduction

"Throwing Like a Girl" takes a concept from Merleau-Ponty and finds a variation on it that he didn't describe. The key passage is Young's account of inhibited intentionality. This reading works through it in four moves, pauses on Young's own later reflections on the essay, flags a common misreading, and ends with a model paraphrase and the essay's challenge to a general account of the body-subject.

Locating the Passage

The passage comes in the part of the essay where Young names the three modalities of feminine motility (Young [1980] 2005). Inhibited intentionality is the second, after ambiguous transcendence and before discontinuous unity.

No free version of the essay is available. The free Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry "Feminist Perspectives on the Body" summarizes the essay and its three modalities (Lennon and Fischer 2024, §8.1). Young's terms are quoted below as terms. Everything else is paraphrased.

Before you start, note the setting. Young has just described women's typical ways of throwing, lifting, and moving, and she has rejected a biological explanation for them. She now turns to Merleau-Ponty for a vocabulary to describe these patterns from within.

Walking Through the Argument

Step 1: Merleau-Ponty's "I can"

Young starts from Merleau-Ponty's account of the body as a field of possibilities (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, pt. 1, ch. 3). For Merleau-Ponty, consciousness is in the first place not an "I think" but an "I can." The body projects its possible actions onto the world. A cup appears as graspable, a stair as climbable. The world shows up as a field of things the body can do.

Notice what Young takes from this. The "I can" isn't a belief about one's abilities. It's how the world appears to a body that's ready to act. So if the "I can" is disturbed, the world itself appears differently.

Step 2: An "I can" undercut by an "I cannot"

The second move is Young's observation. In many women's movement, she finds an "I can" that is undercut, at the same moment, by an "I cannot." A woman approaching a heavy object may see it as liftable and also, at once, as too heavy for her. She reaches toward the task, but she holds back from committing her whole body to it.

Young calls this inhibited intentionality. The body's directedness toward its aim is real, but it's inhibited from within. And the "I cannot" is often self-imposed, in the sense that it isn't set by what the body can actually do. Women often underestimate their bodily capacities, Young notes, and then fail at tasks partly because of the hesitation.

The shift here is from a single "I can" to a split one. Merleau-Ponty's account had room for a body that can't do something. It had less room for a body that can and, at the same time, experiences itself as unable.

Step 3: The body as both subject and object for itself

The third move explains the split. The "I cannot" comes from living the body as an object as well as a subject. A woman who experiences her body as something looked at, fragile, or in need of protection doesn't simply act through it. She also watches it, guards it, and holds it back. Part of her attention is on the body as a thing, rather than on the task.

This is where Young draws on Beauvoir: the idea that a woman in a sexist society lives her body both as capacity and as object (Beauvoir [1949] 2010). Young's contribution is to locate that ambiguity in motor intentionality itself, in the way a throw or a lift is carried out.

Step 4: The cause is situation, not anatomy

The fourth move makes the essay's central claim. The source of inhibited intentionality isn't in women's bodies. It's in their situation. Girls are encouraged to be careful and discouraged from rough play. They get less practice using the whole body in physical tasks. They're looked at as bodies to be seen. Young's claim is that these conditions, not biology, produce a body that reaches out while holding back.

Young's Later Reflections

Young returned to the essay nearly twenty years later (Young 1998). She reconsidered its limits. Among them, she noted that the essay focused on constraint and inhibition, and gave less attention to the pleasures and capacities of women's bodily lives. She also noted that its generalizations were tied to a particular time and place, and that girls' participation in sport had changed since she wrote it. These reflections don't withdraw the essay's method. They situate its findings, which is itself a critical-phenomenological move.

A common misreading. The most common misreading takes the essay as a claim about biological difference: that women's bodies are naturally less capable.

  • Young rejects biological explanation. That's the point of her starting from Straus's observation and refusing his explanation (Straus [1952] 1966).
  • The modalities describe a situation, not a nature. They're produced by upbringing, practice, and objectifying looks.
  • The claim is typical, not universal. It describes common patterns in a particular society, not every woman or every movement.

Summary

The passage moves from Merleau-Ponty's "I can," through an "I can" undercut by an "I cannot," to a body lived as both subject and object, and finally to the claim that the source is situation, not anatomy. Here is a model paraphrase of the argument to check your reading against:

For Merleau-Ponty, the body meets the world as an "I can": a field of things it's ready to do. Young finds that, in many women's movement, this "I can" is undercut at the same moment by a self-imposed "I cannot." The body reaches toward its task but holds back from full commitment. She calls this inhibited intentionality. It arises because a woman in a sexist society lives her body both as a capacity and as an object to be looked at and protected. Its source is her situation, not her anatomy (Young [1980] 2005).

The essay's challenge to a general account of the body-subject follows directly. Merleau-Ponty's "I can" describes a body whose directedness toward the world isn't divided against itself. Young shows that this is one situation among others. A general account of motor intentionality has to allow that the "I can" is shaped, and sometimes inhibited, by the social world in which a body learns to move.
