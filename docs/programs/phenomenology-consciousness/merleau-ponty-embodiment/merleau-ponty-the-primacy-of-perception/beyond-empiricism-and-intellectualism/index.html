Primacy of Perception: Beyond Empiricism and Intellectualism

This lesson presents Merleau-Ponty's argument, in the Introduction to Phenomenology of Perception, that neither empiricism nor intellectualism can account for perception. Empiricism builds perception from sensations and associations; intellectualism explains it by attention and judgment. You'll be able to explain the four "classical prejudices" and the error both theories share: reading the finished, objective world back into perception. This critique still applies to theories of perception in cognitive science, so it gives you a lens for evaluating current models.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain Merleau-Ponty's critique of empiricism and intellectualism as accounts of perception

Bottom-Up, Top-Down, and Merleau-Ponty's Critique of Both

Theories of perception tend to fall into two families. One builds perception from the bottom up: the senses deliver simple features, such as edges, colors, and tones, and processing assembles them into objects. The other works from the top down: the mind brings hypotheses, expectations, or inferences to thin sensory input and settles on the best interpretation. Most current models combine the two, but they still take these as their basic ingredients: something delivered by the senses, and something done to it by the mind.

Consider seeing a friend's face across a crowded room. On the bottom-up picture, you receive patches of color and brightness and build them into a face. On the top-down picture, you infer, from sparse data, that the most likely cause is your friend. Neither matches the experience. You don't see patches and then assemble them, and you don't see evidence and then conclude. You see your friend, smiling, already recognized, with the room around her. The same is true of hearing a word spoken: you hear the word, not a sound that you then interpret as a word.

Predictive processing, an influential current framework, treats the brain as constantly predicting its own sensory input and correcting those predictions against incoming signals. Andy Clark's version stresses that prediction is tied to action and to agents situated in a world (Clark 2013). It's a sophisticated blend of the two families, and its defenders argue that it describes perception as active engagement rather than passive reception. Whether it escapes the families' shared assumptions is a live question, and one this critique helps you pose.

Maurice Merleau-Ponty's Phenomenology of Perception (1945) opens with a sustained attack on both families, in their classical forms. Empiricism builds perception from sensations and associations. Intellectualism explains it by attention and judgment. Merleau-Ponty argues that each fails, and that they fail for the same reason. Both take the finished world of objects, as science describes it, and read it back into perception as its starting point (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, Introduction).

That shared assumption is the target worth learning to see. Once you can identify it, you can ask of any model of perception, including today's, whether it starts from the world as perceived or from the world as already measured. The question doesn't settle which model is right. It clarifies what each model has assumed before it begins.

Sensation and Association: Merleau-Ponty's Critique of Empiricism

Introduction

Empiricism seems the most modest theory of perception: we receive sensations, and experience links them together. Merleau-Ponty argues that neither step describes anything we actually perceive.

This reading explains his critique of pure sensation and of association and the projection of memories, the first two of the Introduction's "classical prejudices." Citations to Phenomenology of Perception give the part and chapter. Landes's translation prints the French page numbers, so learners using Colin Smith's older translation can cross-reference (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2002).

The Myth of Pure Sensation

Empiricism, as Merleau-Ponty uses the term, is the view that perception is built up from elementary sensations, joined by association and memory. Its basic unit is sensation: in empiricism, an elementary, contentless unit of experience, a quality felt at a point, produced by a local stimulus.

Merleau-Ponty's first claim is that nothing like this is ever given (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, Introduction, ch. 1). Take the simplest perception available: a white patch on a uniform background. Even here, the patch isn't an isolated quality. Its contour belongs to it, not to the background. The background continues beneath it. The patch is a figure on a ground. The simplest perceptual datum already has a structure, and a meaning: something standing out from something else. A pure sensation, with no field around it, is never perceived. It's a theoretical construction.

Empiricism supports its sensations with the constancy hypothesis: the assumption that each local stimulus produces a fixed, corresponding sensation, whatever its context. Merleau-Ponty argues, following the Gestalt psychologists, that perception constantly violates it. The same gray looks lighter on a dark ground and darker on a light one. When experience contradicts the hypothesis, empiricism calls the experience an error. The theory decides in advance what perception must contain.

The Müller-Lyer illusion shows the problem sharply. Two lines of equal length, one with outward-pointing fins and one with inward-pointing fins, look unequal. For empiricism, the lines are equal, so the sensations must be equal, and perception adds a mistake. Merleau-Ponty's alternative is that, as perceived, the lines are neither equal nor unequal. They belong to different figures and aren't simply comparable. This is indeterminacy: in Merleau-Ponty, a positive feature of perception, by which the perceived can be neither this nor that, rather than fully determinate. The lesson's close reading works through this example step by step.

Association and the Projection of Memories

Empiricism's second step joins sensations together. Association is the empiricist principle that sensations are linked by contiguity or resemblance, so that one calls up another. When association fails to explain what we see, empiricism adds the projection of memories: the claim that past experiences fill in and complete what the present sensations lack (Introduction, ch. 2).

Merleau-Ponty argues that both presuppose what they're meant to explain. Association links sensations that "go together." But which ones go together depends on the whole they belong to, which association was supposed to produce. And memory can't supply the meaning of the present. To call up the right memory, the present perception must already be organized enough to resemble it. The recalled past can enrich a perception, but it can't be its source.

His example is a walk along a beach toward a ship run aground. At first, its masts and funnel merge with the trees of the forest behind it. Then, suddenly, the scene reorganizes, and the ship appears. Nothing new reached the eyes, and no memory explains the change. What changed was the organization of the field.

Conclusion

Empiricism builds perception from sensations joined by association. Merleau-Ponty argues that pure sensations are never perceived: even the simplest datum is a figure on a ground, and the constancy hypothesis turns experience into error. Association and memory presuppose the organized perception they're meant to explain. What they miss includes perception's own indeterminacy.

Key Terms

  • Empiricism: In Merleau-Ponty's usage, the view that perception is built up from elementary sensations, joined by association and memory.
  • Sensation: In empiricism, an elementary, contentless unit of experience: a quality felt at a point, produced by a local stimulus.
  • Constancy hypothesis: The assumption that each local stimulus produces a fixed, corresponding sensation, whatever its context.
  • Indeterminacy: In Merleau-Ponty, a positive feature of perception, by which the perceived can be neither this nor that, rather than fully determinate.
  • Association: The empiricist principle that sensations are linked by contiguity or resemblance, so that one calls up another.
  • Projection of memories: The empiricist claim that past experiences fill in and complete what present sensations lack.

Attention, Judgment, and the Prejudice of the World

Introduction

If sensations can't explain perception, perhaps the mind does: by attending to what's there, or by judging what it must be. Merleau-Ponty argues that this intellectualist answer fails too, and that it fails for the same reason as empiricism.

This reading explains his critique of attention and judgment, and the shared error he calls the prejudice of the world. Citations to Phenomenology of Perception give the part and chapter.

Intellectualism

Intellectualism, as Merleau-Ponty uses the term, is the view that perception is the work of a mind that attends, judges, and constitutes objects, so that perceiving is a form of thinking. It descends from Descartes and, in France, from teachers such as Alain (Descartes [1641] 2017; Alain 1941). Where empiricism has too little mind in perception, intellectualism has too much.

Attention

Both theories need a concept of attention, and Merleau-Ponty argues that both make it powerless (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, Introduction, ch. 3).

  • For empiricism, attention is like a searchlight. It lights up sensations that were already there, fully formed, in the dark. It adds nothing.
  • For intellectualism, attention reveals what consciousness already contained. The object was already fully determinate in thought, and attention only makes it explicit. It adds nothing either.

In both, attention, as the theories conceive it, uncovers a content that was already complete. Merleau-Ponty's alternative is that attention transforms the field. When you attend to a vague shape at the edge of vision, it doesn't simply get brighter. It takes on a new structure, as a new figure emerges from what was ground. Attention creates a determinate object where there was an indeterminate zone.

Judgment

Intellectualism's central claim is that perceiving is judging. Descartes's example is looking from a window at people passing in the street. Strictly, he says, he sees only hats and coats, and judges that there are people beneath them (Descartes [1641] 2017, AT 7:32). Judgment, in the intellectualist account, is the act by which the mind interprets sensory signs and concludes what the object is.

Merleau-Ponty objects that this loses the perceived world. First, if perception were judgment, illusions would be errors of judgment, and knowing the truth would correct them. But the Müller-Lyer lines still look unequal after you've measured them. Second, judgment has to work on something, and the intellectualist is left with the same empiricist sensations as raw material. Third, perception has its own kind of sense, before any judgment. We see people in the street, not hats that we then interpret.

The Shared Error

Here Merleau-Ponty draws his central conclusion (Introduction, ch. 4). Empiricism and intellectualism look like opposites. Yet they share a premise. Both begin from the world as science describes it: fully determinate, made of objects with measurable properties, laid out in objective space. Empiricism puts that world into the stimuli and sensations. Intellectualism puts it into thought. Neither describes the world as it's actually perceived.

Merleau-Ponty calls this premise the prejudice of the world: the assumption that the determinate, objective world of science is given first, and can be read back into perception as its starting point. It is, in Toadvine's summary, the error of explaining perception by its own finished results (Toadvine 2023, §3).

The four classical prejudices can be set out in a table.

PrejudiceThe theory's claimAn everyday example that resists it
SensationPerception is built from simple, point-like qualitiesA patch of white is already a figure on a ground
Association and memoryLinked sensations and past experiences supply meaningA beached ship "appears" suddenly from among the trees
AttentionAttention lights up contents already thereA vague shape takes a new form when attended to
JudgmentPerceiving is inferring objects from signsThe Müller-Lyer lines still look unequal after measuring

What Merleau-Ponty recommends is a return to the phenomenal field: the field of experience as it's actually lived, before it's recast in terms of objective sensations or acts of thought.

Conclusion

Intellectualism explains perception by attention and judgment. Merleau-Ponty argues that both theories make attention powerless, and that the judgment view can't explain why illusions persist or what perception already means. Empiricism and intellectualism share the prejudice of the world: they read the determinate world of science back into perception. His alternative is a return to the phenomenal field.

Key Terms

  • Intellectualism: In Merleau-Ponty's usage, the view that perception is the work of a mind that attends, judges, and constitutes objects, so that perceiving is a form of thinking.
  • Attention: As the classical theories conceive it, an act that uncovers a content already complete, whether in sensation or in thought.
  • Prejudice of the world: Merleau-Ponty's name for the assumption that the determinate, objective world of science is given first and can be read back into perception as its starting point.
  • Phenomenal field: In Merleau-Ponty, the field of experience as it's actually lived, before it's recast in terms of objective sensations or acts of thought.

Merleau-Ponty's Sources: Husserl, Gestalt Psychology, and The Structure of Behavior

Introduction

Phenomenology of Perception reads as if written against scientific psychology, yet it's full of experiments and clinical cases (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012). Merleau-Ponty didn't reject science. He read it critically, for what it showed despite its own theories.

This reading places the book among its sources: Husserl's late manuscripts, Merleau-Ponty's first book, The Structure of Behavior, and the Gestalt and neurological research he drew on.

Husserl's Manuscripts and the War

Merleau-Ponty was among the first French philosophers to read Husserl's unpublished late manuscripts. In 1939 he visited the newly founded Husserl Archives at Louvain, where he read material that included the second book of Ideas and parts of the Crisis (Husserl [1952] 1989; Husserl [1936] 1970; Toadvine 2023, §1). The late Husserl's themes, including the lived body, the lifeworld, and a pre-reflective layer of experience, run through Merleau-Ponty's own work.

He wrote Phenomenology of Perception largely during the German occupation of France, and it was published in 1945, the year after Paris was liberated.

The Structure of Behavior

His first book, The Structure of Behavior (1942), approaches the same problems from the side of science (Merleau-Ponty [1942] 1963). Its target is the reflex model of behavior, in which an organism responds to stimuli with fixed, local reactions.

Merleau-Ponty argues instead for behavior as form: the view that an organism's behavior is an organized whole, responding to meaningful situations rather than to isolated stimuli. He distinguishes three orders of form (ch. 3).

  1. The physical order is the level of physical systems, which tend toward equilibrium under external conditions, like a soap bubble or an electrical field.
  2. The vital order is the level of living organisms, which actively maintain themselves in an environment of their own, responding to what matters for their life.
  3. The human order is the level of human behavior, which responds to symbolic and cultural meanings and can take up possibilities beyond any present situation.

Each order integrates the one below it without reducing to it. The book ends by asking how consciousness relates to these forms, and it points toward a phenomenology of perception to answer.

Science Read Critically

Merleau-Ponty's use of science follows a pattern. He takes experimental findings seriously, and then shows that they undermine the theories used to explain them.

  • Gestalt psychology supplied the evidence that perception is organized into figures on grounds, and that the constancy hypothesis fails (Wagemans et al. 2012). But he criticized the Gestalt psychologists for still explaining these structures by physiological processes, in effect returning to a causal theory.
  • Neurology, especially Gelb and Goldstein's studies of brain-injured patients, supplied cases in which perception and movement came apart in revealing ways (Goldstein [1934] 1995). He drew on Goldstein's holism while reinterpreting its cases.

The point is methodological. Phenomenology, for Merleau-Ponty, doesn't compete with science by offering rival causal explanations. It describes the phenomena that scientific explanations must account for, and it notices when an explanation has quietly replaced the phenomenon with a theory.

Conclusion

Merleau-Ponty drew on Husserl's late manuscripts, read at Louvain in 1939, and wrote Phenomenology of Perception during the war. His earlier The Structure of Behavior treats behavior as form, in physical, vital, and human orders. He used Gestalt psychology and neurology critically, taking their findings while rejecting their causal theories.

Key Terms

  • Behavior as form: In Merleau-Ponty's The Structure of Behavior, the view that an organism's behavior is an organized whole, responding to meaningful situations rather than to isolated stimuli.
  • Physical order: In The Structure of Behavior, the level of physical systems, which tend toward equilibrium under external conditions.
  • Vital order: In The Structure of Behavior, the level of living organisms, which actively maintain themselves in an environment of their own, responding to what matters for their life.
  • Human order: In The Structure of Behavior, the level of human behavior, which responds to symbolic and cultural meanings and can take up possibilities beyond any present situation.

Empiricism, Intellectualism, and Merleau-Ponty Compared

Introduction

Merleau-Ponty's critique is easy to misread as a search for a middle ground between empiricism and intellectualism. It isn't. He rejects the premise they share, and so his own position doesn't sit on the line between them.

This reading compares the three positions directly, gathers the core terms, notes a translation difference, and points to an accessible entry into Merleau-Ponty's thought.

Three Positions Compared

EmpiricismIntellectualismMerleau-Ponty
Unit of perceptionSensations, each tied to a local stimulusJudgments about sensory signsA figure on a ground, in a field
Role of mindPassive: receives and associatesActive: attends, judges, constitutesNeither: perception has its own sense, lived by a bodily subject
Status of the worldAlready there, causing sensationsAlready there, as the object of thoughtEmerging in perception, never fully determinate
The errorReads the objective world into sensationsReads the objective world into thoughtBoth share the prejudice of the world

The third column doesn't average the other two. It changes the starting point. For Merleau-Ponty, perception is our most basic contact with a world that's meaningful before we think about it, and that contact isn't built from sensations or produced by judgments (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, Introduction).

A Translation Note

The two English translations title the Introduction differently. Donald Landes's translation calls the prejudices "Classical Prejudices" (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012). Colin Smith's older translation calls them "Traditional Prejudices" (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2002). The French is préjugés classiques. Landes's rendering is closer to the French, but both are common in the secondary literature. Smith's translation, first published in 1962, is also the source of a known confusion over the term "body schema," which he sometimes rendered as "body image."

An Accessible Entry Point

Phenomenology of Perception is long and dense. Merleau-Ponty also gave a series of short radio lectures in 1948, published in English as The World of Perception (Merleau-Ponty [1948] 2008). In plain language, they cover the rediscovery of the perceived world, space, the things of perception, animal life, and the relation between perception and art. They make a useful first reading, or a companion to the harder book.

Conclusion

Empiricism and intellectualism differ over the unit of perception and the role of mind, but share the prejudice of the world. Merleau-Ponty rejects that premise rather than splitting the difference. Landes's "Classical Prejudices" and Smith's "Traditional Prejudices" name the same section. The World of Perception offers a short, accessible way in.

Guided Close Reading: The Müller-Lyer Illusion in Phenomenology of Perception

Introduction

Merleau-Ponty's treatment of the Müller-Lyer illusion takes up only a few sentences of the Introduction to Phenomenology of Perception. Yet it contains his whole critique in miniature: one illusion, three theories, and a claim that sounds almost absurd, that the lines are neither equal nor unequal. This reading works through the passage move by move, and ends with a model paraphrase of the argument and its target.

Locating the Passage

The passage is in the first chapter of the Introduction, "Sensation" (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, Introduction, ch. 1). Landes's translation prints the French page numbers in the margin, so learners using Colin Smith's translation can follow along (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2002). Key phrases below are given in French, with a note on the English under Key Considerations.

No free English translation is available. The free Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Merleau-Ponty summarizes the Introduction's critique (Toadvine 2023, §3).

The figure itself is simple. Two horizontal lines of equal length each have short fins at their ends. On one, the fins point outward, like tails. On the other, they point inward, like arrowheads:

>———————<
<———————>

Most people see the first line as longer, even after measuring them.

Before you start, note the setting. Merleau-Ponty is attacking the idea of pure sensation, and the constancy hypothesis that supports it. The illusion is his test case.

Walking Through the Argument

Step 1: The empiricist says the lines are equal, and perception errs

For empiricism, what reaches the eye determines the sensation. The two lines project equal lengths onto the retina, so the sensations of length must be equal. If the lines nonetheless look unequal, something has been added to the sensations: an error, due perhaps to inattention or a habit of association.

Notice what this does. The theory tells us what we "really" sense before we've looked. When experience disagrees, experience is declared mistaken. The constancy hypothesis is protected by treating perception itself as the anomaly.

Step 2: The intellectualist says a judgment corrects it

Intellectualism reverses the direction but keeps the frame. Perception, for the intellectualist, is judgment. We see unequal lines because we've judged them unequal, misled by the fins. Once we measure and judge correctly, the error is corrected.

Merleau-Ponty's objection is that the correction doesn't reach perception. After you've measured the lines, you know they're equal, and they still look unequal. If perceiving were judging, the new judgment would change the perception. It doesn't. So perception isn't a judgment, or at least not one that knowledge can overrule.

Step 3: In perception, the lines belong to different contexts and aren't comparable

Now Merleau-Ponty offers his own description. As perceived, the lines are ni égales ni inégales: neither equal nor unequal. The question of whether they're equal arises only in the objective world, where lengths are measured against a common standard.

Here's why. In perception, each line isn't an isolated length. It belongs to a figure, with its fins. One line is part of an expanding shape, and the other is part of a contracting shape. The perceived lines are moments of two different wholes. They haven't been placed side by side on a single scale, so the demand that they be either equal or unequal doesn't apply.

That's not a failure of perception. It's what perception is like before measurement. Perception presents things within contexts, and comparison across contexts requires a further, objectifying step.

Indeterminacy as a Positive Feature

The passage's lasting contribution is the concept of indeterminacy. Both classical theories assume that the perceived world is fully determinate: every length is some exact length, and every color some exact shade. Where perception doesn't deliver that, they call it error or vagueness.

Merleau-Ponty argues that indeterminacy is a positive feature of perception, and must be described as such. We perceive things that are neither this nor that: an expression that's neither quite a smile nor quite a frown, a color at the edge of the visual field that has no definite shade. The perceived world contains what he calls ambiguity and indeterminacy, and a phenomenology that forced it into determinate categories would falsify it.

A common misreading. The most common misreading takes Merleau-Ponty to deny that the lines measure the same. That misses his point.

  • He accepts the measurement. In the objective world, with a ruler, the lines are equal. He doesn't dispute it.
  • He denies that the measurement describes the perception. The question "equal or unequal?" belongs to the measured world, not to the perceived one.
  • He isn't calling perception right and measurement wrong. He's saying they answer different questions, and that treating perception as a failed measurement misdescribes it.

Summary

The passage moves from the empiricist's "equal, and perception errs," through the intellectualist's "a judgment corrects it," to Merleau-Ponty's "neither equal nor unequal." Here is a model paraphrase to check your reading against:

The empiricist says the Müller-Lyer lines produce equal sensations, so seeing them as unequal is an error added to perception. The intellectualist says we judge them unequal and can correct the judgment, yet the lines still look unequal after we measure them. Merleau-Ponty says that, as perceived, the lines are neither equal nor unequal. Each belongs to a different figure, and comparing them on one scale is a question that arises only in the objective world of measurement. Both classical theories assume that world from the start. Perception's indeterminacy is a positive feature to be described, not an error to be explained away (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, Introduction, ch. 1).

The argument's target. The passage is aimed at the prejudice of the world: the assumption, shared by empiricism and intellectualism, that the determinate world of measurement is given first and can be read back into perception.

Claude, please facilitate a reflective dialogue with me. I'm a graduate-level learner studying Merleau-Ponty's critique of empiricism and intellectualism. Follow this guidance for the whole conversation.

GOAL
Help me explain Merleau-Ponty's critique of empiricism and intellectualism by identifying empiricist and intellectualist assumptions in my own account of something I perceive, and by describing an indeterminate feature of the perception.

HOW TO RUN THE CONVERSATION
- Ask one question at a time, then wait for my reply. Keep each of your turns under about 120 words.
- Don't lecture. Explain a point only when I need it to continue, then return to my perception.
- Be patient, curious, and collegial. Encourage description before explanation. Define terms of art briefly on first use.
- Plain conversation only: no web search, files, or artifacts.
- Aim for about 12 minutes. If I struggle to find indeterminacy, suggest looking at something in peripheral vision without moving my eyes. If I seem uncertain, shorten the conversation to 5-7 minutes. Always reach topic 4.
- Start now. Open with one or two warm sentences: this is an exploratory conversation, not a test; we'll look closely at an ordinary perception; I can ask you to explain any term. Then ask me to choose something across the room.

TOPICS, IN ORDER
1. Describe, then explain. Ask me to look at something across the room and describe what I see. Then ask me to explain how I see it, in the way a textbook would. Let me give both. Point out the gap between the description (a mug, on a shelf, in the light) and the explanation (light, retina, signals, processing).
2. Sorting the explanation. Ask which parts of my explanation build perception from sensations, and which parts treat it as a judgment. Help me find the empiricist elements (points of light, features, sensations combined) and the intellectualist ones (the brain infers, hypothesizes, or concludes). Then introduce the shared error: both start from the objective world as science describes it.
3. Looking for indeterminacy. Ask whether there is anything in my perception that is indeterminate, neither one thing nor another, until I look closer. Guide me to the edges of the visual field, a shadow, a distant object, or an ambiguous expression. Introduce indeterminacy as a positive feature of perception, not a defect.
4. Closing reflection. Ask which prejudice I catch myself relying on most. Help me name one: sensation, association, attention, or judgment.

POSITIONS TO KEEP ACCURATE
- Empiricism builds perception from sensations joined by association and memory, backed by the constancy hypothesis.
- Intellectualism treats perceiving as attending and judging.
- Both make attention powerless: it uncovers contents already complete.
- Both share the prejudice of the world: they read the determinate world of science back into perception.
- Even the simplest perception is a figure on a ground.
- Indeterminacy is a positive feature of perception, as in the Müller-Lyer lines, which are neither equal nor unequal as perceived.
- Merleau-Ponty reads science critically. He doesn't reject it.

MISREADINGS TO CORRECT GENTLY
Keep every point tied to my own perception. When a misreading appears, name the accurate position briefly, then return to my experience.
- Merleau-Ponty as "somewhere in between" the two theories. He rejects their shared premise rather than combining them.
- Merleau-Ponty as anti-science. He uses experimental findings extensively, while rejecting explanations that replace the phenomenon with a theory.
- Indeterminacy as vagueness or error. It's a positive feature of how things are perceived.
- Denying that the Müller-Lyer lines are equal. He accepts the measurement, but holds that it answers a different question.
- Current models as automatically guilty. Encourage me to test a model for the shared assumption, rather than assuming it's there.

TO FINISH
After I name my prejudice, close in one short turn:
- Affirm one precise observation I made, in my own words where possible.
- Suggest one or two next steps that fit how the conversation went. Possible steps: analyze a perception through all four prejudices (sensation, association, attention, judgment); test a current model of perception, such as predictive processing, for the shared assumption; explore how Merleau-Ponty uses Gestalt findings while rejecting Gestalt explanations; review what he says about attention, judgment, and the prejudice of the world; return to the Müller-Lyer lines and paraphrase why they are neither equal nor unequal as perceived; review what empiricism, intellectualism, and the prejudice of the world mean; retry this conversation, describing the perception before explaining it.
- Restate the prejudice I named on its own line, labeled "Journal note," so I can copy it as the focus for a journal entry.

Overview

If you don't keep a phenomenology journal yet, start one now; any notebook or document works. This activity is optional, and your entry is for your own development.

You'll write a 300–500 word entry. The entry applies Merleau-Ponty's critique of empiricism and intellectualism to one of your own perceptions.

Writing Prompt

In 300–500 words, describe one perception, give an empiricist and an intellectualist explanation of it, explain what each misses according to Merleau-Ponty, and describe one indeterminate feature of the perception as you lived it.

Steps

  1. Describe a perception. Choose one perception. Illusions, faces, and blurry or distant objects make good examples. Describe what you saw or heard as you lived it.

  3. Explain what each misses. Include the error they share.
  4. Describe one indeterminate feature of the perception as lived. Name something that was neither one thing nor another.

  • Gives an intellectualist explanation that treats perception as judgment or inference

  • Describes an indeterminate feature concretely
