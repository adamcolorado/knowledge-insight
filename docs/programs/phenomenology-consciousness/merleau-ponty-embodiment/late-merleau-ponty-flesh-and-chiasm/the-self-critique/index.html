Merleau-Ponty's Self-Critique and the Revision of Research Foundations

Philosophers rarely say that their most influential book rests on a mistake. Maurice Merleau-Ponty did. In private notes written in the last years of his life, he judged that the problems of Phenomenology of Perception couldn't be solved as the book had posed them (Merleau-Ponty [1964] 1968, Working Notes, July 1959). The book, he wrote, still started from the distinction between consciousness and its object, the very distinction it had set out to overcome.

He didn't live to work this criticism out in full. Merleau-Ponty died suddenly in May 1961, at 53 (Toadvine 2023, §1). He left an unfinished manuscript and a set of working notes, which were published after his death as The Visible and the Invisible. The notes record a philosopher revising his own foundations while the revision was still under way.

The intellectual stake is a question any account of experience faces. Can a theory that begins with "the subject" avoid dividing the world into a subject on one side and objects on the other? Phenomenology of Perception had replaced the mind with the lived body, and it had described perception as a bodily engagement with the world rather than a mental representation of it (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012). Yet by its author's later judgment, it still took consciousness as the place where the world appears. Replacing a mind with a body-subject may leave the subject–object structure in place.

The same question runs through current work on embodied cognition. Accounts that describe cognition as embodied, embedded, or enactive often present themselves as alternatives to dualism. Critics ask whether some of them still assume an inner subject, now located in the body or the brain rather than the mind, which then relates to an outer world. Whether a framework has really left dualism behind depends on what it takes as its starting point, not on what it says about the body.

This is what Merleau-Ponty's self-critique lets a researcher do. It gives you a way to identify a framework's hidden starting assumption: the distinction it takes for granted before it begins to describe anything. And it models a harder discipline, which is applying that test to your own work. Merleau-Ponty found the assumption in a book that had already changed the field. The lesson isn't that the book was worthless. He kept most of its findings. The lesson is that a research program's results and its starting point can come apart, and that revising the starting point can be the way to keep the results.

The Consciousness–Object Starting Point and the Tacit Cogito

Introduction

Phenomenology of Perception argued against dualism at every turn (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012). So it's surprising to find its author concluding that the book was still dualist in its starting point.

This reading explains the self-critique and the problem of the tacit cogito, and places them on a timeline of Merleau-Ponty's work. Citations to The Visible and the Invisible give the chapter, or "Working Notes" with the note's date.

The Self-Critique

Merleau-Ponty's self-critique is his late judgment that the problems of Phenomenology of Perception are insoluble because it starts from the consciousness–object distinction. In a working note from 1959, he writes that the problems posed in Phenomenology of Perception are insoluble, because the book starts from the distinction between "consciousness" and "object" (Merleau-Ponty [1964] 1968, Working Notes, July 1959).

The charge needs careful reading. The earlier book didn't claim that consciousness and object are separate substances. It argued that the perceiving body and the perceived world are bound together. The problem lies in the order of explanation. The book begins with a perceiving subject, even an embodied one, and then describes how the world appears to it. Once that starting point is set, the world is always the world for a consciousness. Every later attempt to show that subject and world belong together has to bridge a gap the starting point already opened.

That's the core of what Merleau-Ponty came to call a philosophy of consciousness: any philosophy that takes consciousness, or a subject for whom things appear, as its starting point, and treats the world as what appears to it. On his later view, even a phenomenology of the body-subject can be a philosophy of consciousness in this sense.

The Tacit Cogito

The clearest symptom is the tacit cogito. In Phenomenology of Perception, Merleau-Ponty had criticized Descartes's cogito as a late, reflective achievement (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, pt. 3, ch. 1). The cogito is Latin for "I think." It names Descartes's finding that "I am, I exist" is necessarily true whenever he thinks it (Descartes [1641] 2017, AT 7:25). But he argued that beneath it lay a tacit cogito: in the earlier Merleau-Ponty, a silent, pre-reflective presence of the self to itself, underlying every explicit act of reflection and speech.

In a working note, he questions this. The explicit cogito works with words and meanings. The tacit cogito was supposed to be a contact of the self with itself before any words. He now asks how a self-presence without language could ever be known or said, and he concludes that what he had called the tacit cogito is impossible (Merleau-Ponty [1964] 1968, Working Notes, January 1959). The tacit cogito had kept a silent subject at the base of experience. Removing it removes the last foundation of the philosophy of consciousness.

Conclusion

Merleau-Ponty judged that Phenomenology of Perception couldn't solve its problems because it started from the consciousness–object distinction. That made it, in his later terms, a philosophy of consciousness. The tacit cogito, a silent self-presence beneath reflection, was the symptom he singled out, and he came to judge it impossible. The shift here is from correcting the earlier book's claims to replacing its starting point.

  • Tacit cogito: In the earlier Merleau-Ponty, a silent, pre-reflective presence of the self to itself, underlying every explicit act of reflection and speech.

Beyond Reflection, Intuition, and Dialectic: Hyper-Reflection and Wild Being

Introduction

If the old starting point fails, what replaces it? The first three chapters of The Visible and the Invisible approach the question by testing three philosophical methods and finding each one wanting.

This reading summarizes the critiques of reflection, intuition, and dialectic, then introduces hyper-reflection and wild being. Citations give the chapter.

Three Methods Examined

Each chapter examines a method that promises to get back to the world as it is, and each finds that the method loses the world it seeks.

  1. Reflection (ch. 1). A philosophy of reflection, in Merleau-Ponty, is one that seeks the ground of experience by turning back from the world to the consciousness that thinks it, as in Descartes, Kant, and some of Husserl (Merleau-Ponty [1964] 1968, ch. 1; Descartes [1641] 2017; Kant [1781/1787] 1998; Husserl [1913] 2014). Its problem is that it forgets where it started. Reflection begins inside a world already perceived, then treats that world as the product of the consciousness that reflects on it. It can't account for its own origin in unreflected experience.
  2. Dialectic (ch. 2). Merleau-Ponty examines a philosophy of negativity, chiefly Sartre's opposition of being and nothingness (Sartre [1943] 2018). It promises to overcome dualism by setting a pure nothingness, consciousness, against pure being. But Merleau-Ponty argues that the opposition is itself a dualism. It sees the world from above, as a whole laid out for a thought that isn't part of it. He calls for a better dialectic, which he names hyperdialectic: one that doesn't settle its oppositions in advance.
  3. Intuition (ch. 3). He examines philosophies that seek direct contact with things, whether through a grasp of essences, as in Husserl (Husserl [1913] 2014), or through coincidence with the flow of life, as in Bergson (Bergson [1907] 1911). Both aim at a fusion with the thing. Merleau-Ponty argues that we never coincide with what we perceive. There is always a gap, and at most a partial coincidence.

Hyper-Reflection

None of these methods can be dropped. We can't stop reflecting. What Merleau-Ponty proposes is hyper-reflection (surréflexion): in Merleau-Ponty, a reflection that takes account of its own roots in unreflected experience and of the changes it makes to what it reflects on.

The difference is one of self-awareness. Ordinary reflection presents its results as what was there all along. Hyper-reflection keeps in view that it began in a perceived world it can't fully capture, and that turning back on experience alters it. It doesn't escape the world to describe it from outside. It describes from within, knowing it does.

Wild Being

Hyper-reflection aims at what Merleau-Ponty calls wild being (être sauvage), also called brute being: in Merleau-Ponty, being as it is encountered before it is divided into subject and object, or into consciousness and thing. Wild being isn't a hidden world behind the perceived one. It's the perceived world itself, taken before philosophy has sorted it into a subject that knows and an object that is known.

This is the turn to ontology: the study of being, of what it is for something to be at all. The earlier book described how the world appears to a perceiving body. The late work asks what being must be like, if perceiver and perceived belong to it together.

Conclusion

Merleau-Ponty finds that reflection forgets its origin, dialectic sees from above, and intuition seeks a coincidence that never comes. In their place, hyper-reflection takes account of its roots and its effects, and it aims at wild being, prior to the subject–object split. The result is a turn from phenomenology of perception to ontology.

Key Terms

  • Philosophy of reflection: In Merleau-Ponty, a philosophy that seeks the ground of experience by turning back from the world to the consciousness that thinks it.
  • Hyper-reflection (surréflexion): In Merleau-Ponty, a reflection that takes account of its own roots in unreflected experience and of the changes it makes to what it reflects on.
  • Wild being (être sauvage): In Merleau-Ponty, being as it is encountered before it is divided into subject and object, or into consciousness and thing. Also called brute being.
  • Ontology: The study of being: what it is for something to be at all.

Reading The Visible and the Invisible: Manuscript and Working Notes

Introduction

The Visible and the Invisible isn't a book Merleau-Ponty finished. How you read it depends on knowing what it is: a partial manuscript and a set of private notes, assembled by an editor after his death.

This reading describes the book's state, explains how to read the working notes, and compares it with Phenomenology of Perception. Citations give the chapter, or "Working Notes" with the note's date.

The Book's State

When Merleau-Ponty died in 1961, he left a manuscript of about 150 pages under the title The Visible and the Invisible, along with working notes written from early 1959 to early 1961 (Merleau-Ponty [1964] 1968). His friend and former student Claude Lefort edited them for publication in 1964. Alphonso Lingis's English translation followed in 1968.

The published book has four chapters, an appendix, and the working notes. The first three chapters examine reflection, dialectic, and intuition. The fourth, "The Intertwining—The Chiasm," is the most developed statement of the new ontology. The manuscript was meant to open a much larger work, whose plan survives only in outline.

The editorial history matters mainly as a caution. The order of the published text, and the selection of notes, are Lefort's. Scholars have debated some of his decisions, and the details of that history are beyond the scope of this reading.

How to Read the Working Notes

The working notes are the private, dated notes Merleau-Ponty wrote while drafting the manuscript, published with it after his death. They're among the most cited parts of the book, and among the hardest to use. Three features call for care.

  1. They're provisional. Merleau-Ponty wrote them to think, not to publish. A note may try out a position he later dropped.
  2. They're compressed. Many notes are lists of phrases, questions, and abbreviations, such as "Ph.P." for Phenomenology of Perception. What they leave out has to be supplied from context.
  3. They're sometimes inconsistent. Different notes pull in different directions, and no note settles the question on its own.

The practical rule follows from these features. Read a note alongside other notes on the same theme and alongside the finished chapters, and treat a single note as evidence of a line of thought, not as a final doctrine.

The tacit cogito, in the earlier Merleau-Ponty, is a silent, pre-reflective presence of the self to itself, underlying every explicit act of reflection and speech (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, pt. 3, ch. 1). Conclusion

The Visible and the Invisible is an unfinished manuscript and a set of working notes, edited by Claude Lefort and published in 1964. The notes are provisional, compressed, and sometimes inconsistent, so they should be read together and against the chapters. Compared with the earlier book, the late work changes its starting point from the body-subject to wild being.

Continuity or Rupture: Dillon and Barbaras on the Late Ontology

Introduction

Because The Visible and the Invisible is unfinished, readers have to decide how it relates to the earlier work. Is it a development of Phenomenology of Perception (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012) or a break with it? The question divides Merleau-Ponty scholarship.

This reading sets out the two main positions and two representative interpreters, M. C. Dillon and Renaud Barbaras, in one comparative passage.

Two Positions

A continuity reading holds that the late ontology develops Phenomenology of Perception rather than breaking with it: the earlier book's findings are kept and given an ontological basis. A rupture reading holds that the late ontology breaks with the earlier work's starting point, so that its concepts can't be read as extensions of the earlier ones.

Each position can cite Merleau-Ponty himself. The working notes criticize the earlier book sharply, which supports rupture (Merleau-Ponty [1964] 1968, Working Notes, July 1959). But other notes speak of bringing the earlier results to ontological explicitation, which supports continuity (Merleau-Ponty [1964] 1968, Working Notes, February 1959).

Dillon and Barbaras Compared

M. C. Dillon's Merleau-Ponty's Ontology is the best-known continuity reading (Dillon 1997). Dillon argues that the ontology of the late work is already implicit in the phenomenology of perception. On his reading, the primacy of perception runs through the whole of Merleau-Ponty's work, and the late concepts make explicit what the earlier descriptions of the body and the perceived world already showed. He resists readings that detach the late work from perception altogether.

Renaud Barbaras's The Being of the Phenomenon reads the late work as a more radical project (Barbaras [1991] 2004). On Barbaras's reading, the self-critique is serious. The late work tries to think the being of the phenomenon itself, the appearing of the world, without grounding it in a consciousness to which it appears. That requires going further than the earlier book could, and Barbaras treats the unfinished state of the project as reflecting real difficulties, not only an interrupted life.

These positions are interpretations of a contested text. Neither is the settled view, and many scholars take intermediate positions.

Conclusion

Readers divide over whether the late ontology continues or breaks with Phenomenology of Perception. Dillon argues for continuity, with the primacy of perception running throughout. Barbaras reads a more radical project that thinks the being of the phenomenon apart from consciousness. The unfinished text supports more than one reading.

Guided Reading 7 min

Guided Close Reading: The Working Note on the Consciousness–Object Distinction

Introduction

One working note in The Visible and the Invisible is quoted more than almost any other. In a few lines, Merleau-Ponty says that the problems of Phenomenology of Perception can't be solved because the book starts from the consciousness–object distinction. This reading works through the note in three moves, pauses on how to read its compressed style, flags a common misreading, and ends with a model paraphrase and a statement of what is kept and what is revised.

Locating the Note

The note is among the working notes printed after the manuscript, dated July 1959 (Merleau-Ponty [1964] 1968, Working Notes, July 1959). The notes are printed in date order, so you can find it by its date in any edition.

No free English translation is available. The free Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Merleau-Ponty summarizes the self-critique and the late ontology (Toadvine 2023, §6). The note's key phrases are paraphrased below. Its abbreviations are expanded in brackets.

Before you start, note the setting. By mid-1959, Merleau-Ponty had drafted parts of the manuscript and was testing reflection, dialectic, and intuition against the task of describing being before the subject–object split. The note looks back from that task at his earlier book.

Walking Through the Argument

Step 1: What "starting from" the distinction means

The note's first claim is that Phenomenology of Perception starts from the distinction between "consciousness" and "object." Merleau-Ponty puts both words in quotation marks, which signals that he's naming a framework, not endorsing its terms.

Notice what "starting from" means here. The earlier book doesn't assert that consciousness and object are separate. It argues against that at length (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012). The claim is about the order of explanation. The book begins with a perceiving subject and describes the world as it appears to that subject. Even when the subject is a body, and even when the world is described as bound up with it, the description is organized around the subject's point of view.

Step 2: Why this starting point leaves the problems unsolvable

The second claim is that, from this starting point, the book's problems are insoluble. Merleau-Ponty's example in the note concerns how the book passes from the order of objective facts, such as a lesion in the brain, to the order of lived experience, such as a disturbance of perception.

Here is why the problem can't be solved. If you start with consciousness on one side and objects on the other, then a physical fact and a lived experience belong to different orders. Any link between them has to be built across the gap. The earlier book described how the lived body mediates between them, but it couldn't explain why there's one world in which both occur. Every bridge assumes the two banks it was built to connect.

The shift here is from a problem to be solved to a problem to be dissolved. Merleau-Ponty doesn't propose a better bridge. He proposes giving up the starting point that made a bridge necessary.

Step 3: What the note proposes instead

The third move points forward. The problems can be addressed only by starting elsewhere: from a being that isn't yet divided into consciousness and object. Elsewhere in the notes, Merleau-Ponty calls this wild or brute being (Merleau-Ponty [1964] 1968, Working Notes). It's the starting point of the ontology he was trying to write.

The note doesn't say what this ontology is. It says what it must avoid. That's typical of the notes: they often clear the ground more clearly than they build on it.

How to Read the Note's Style

The note is compressed. It uses abbreviations ("Ph.P." for Phenomenology of Perception), leaves out verbs, and moves quickly between claims. Three habits help.

  1. Expand the abbreviations and restore the verbs. Write out the note as full sentences before you interpret it.
  2. Read it with its neighbors. Other notes from 1959, especially those on the tacit cogito and on bringing the earlier results to ontological explicitation, show what this note assumes.
  3. Treat it as a stage in a line of thought. The note records a diagnosis made while the new work was being drafted. It isn't a finished verdict.

A common misreading. The most common misreading takes the note as a rejection of everything in Phenomenology of Perception: as if Merleau-Ponty had decided his earlier work on the body was simply wrong.

  • The note criticizes a starting point, not the findings. The descriptions of the body schema, motor intentionality, habit, and the perceptual field aren't withdrawn.
  • The late work deepens embodiment. The body remains central in the late ontology. What changes is that it's no longer the body of a consciousness facing a world.
  • Other notes call for continuity. Merleau-Ponty speaks of bringing the earlier results to ontological explicitation, which assumes the results stand (Merleau-Ponty [1964] 1968, Working Notes, February 1959).

Summary

The note moves from naming the earlier book's starting point, through showing why that starting point makes its problems unsolvable, to calling for a new starting point prior to the split. Here is a model paraphrase of the argument to check your reading against:

Phenomenology of Perception began with consciousness and its object, even though it argued that the two belong together. Because it started there, it could only build bridges between the order of physical facts and the order of lived experience, and every bridge assumed the gap it was meant to close. So its problems can't be solved on its own terms. They can be addressed only by starting from being as it is before it's divided into consciousness and object (Merleau-Ponty [1964] 1968, Working Notes, July 1959).

Claude, please facilitate a reflective dialogue with me. I'm a graduate-level learner studying Merleau-Ponty's late self-critique of Phenomenology of Perception. Follow this guidance for the whole conversation.

GOAL
Help me explain why Merleau-Ponty came to judge Phenomenology of Perception as still bound to a philosophy of consciousness by applying the logic of his self-critique to an account of experience I find persuasive.

HOW TO RUN THE CONVERSATION
- Ask one question at a time, then wait for my reply. Keep each of your turns under about 120 words.
- Don't lecture. Explain a point only when I need it to continue, then return to the account I chose.
- Be patient, curious, and collegial. Encourage me to identify assumptions precisely. Define each term of art briefly on first use.
- Plain conversation only: no web search, files, or artifacts.
- Aim for about 12 minutes. If I can't choose an account, suggest the view that the brain builds a model of the world from sensory input. If I seem uncertain, shorten the conversation to 5-7 minutes. Always reach topic 4.
- Start now. Open with one or two warm sentences: this is an exploratory conversation, not a test; any account works, philosophical or scientific, even one I've only read about in outline. Then ask me to choose one.

TOPICS, IN ORDER
1. The starting point. Ask me to choose an account of perception or consciousness I find persuasive, and ask what it takes as its starting point. If I need examples, suggest a predictive-processing model of perception, a representational theory of mind, or an enactive account. Help me state what the account takes as given before it explains anything.
2. The hidden split. Ask whether the account begins with a subject and an object, even implicitly, and where. Help me look for signs: an inside and an outside, a model and what it models, a perceiver receiving input from a world. Introduce Merleau-Ponty's self-critique: that Phenomenology of Perception started from the consciousness–object distinction even while arguing against dualism.
3. Starting without it. Ask what it would look like to start without that distinction. Help me try. Introduce wild being, prior to the subject–object split, and hyper-reflection, which takes account of its own roots. Acknowledge that this is hard, and that Merleau-Ponty himself left it unfinished.
4. Closing reflection. Ask whether Merleau-Ponty's self-critique is fair to his earlier work, and ask me to note my view for a journal entry. Help me give a reason. Either view is acceptable.

POSITIONS TO KEEP ACCURATE
- Merleau-Ponty judged that Phenomenology of Perception's problems were insoluble because it started from the consciousness–object distinction.
- The criticism concerns the starting point and order of explanation, not a claim that the book asserted two substances.
- The tacit cogito, a silent self-presence beneath reflection, was the symptom he singled out. He came to judge it impossible.
- He found reflection, dialectic, and intuition each wanting, and proposed hyper-reflection aimed at wild being.
- The working notes are provisional, compressed, and sometimes inconsistent.
- Whether the late work continues or breaks with the earlier one is debated.

MISREADINGS TO CORRECT GENTLY
Keep every point tied to the account I chose. When a misreading appears, name the accurate position briefly, then return to my account.
- The self-critique as abandoning embodiment. The late work deepens embodiment rather than dropping it.
- The self-critique as rejecting all earlier findings. It targets a starting point, not the descriptions of the body and perception.
- "Consciousness–object distinction" as mind–body dualism only. An embodied subject facing a world can still have this structure.
- The working notes as final doctrine. They're provisional and should be read together.
- The tacit cogito as the same as Descartes's cogito. It was meant as a silent, pre-reflective self-presence beneath the explicit "I think."

TO FINISH
After I state my view, close in one short turn:
- Affirm one precise observation I made, in my own words where possible.
- Suggest one or two next steps that fit how the conversation went. Possible steps: write the journal entry; compare the continuity and rupture readings of the late work; apply the same test to the starting point of my own research; review the consciousness–object starting point and the tacit cogito; reread Merleau-Ponty's statement of the self-critique closely and paraphrase it; review the terms philosophy of consciousness, tacit cogito, and wild being; retry the conversation with the example of a brain building a model of the world.
- Restate my view and my reason on its own line, labeled "Journal note," so I can copy it as the focus for a journal entry.
