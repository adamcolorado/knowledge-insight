Body-Subject: Body Schema and Motor Intentionality

This lesson presents Merleau-Ponty's account of one's own body (corps propre): its body schema, its "spatiality of situation," and motor intentionality, the way movement is directed at things without explicit representation. You'll be able to explain these concepts, distinguish the body schema from the body image, and apply them to ordinary actions like reaching and walking. These concepts are foundational for current research on action, agency, and embodied cognition.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the body schema and motor intentionality as forms of bodily understanding that precede explicit thought

The Body Schema and Pre-Reflective Bodily Knowledge in Current Research

You can reach for the light switch in a dark room you know, and your hand lands on it. A cup slides off the table, and you catch it before you've noticed it falling. In neither case did you look at your hand, calculate its position, or plan a trajectory. Your body knew where it was and where to go.

This everyday competence poses a real question for theories of action. It's also fragile in revealing ways. After a stroke, or when a limb has been numbed, people can lose it, and have to watch their hands to guide them. A familiar model treats action as the execution of a plan. The brain represents the body's current position, represents the goal, computes a path between them, and sends commands to the muscles. On this model, catching the cup requires a rapid series of representations and calculations, all performed unconsciously.

The model has been enormously productive in motor-control research. But it also invites a question. Does skilled action really need representations of the body and its goals, or does the body have a more direct, practical grasp of its own position and possibilities? The answer shapes what researchers look for, and what they count as explaining an action.

Philosophers and cognitive scientists have approached the question through the concept of the body schema. Frédérique de Vignemont surveys the field: the body schema is usually understood as the body representation, or the set of sensorimotor capacities, used for guiding action, often without awareness (de Vignemont 2024). How to characterize it, and whether it should be called a representation at all, remains disputed. So does its relation to the body image, the body as we perceive and think about it.

Maurice Merleau-Ponty's Phenomenology of Perception is one of the main sources of the concept in philosophy (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, pt. 1, ch. 3). He argues that the body's knowledge of where it is and what it can do comes before explicit thought. It isn't a map consulted by a mind. It's the body's own practical grip on its situation, directed at tasks rather than coordinates.

Having a precise vocabulary for this kind of pre-reflective bodily understanding lets you ask of any model of action what it assumes the body knows, how, and whether that knowledge has to take the form of a representation. The question doesn't rule out computational models. It makes clear what they need to explain.

The Body as Object and One's Own Body in Merleau-Ponty

Introduction

Physiology describes the body as an object: a system of tissues, nerves, and reflexes, studied like any other thing. Merleau-Ponty argues that this description, however accurate, misses the body we live.

This reading explains why he rejects the mechanistic account of the body, and how he describes one's own body instead: its permanence, its double sensations, and its kinaesthetic character. Citations to Phenomenology of Perception give the part and chapter. Landes's translation prints the French page numbers, so learners using Colin Smith's translation can follow along (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2002).

The Body as Object and Why It Fails

Merleau-Ponty opens Part One with the body as mechanistic physiology describes it (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, pt. 1, ch. 1). On this account, the body is a complex object in which stimuli trigger nerve impulses that produce sensations and movements, one link at a time. Mechanistic physiology, in his usage, is the approach that explains bodily experience and behavior as chains of local causes in a body treated as an object among others.

He argues that the approach can't account for what bodies do. Clinical cases, such as the phantom limb, show experiences that don't follow the chain of local causes. Nor can psychological explanations simply fill the gap. The failure of both points to a body that's neither a mere object nor a mere idea: the body as lived.

One's Own Body

Merleau-Ponty's term for the lived body is one's own body (corps propre): the body as I live it, as the permanent point of view from which I perceive and act, rather than as an object I observe. He describes its features in Part One, chapter 2.

Permanence. Ordinary objects can be approached, moved away from, and left behind. I can walk around a table and see it from every side. My own body is different. It's always with me. I can't distance myself from it or view it from all sides, as I can a table. The permanence of one's own body, in Merleau-Ponty's account, is its constant presence as the point of view from which everything else is perceived, never an object I could leave or survey from all sides. It isn't one more thing in my visual field. It's what I see from.

Double sensations. When my left hand touches my right, the right hand can be felt as touched, or as touching, but not both at once. Merleau-Ponty draws this analysis from Husserl, who used it to show that the body is given both as a thing and as a sensing, lived body (Husserl [1952] 1989, §36). A double sensation is the experience of one hand touching the other, in which each hand can be felt as touching or as touched. The alternation shows that my body is both subject and object of perception, but never both at the same moment.

Kinaesthetic character. I don't move my body as I move an object. To move a cup, I must first reach for it. To move my hand, I don't need to find it first. I simply move. Kinaesthesis is the felt sense of one's own movement, including movements of the eyes, head, and limbs. It gives my body to me from within, not as an object located and then displaced.

Merleau-Ponty adds that one's own body is also affective. Pain, for example, is felt in the foot, not as a report about the foot. These features together mark the difference between the lived body and the body as object.

Conclusion

Mechanistic physiology treats the body as an object moved by chains of local causes, and Merleau-Ponty argues that it can't account for lived bodily experience. One's own body is permanent, the point of view I can't leave. It is both subject and object in double sensations, never both at once. And it is given kinaesthetically, moved directly rather than located first.

Key Terms

  • Mechanistic physiology: In Merleau-Ponty's usage, the approach that explains bodily experience and behavior as chains of local causes in a body treated as an object among others.
  • One's own body (corps propre): In Merleau-Ponty, the body as I live it, as the permanent point of view from which I perceive and act, rather than as an object I observe.
  • Permanence of one's own body: In Merleau-Ponty, its constant presence as the point of view from which everything else is perceived, never an object I could leave or survey from all sides.
  • Double sensation: The experience of one hand touching the other, in which each hand can be felt as touching or as touched.
  • Kinaesthesis: The felt sense of one's own movement, including movements of the eyes, head, and limbs.

Body Schema, Spatiality of Situation, and Motor Intentionality

Introduction

How do you know where your hand is when you're not looking at it? The obvious answer is that the brain keeps a map of the body. Merleau-Ponty argues that bodily knowledge isn't a map at all. It's a practical grip on possible action.

This reading explains the body schema, the distinction between spatiality of situation and spatiality of position, and motor intentionality, from Part One, chapter 3. Citations to Phenomenology of Perception give the part and chapter.

The Body Schema

Merleau-Ponty takes the term body schema (schéma corporel) from neurology and psychology. For him, it's a dynamic, unified sense of the body's position and possibilities for action, not an inventory of parts or a mental image of the body (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, pt. 1, ch. 3).

Two contrasts clarify the definition.

  • Not an inventory. The body schema isn't a list of body parts and their positions, updated piece by piece. I grasp my body as a whole, and each part's position is given through its role in that whole.
  • Not an image. The body schema isn't a picture of my body that I consult. It works in the background, in what I'm doing, rather than as something I look at.

What it is, positively, is a way of being oriented toward tasks. My body schema is my body as poised for action in a situation.

Two Kinds of Spatiality

This leads to Merleau-Ponty's distinction between two kinds of bodily space.

  • Spatiality of position is the space of objects: points located in an objective coordinate system, each outside the others, measurable by distances and angles.
  • Spatiality of situation is the space of one's own body: organized by tasks and possible actions rather than coordinates, with each part situated by its role in what the body is doing.

The contrast can be set out in a table, rendering the outline's two-panel figure.

Spatiality of positionSpatiality of situation
What it locatesObjects, including the body as objectOne's own body as lived
Organized byCoordinates and distancesTasks and possible actions
A hand's locationAt x, y, z relative to the torsoWhere it's needed: on the handle, at the switch
How parts relateSide by side, each outside the othersEnveloping one another in one action

My hand isn't, for me, at a point in space that I then compute my way to. It's at the handle I'm about to turn. The lesson's close reading works through this passage.

Motor Intentionality and the "I Can"

The body schema and spatiality of situation are expressions of a more basic capacity. Motor intentionality, in Merleau-Ponty's account, is the body's directedness toward things and goals through movement itself, aimed at things rather than at coordinates, and not mediated by explicit representations (Toadvine 2023, §3).

When I reach for a cup, my movement is directed at the cup. It isn't directed at a location, calculated and then targeted. The cup solicits the reach, and my hand goes to it. The intentionality is in the movement, not in a thought that precedes it.

Merleau-Ponty, following Husserl, calls this bodily power the "I can": the body's felt capacities for movement and action, which shape what is perceived as possible (Husserl [1952] 1989). Consciousness, he says, is originally not an "I think that" but an "I can." My world shows up as a field of possibilities for my body: things are within reach, graspable, climbable, or too far.

Conclusion

The body schema is a dynamic, unified sense of the body's possibilities, not an inventory or an image. One's own body occupies a spatiality of situation, organized by tasks, rather than a spatiality of position, organized by coordinates. Motor intentionality directs movement at things through the "I can," before any explicit thought.

Key Terms

  • Body schema (schéma corporel): In Merleau-Ponty, a dynamic, unified sense of the body's position and possibilities for action, not an inventory of parts or a mental image of the body.
  • Spatiality of position: In Merleau-Ponty, the space of objects: points located in an objective coordinate system, each outside the others.
  • Spatiality of situation: In Merleau-Ponty, the space of one's own body, organized by tasks and possible actions rather than coordinates, with each part situated by its role in what the body is doing.
  • Motor intentionality: In Merleau-Ponty, the body's directedness toward things and goals through movement itself, aimed at things rather than coordinates, and not mediated by explicit representations.
  • "I can": The body's felt capacities for movement and action, which shape what is perceived as possible.

The Body Schema from Head and Holmes to Gallagher's Distinction

Introduction

"Body schema" and "body image" are used in philosophy, neurology, and psychology, often interchangeably and often confusingly. Part of the confusion is historical, and part of it comes from translation.

This reading traces the body-schema concept from early 20th-century neurology through Merleau-Ponty, explains a translation problem in the older English version of Phenomenology of Perception, and presents Shaun Gallagher's influential distinction between body schema and body image.

Origins in Neurology

The concept entered modern neurology through Henry Head and Gordon Holmes's 1911 study of patients with brain lesions (Head and Holmes 1911). Some patients couldn't tell where a limb was, or whether it had moved, without looking. Their proprioception, the sense of the position and movement of one's own body parts, based on receptors in muscles, tendons, and joints, was disturbed. Head and Holmes proposed that the normal brain maintains a postural schema: in their account, a continually updated standard of the body's posture against which each new change of position is registered, largely outside awareness.

Their key insight was that the schema works as a background standard. Each new posture is registered relative to the previous ones, before any change reaches consciousness. The idea spread quickly into psychology and psychiatry, where it took on many different meanings.

Merleau-Ponty's Use and a Translation Problem

Merleau-Ponty adopted the term schéma corporel from this literature. He criticized both its early, associationist versions and its purely psychological ones. For him, the body schema (schéma corporel) is a dynamic, unified sense of the body's position and possibilities for action, not an inventory of parts or a mental image of the body (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, pt. 1, ch. 3).

English readers long met the term in a confusing form. Colin Smith's 1962 translation sometimes renders schéma corporel as "body image" (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2002). That's a problem, because "body image" suggests a picture of the body, which is exactly what Merleau-Ponty denies the schema is. Donald Landes's 2012 translation consistently uses "body schema." When you read Smith, or commentary based on it, check the French.

Gallagher's Distinction

Shaun Gallagher proposed a distinction that has become standard in philosophy of mind, while remaining debated (Gallagher 2005, ch. 1).

  • The body schema, for Gallagher, is a system of sensorimotor capacities that function without awareness or the need for perceptual monitoring. It governs posture and movement.
  • The body image is a system of perceptions, attitudes, and beliefs about one's own body. It's how the body appears to me and what I think and feel about it.

The distinction is conceptual, but Gallagher argues that it's supported by cases in which the two come apart. A person can lose much of the body schema's normal support while keeping a body image, and the reverse. Frédérique de Vignemont's survey shows how widely the distinction has been taken up, and also how contested it is (de Vignemont 2024, §3.2). Researchers disagree about how many distinct body representations there are, and about whether the body schema should be called a representation at all.

Gallagher's body schema is narrower than Merleau-Ponty's. Merleau-Ponty's schema includes the body's lived orientation toward tasks and meanings. Gallagher's is specifically the sub-personal system of sensorimotor capacities.

Conclusion

The body-schema concept began with Head and Holmes's postural schema, a background standard of bodily posture. Merleau-Ponty reworked it as a dynamic sense of the body's possibilities. Smith's older translation sometimes renders it as "body image," a known source of confusion. Gallagher distinguishes the body schema, sensorimotor capacities working without awareness, from the body image, perceptions, attitudes, and beliefs about the body. The distinction is influential and debated.

Key Terms

  • Proprioception: The sense of the position and movement of one's own body parts, based on receptors in muscles, tendons, and joints.
  • Postural schema: In Head and Holmes's account, a continually updated standard of the body's posture against which each new change of position is registered, largely outside awareness.
  • Body schema (schéma corporel): In Merleau-Ponty, a dynamic, unified sense of the body's position and possibilities for action, not an inventory of parts or a mental image of the body.
  • Body image: In Gallagher's distinction, a system of perceptions, attitudes, and beliefs about one's own body.

Body Schema and Body Image Compared: Awareness, Function, and Disorders

Introduction

A distinction earns its keep when it helps sort cases. The body schema and body image distinction is tested most sharply by disorders in which one is disrupted and the other isn't.

This reading compares the two across awareness, function, examples, and disorders, and gathers the lesson's core terms.

The Distinction Compared

The table follows Gallagher's distinction (Gallagher 2005, ch. 1) and de Vignemont's survey (de Vignemont 2024, §3.2).

Body schemaBody image
AwarenessMostly works without awareness, in the backgroundInvolves awareness: perceiving, thinking, or feeling about the body
FunctionGoverns posture and movementShapes how the body appears to me and what I believe about it
ExamplesKeeping balance while reaching; walking through a doorway without judging its widthSeeing yourself in a mirror; feeling self-conscious about your height
When disruptedMovement loses its automatic supportPerception of or attitudes toward the body are distorted

Cases Where They Come Apart

Gallagher supports the distinction with cases where the two dissociate.

  • Deafferentation is the loss of proprioceptive and touch input from the body, usually from nerve damage, so that the body's position isn't felt from within. Gallagher discusses a patient who lost this input below the neck (Gallagher 2005, ch. 2). Without the body schema's normal support, he could still move, but only by watching his limbs and controlling each movement deliberately. He relied on vision and attention, drawing on something like the body image to do work the body schema usually does without awareness.
  • In other conditions, such as some forms of unilateral neglect, the reverse pattern appears. Patients may move with some part of the body while failing to perceive or acknowledge it as their own.

These cases are among the strongest evidence for treating the two as distinct. Critics reply that the real picture involves more than two systems, and that the labels hide important variation (de Vignemont 2024, §3.2). The distinction is best treated as a useful but debated tool.

Where Merleau-Ponty Fits

Merleau-Ponty's body schema overlaps with Gallagher's, but it isn't the same. Merleau-Ponty describes it from the first-person side, as the body's lived orientation toward tasks (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, pt. 1, ch. 3). Gallagher specifies it at the level of sensorimotor systems. Both deny that the body schema is a picture of the body. That shared denial is what matters most for Merleau-Ponty's argument.

Conclusion

The body schema mostly works without awareness and governs movement. The body image involves awareness and concerns how the body appears and what we believe about it. Deafferentation and some forms of neglect show them coming apart, though critics argue that two categories are too few. Merleau-Ponty's and Gallagher's body schemas differ in level, but both deny that it's a picture of the body.

Guided Close Reading: Spatiality of Situation in Phenomenology of Perception

Introduction

Where is your hand? The answer seems obvious: at a certain distance and direction from your shoulder. Merleau-Ponty argues that this answer describes your hand as an object, not as you live it. His passage on the spatiality of one's own body in Part One, chapter 3, gives the lived answer. This reading works through it move by move, and ends with a model paraphrase of the distinction.

Locating the Passage

The passage is at the start of Part One, chapter 3, "The Spatiality of One's Own Body and Motricity" (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, pt. 1, ch. 3). Landes's translation prints the French page numbers in the margin, so learners using Colin Smith's translation can follow along (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2002). Key phrases below are given in French, with typical English renderings under Key Considerations.

No free English translation is available. The free Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Merleau-Ponty summarizes the chapter's account of the body (Toadvine 2023, §3).

Before you start, note the setting. Merleau-Ponty has argued that one's own body isn't an object. He now asks what kind of space it occupies.

Walking Through the Argument

Step 1: Body parts aren't spread out side by side like objects; they envelop one another

Merleau-Ponty begins with a contrast. Objects in space are laid out partes extra partes: each part outside the others, side by side, like the legs and top of a table. My body, as I live it, isn't like that. My arm and my hand aren't two objects I know to be adjacent. I have my arm and my hand together, as one limb, in one action. The parts of my body envelop one another (s'enveloppent). Each is present through the whole.

That's why I don't need to locate my hand by finding it relative to my shoulder. I know where it is because I'm living it, as part of a single bodily whole.

Step 2: The body is polarized by its tasks

Next, Merleau-Ponty asks what organizes this whole. His answer is: what the body is doing. The body's space is oriented, or polarized, by its tasks. It exists toward them and gathers itself around them.

So the "same" body occupies a different space depending on the task. When I write, my body is organized around the pen's tip. When I walk, it's organized around the ground ahead. The parts that matter for the task stand out, and the others recede. This is the spatiality of situation, as opposed to the spatiality of position that objects have.

Step 3: "Here" means the anchoring of the active body in an object

The final move redefines the word "here." For the space of objects, "here" is a point: the origin of a coordinate system. For one's own body, Merleau-Ponty argues, "here" isn't a point at all. It means the anchoring of the active body in an object, and the body's situation facing its tasks.

When I say my hand is "here," on the handle, I'm not naming a coordinate. I'm naming a situation: my hand engaged with the handle, in the task of opening the door.

Leaning on a Desk

Merleau-Ponty's example makes the analysis concrete. Suppose I'm standing at my desk, leaning on it with both hands. Where is my body, as I live it?

Only my hands are accentuated. The pressure is in my palms, where they bear my weight on the desk. The rest of my body trails behind them, comme la queue d'une comète, like the tail of a comet. I'm not aware of the position of my shoulder or my elbow as points in space. I'm aware of them only through their role in the leaning. My body is gathered around the task and anchored in the desk.

If you tried to describe the same posture in spatiality of position, you'd give angles and distances: elbows at so many degrees, hands so far apart. That description is accurate, but it isn't what the leaning is like. The comet image captures the lived structure: a point of intensity where the body meets its task, and everything else following.

A common misreading. The most common misreading treats "situation" as psychological: how I feel about where I am, or my mental attitude toward a task. That misses the passage's point.

  • Situation is bodily. It's how the body itself is organized in space by its tasks, not a mood or belief about them.
  • It's not less real than position. Spatiality of situation is the space in which bodily action actually happens. Spatiality of position is abstracted from it.
  • It doesn't deny objective space. The body can also be measured. Merleau-Ponty's claim is that measurement describes the body as object, not as lived.

Summary

The passage moves from body parts enveloping one another, through the body polarized by its tasks, to "here" as the anchoring of the active body in an object. The desk example shows the structure. Here is a model paraphrase to check your reading against:

Objects occupy space part outside part, located by coordinates. One's own body doesn't. Its parts envelop one another in one whole, organized by what the body is doing. Leaning on a desk, I feel my hands and the rest of my body trails behind them like a comet's tail. "Here," for my body, isn't a point in a coordinate system but the anchoring of my active body in its task. That's spatiality of situation. Spatiality of position is the space of objects, including my body treated as one (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, pt. 1, ch. 3).
