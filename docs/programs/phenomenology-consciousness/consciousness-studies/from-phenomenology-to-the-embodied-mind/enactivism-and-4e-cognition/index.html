Embodied Mind: Enactivism and 4E Cognition

This lesson describes the enactive approach introduced in The Embodied Mind and the broader "4E" family: embodied, embedded, extended, and enactive cognition. You'll be able to explain the core claims of autopoietic, sensorimotor, and radical enactivism and of the extended mind thesis, and identify where these views agree and conflict. These frameworks structure much of current research on mind and consciousness beyond the brain-bound model.

What you will be able to do

  • Describe the core claims of enactivism and of embodied, embedded, extended, and enactive (4E) cognition

Brain-Bound and 4E Models of Where Cognition Happens

A standard assumption in cognitive science is that cognition happens in the head. The senses take in information, the brain processes it, and the brain sends commands to the body. On this picture, the body and the world supply the mind's inputs and carry out its outputs. They aren't parts of the mind.

Some everyday cases put pressure on the assumption. Multiply two four-digit numbers in your head, then do it with pen and paper. On paper, you write down partial results, and the page holds them while you work on the next step. Much of the difficulty of mental arithmetic is the difficulty of doing without that page. Navigating a city with a phone is similar. The phone doesn't only report where you are. It changes which decisions you have to make at all. And a musician improvising may say she thinks through the instrument: the phrase takes shape under her fingers, not in a plan she then carries out.

It's easy to say these cases are just the brain using tools. The question is whether that description is accurate or only habitual. If the page stores partial results the way working memory would, why count one as part of the thinking and not the other? If the musician's fingers find the phrase, where did the thinking happen?

These questions define an active research program. Under the label "4E cognition," researchers in philosophy, psychology, and robotics argue that cognition is embodied (shaped by the body), embedded (dependent on the environment), extended (partly made up of processes outside the body), or enactive (constituted by an organism's active engagement with its world). The program is diverse, and its claims range from modest to radical (Spaulding 2021). The modest claim is that bodies and environments influence cognition, which few researchers deny. The radical claim is that they're sometimes part of it.

For a researcher, the value is a map. Every theory of mind makes an assumption about where cognition happens, even when it doesn't state one. With the map in hand, you can locate a theory as internalist or as one of the 4E positions, and ask what follows. Does a study of memory treat notes as external aids or as part of remembering? Does a model of vision treat movement as input or as part of seeing? The answers shape what counts as evidence, and they're often settled before any data are collected.

The Embodied Mind: Enaction, the Middle Way, and Mindfulness

Introduction

In 1991 a neuroscientist, a philosopher, and a cognitive psychologist argued that cognitive science had left out the one thing it was supposed to explain: experience. Their book, The Embodied Mind, gave the enactive approach its name.

This reading explains the book's central proposal, cognition as enaction, the "middle way" between realism and idealism, the role it gave to Buddhist mindfulness practice, and Evan Thompson's later revision of its Buddhist framing. Citations give chapter numbers from the 2016 revised edition.

Against Cognitivism

Francisco Varela, Evan Thompson, and Eleanor Rosch wrote against the dominant view of their time (Varela, Thompson, and Rosch 2016). Cognitivism is the view, dominant in early cognitive science, that cognition is computation over symbolic representations of a world whose features are given in advance.

Their objection wasn't only that cognitivism was too rule-bound. They argued that it shared an assumption with many of its rivals: that the world comes with its features already fixed, and the mind's job is to represent them. They proposed to drop the assumption.

Enaction

Enaction is the view that cognition is embodied action: an organism brings forth its world through its sensorimotor activity rather than representing a pregiven world. The authors state it in two parts. Perception consists in perceptually guided action. And cognitive structures emerge from the recurring sensorimotor patterns that let action be guided by perception (Varela, Thompson, and Rosch 2016, ch. 8).

Their main example is color. On one view, colors are properties of surfaces, there to be detected. On another, they're projections of the mind. The authors argue that neither is right. Which colors an animal sees depends on its visual system as well as on the light reflected from surfaces. Different species, with different visual systems, live in different color worlds. Color is neither simply out there nor simply in here. It's brought forth in the animal's perceptual engagement with its surroundings.

The Middle Way

The color example stands for a general position. Middle way: in The Embodied Mind, the position between realism and idealism on which organism and world specify each other, so that neither is a fixed ground for the other (Varela, Thompson, and Rosch 2016, ch. 10).

Realism holds that the world is pregiven, and cognition recovers it. Idealism holds that the mind projects its world. The middle way rejects the shared premise that cognition needs a fixed ground, either in the world or in the mind. The shift is from asking which side supplies the ground to denying that there is one. The authors took the name from the Madhyamaka ("middle way") tradition of Buddhist philosophy.

Mindfulness and Experience

Why bring Buddhist practice into cognitive science? The authors argued that cognitive science lacked a disciplined way to examine experience. It could study the mind from outside, but it had no method for attending to experience as lived. They proposed that Buddhist meditative traditions supplied one.

Mindfulness-awareness practice, in The Embodied Mind, is a meditative discipline, drawn from Buddhist traditions and used as a method for examining experience, in which attention is trained to stay with what's happening in the mind rather than being carried away by it. The authors reported that such practice reveals no stable, unified self behind experience. They connected this to findings in cognitive science that the mind has no central controller.

Thompson's Revision

Thompson has since revised aspects of this framing (Thompson 2020). He criticizes Buddhist exceptionalism: in Thompson's usage, the view that Buddhism is uniquely rational or scientific among religions, and so is more a science of mind than a religion. He argues that Buddhism is a religion like others, and that presenting meditation as confirmed by neuroscience misdescribes both. He continues to value contemplative practice and cross-cultural philosophy. The enactive claims about cognition don't depend on the book's Buddhist framing, and Thompson continues to defend them.

Conclusion

The Embodied Mind treats cognition as enaction, the bringing forth of a world through sensorimotor activity, against cognitivism's pregiven world. Its middle way rejects any fixed ground in world or mind. It proposed mindfulness-awareness practice as a method for examining experience, a framing Thompson has since revised in his critique of Buddhist exceptionalism.

Key Terms

  • Cognitivism: The view, dominant in early cognitive science, that cognition is computation over symbolic representations of a world whose features are given in advance.
  • Enaction: The view that cognition is embodied action: an organism brings forth its world through its sensorimotor activity rather than representing a pregiven world.
  • Middle way: In The Embodied Mind, the position between realism and idealism on which organism and world specify each other, so that neither is a fixed ground for the other.
  • Mindfulness-awareness practice: In The Embodied Mind, a meditative discipline, drawn from Buddhist traditions and used as a method for examining experience, in which attention is trained to stay with what's happening in the mind rather than being carried away by it.
  • Buddhist exceptionalism: In Thompson's usage, the view that Buddhism is uniquely rational or scientific among religions, and so is more a science of mind than a religion.

Autopoietic, Sensorimotor, and Radical Enactivism and Their Phenomenological Sources

Introduction

"Enactivism" now names several research programs. They share a rejection of cognition as the representation of a pregiven world. They disagree about what cognition fundamentally is.

This reading explains the three main varieties, autopoietic, sensorimotor, and radical enactivism, and the phenomenological sources each draws on.

Autopoietic Enactivism

Autopoietic enactivism is the variety of enactivism, developed most fully by Evan Thompson, that grounds cognition in the self-producing organization of living systems and in their sense-making (Thompson 2007). It builds on a theory of the living cell developed in theoretical biology in the 1970s.

The theory's central concept is autopoiesis: the self-producing organization of a living system, which continually produces the components that produce it and maintains a boundary with its environment. A cell's metabolism makes the membrane that contains the metabolism. The system is its own product.

Because a living system must keep producing itself, things in its environment matter to it. Sense-making, in enactivism, is an organism's bringing forth of a meaningful world through its self-maintaining activity, so that things show up as relevant or irrelevant, good or bad, for it. A bacterium swims up a sugar gradient. Sugar isn't food in itself. It's food for this organism, given its metabolism.

This grounds Thompson's thesis of a deep continuity between life and mind. The organizational features of mind, such as having a point of view and a world of significance, are enriched versions of features every living thing has (Thompson 2007).

Sensorimotor Enactivism

Sensorimotor enactivism is the variety of enactivism, developed by J. Kevin O'Regan and Alva Noë, on which perceiving is an activity of exploring the environment, made possible by mastery of sensorimotor contingencies (O'Regan and Noë 2001). Sensorimotor contingencies are the law-like ways in which sensory stimulation changes as a perceiver moves, or as objects move relative to the perceiver.

When you walk around a plate, the shape it projects on your eyes changes from a circle to an ellipse and back. You see a round plate because you've mastered how its appearance varies with movement. On this view, seeing and touching differ because they involve different sensorimotor contingencies, not because their signals travel to different parts of the brain.

Noë extends the view to the experience of detail (Noë 2004). You experience a tomato as whole and three-dimensional, though you see only its front. Its hidden back is present to you as available: you know how turning it, or moving your head, would bring the back into view. Perceptual presence, on this view, is a kind of access.

Radical Enactivism

Radical enactivism is Daniel Hutto and Erik Myin's variety of enactivism, which holds that basic cognition, such as perceiving and acting, involves no mental content at all (Hutto and Myin 2013). Content, meaning truth or accuracy conditions, arises only with language and shared social practices.

Their main argument is the hard problem of content: Hutto and Myin's name for the challenge that naturalistic theories can't explain how basic cognition could have content, using only the resources, such as information understood as covariation, that those theories allow. A thermometer's reading covaries with temperature, but covariation isn't truth. On their view, the problem dissolves if basic minds never had content.

Phenomenological Sources

Shaun Gallagher and Dan Zahavi trace each variety to phenomenological sources (Gallagher and Zahavi 2021).

  1. Merleau-Ponty's motor intentionality. The body is directed toward things through movement itself, without explicit representations (Merleau-Ponty [1945] 2012, pt. 1, ch. 3). All three varieties draw on this, the radical variety most directly.
  2. Husserl's kinaesthesis. Husserl described how the appearances of a thing are correlated with the perceiver's own movements (Husserl [1973] 1997, §§44–57). Sensorimotor contingencies restate that correlation.
  3. Heidegger's being-in-the-world. Heidegger described human existence as involvement with things and tasks, not as a mind facing objects (Heidegger [1927] 2008, §12). The organism-in-its-world of the autopoietic variety extends this beyond human existence.

Conclusion

Autopoietic enactivism grounds mind in living self-production and sense-making. Sensorimotor enactivism treats perceiving as skilled exploration governed by sensorimotor contingencies. Radical enactivism denies that basic cognition has content, pressing the hard problem of content. All three draw on phenomenological accounts of motor intentionality, kinaesthesis, and being-in-the-world.

  • Autopoiesis: The self-producing organization of a living system, which continually produces the components that produce it and maintains a boundary with its environment.
  • Sense-making: In enactivism, an organism's bringing forth of a meaningful world through its self-maintaining activity, so that things show up as relevant or irrelevant, good or bad, for it.
  • Sensorimotor enactivism: The variety of enactivism, developed by J. Kevin O'Regan and Alva Noë, on which perceiving is an activity of exploring the environment, made possible by mastery of sensorimotor contingencies.
  • Sensorimotor contingencies: The law-like ways in which sensory stimulation changes as a perceiver moves, or as objects move relative to the perceiver.
  • Radical enactivism: Daniel Hutto and Erik Myin's variety of enactivism, which holds that basic cognition, such as perceiving and acting, involves no mental content at all.
  • Hard problem of content: Hutto and Myin's name for the challenge that naturalistic theories can't explain how basic cognition could have content, using only the resources, such as information understood as covariation, that those theories allow.

The Four E's and Clark and Chalmers's Extended Mind

Introduction

"4E" groups four claims that are often run together. They differ in how far they move cognition beyond the brain, and their proponents don't always agree.

This reading distinguishes the four claims and explains the most debated of them, the extended mind thesis. Citations to "The Extended Mind" give its section numbers (§).

Four Claims

4E cognition is an umbrella term for approaches holding that cognition is embodied, embedded, extended, or enactive, rather than confined to processes in the brain (Spaulding 2021). The four claims differ in strength.

Embodied cognition is the claim that cognition depends on, and is shaped by, the body's form, capacities, and movements, not only on the brain. Embedded cognition is the claim that cognition depends on the environment and relies on structures in it, which remain outside the cognitive process itself. The extended mind thesis is Clark and Chalmers's claim that cognitive processes, and even beliefs, can be partly made up of resources outside the body when those resources play the right functional role (Clark and Chalmers 1998). The enactive claim, the fourth, holds that cognition is embodied action through which an organism brings forth its world.

The table sets the four side by side.

ClaimCore claimExampleA key figure
EmbodiedThe body shapes cognitionGesture helps speakers find wordsGallagher (2005)
EmbeddedCognition relies on the environment, which stays outside itA cook lays out ingredients in order of useAdams and Aizawa (2008), as critics of extension who accept embedding
ExtendedCognition can be partly made up of external resourcesA notebook that stores a person's beliefsClark and Chalmers (1998)
EnactiveCognition is embodied action that brings forth a worldPerception as skilled explorationVarela, Thompson, and Rosch (2016)

The difference between embedded and extended is the one that matters most for the debate. Both agree that the cook's arrangement of ingredients helps. The embedded claim says it helps a cognitive process that stays in the head. The extended claim says, in the right conditions, that it's part of the process.

The Parity Principle

Andy Clark and David Chalmers argue for extension with a principle of fair treatment. The parity principle is Clark and Chalmers's principle that if a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would count as cognitive, then that part of the world is part of the cognitive process (Clark and Chalmers 1998, §2).

Their first example is the video game Tetris. A player can decide whether a falling shape fits a slot in three ways. She can rotate it mentally. She can press a button that rotates it on screen. Or, in a future case, she might use a neural implant that rotates it as fast as the button does. Clark and Chalmers argue that the three cases don't differ in any way that should make only the first count as cognitive. Location alone isn't a reason.

When a person and an external resource interact in this way, they form a coupled system: in Clark and Chalmers, a system in which an organism and an external resource interact in both directions, so that the whole can be seen as a cognitive system in its own right. Remove the external part, and the system's performance drops, as it would if part of the brain were removed (Clark and Chalmers 1998, §2).

From Cognition to Mind

Clark and Chalmers then go further. Not only processes but beliefs can extend. Their example is Otto, a man with Alzheimer's disease who keeps the information he needs in a notebook, including the address of a museum he wants to visit (Clark and Chalmers 1998, §4). They argue that the notebook's entry plays the same role for Otto that memory plays for someone else. So the belief about the museum's address is partly in the notebook, beyond the skin.

Conclusion

The four E's differ in strength: embodied and embedded cognition keep cognition in the organism while stressing body and environment, and extended and enactive cognition go further. The extended mind thesis rests on the parity principle: location alone doesn't decide what counts as cognitive. Coupled systems can be cognitive systems, and even beliefs can extend beyond the skin.

Key Terms

  • 4E cognition: An umbrella term for approaches holding that cognition is embodied, embedded, extended, or enactive, rather than confined to processes in the brain.
  • Embodied cognition: The claim that cognition depends on, and is shaped by, the body's form, capacities, and movements, not only on the brain.
  • Embedded cognition: The claim that cognition depends on the environment and relies on structures in it, which remain outside the cognitive process itself.
  • Extended mind thesis: Clark and Chalmers's claim that cognitive processes, and even beliefs, can be partly made up of resources outside the body when those resources play the right functional role.
  • Parity principle: Clark and Chalmers's principle that if a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would count as cognitive, then that part of the world is part of the cognitive process.
  • Coupled system: In Clark and Chalmers, a system in which an organism and an external resource interact in both directions, so that the whole can be seen as a cognitive system in its own right.

Objections and Tensions in 4E Cognition, and Gallagher's Bridge

Introduction

The extended mind thesis drew immediate objections. The 4E family also contains disagreements among its own members, which the label can hide.

This reading explains the two main objections to the extended mind, a central tension within 4E cognition, and Shaun Gallagher's body schema work as a bridge between phenomenology and experimental science. It ends with a table of core terms.

Coupling Is Not Constitution

Frederick Adams and Kenneth Aizawa gave the best-known objection (Adams and Aizawa 2008). The coupling-constitution fallacy is Adams and Aizawa's name for the inference from the fact that an external resource is causally coupled to a cognitive process to the conclusion that it's part of that process.

Their point is that causal connection is cheap. A pencil is coupled to the process of writing, and a thermostat is coupled to the furnace. Neither fact makes the pencil part of the writer's thinking. To show that something is part of a cognitive process, they argue, you need a mark of the cognitive: a feature that cognitive processes have and other processes lack. Their candidate is non-derived content, meaning content that doesn't depend on anyone's interpretation. The words in Otto's notebook mean something only because people give them meaning. Otto's memories, they argue, don't depend on anyone's interpretation.

Defenders of the extended mind reply that the argument never rested on coupling alone. It rested on functional role, together with conditions of reliable availability and trust.

Cognitive Bloat

A second worry is cognitive bloat: the worry that, if the extended mind thesis is true, too much will count as cognitive, such as the whole internet for anyone with a phone. If a notebook can store beliefs, it's hard to see where extension stops. Clark and Chalmers's conditions on extended belief are designed to block the worry. The resource must be reliably available and typically consulted, its information must be easy to retrieve, and the person must automatically endorse what it says (Clark and Chalmers 1998, §5). A library book consulted once fails these conditions. Critics dispute whether the conditions succeed, since a smartphone can meet them.

A Tension within 4E

The four E's aren't a single theory. A central tension runs between the extended mind thesis and enactivism (Spaulding 2021).

Extended functionalism is the version of the extended mind that defines cognitive states by their functional roles, whatever physically carries them out. Location doesn't matter, and neither, in principle, does the kind of body.

Enactivists object that this keeps the picture they reject. For extended functionalism, cognition is still information processing. It has only been relocated, partly outside the skull. For autopoietic enactivism, cognition is the sense-making of a living system, and a notebook isn't part of any such system (Thompson 2007). The disagreement isn't about where cognition is. It's about what cognition is.

Gallagher's Bridge

Shaun Gallagher's work shows how phenomenology can serve experimental science (Gallagher 2005). He distinguishes the body schema, a system of sensorimotor capacities that works below awareness to control posture and movement, from the body image, a system of perceptions, attitudes, and beliefs about one's own body.

The distinction is phenomenological: it separates how the body functions in action from how it appears when we attend to it. But it's also testable. Gallagher uses it to interpret clinical cases, such as a patient who lost the sense of touch and body position below the neck and relearned to move by watching his limbs, relying on the body image to do work the body schema normally does. The distinction lets experimenters say what a deficit is a deficit of.

Within 4E, extended functionalism and enactivism disagree about what cognition is, not only where.   • Sense-making: In enactivism, an organism's bringing forth of a meaningful world through its self-maintaining activity, so that things show up as relevant or irrelevant, good or bad, for it.

  • Parity principle: Clark and Chalmers's principle that if a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would count as cognitive, then that part of the world is part of the cognitive process.
  • 4E cognition: An umbrella term for approaches holding that cognition is embodied, embedded, extended, or enactive, rather than confined to processes in the brain.

Guided Reading 7 min

Guided Close Reading: Otto's Notebook and the Parity Principle in "The Extended Mind"

Introduction

The claim that a notebook can hold a belief sounds like a provocation. Clark and Chalmers mean it as a conclusion, reached by an argument whose premises many readers accept. The example of Otto and Inga carries the argument from extended cognition to extended mind. This reading works through the example in four moves, sets out the conditions Clark and Chalmers place on extended belief, flags a common misreading, and ends with a model paraphrase and one phenomenological objection.

Locating the Passage

The example is in section 4 of "The Extended Mind," titled "From Cognition to Mind" (Clark and Chalmers 1998, §4). The conditions on extended belief follow in section 5. The article is free on Chalmers's website, so you can read the passage in full as you go. Key phrases are quoted briefly below, and the rest is paraphrased.

Before you start, note the setting. In section 2, Clark and Chalmers have argued that cognitive processes can extend, using the parity principle: if a part of the world functions as a process that we'd count as cognitive if it were done in the head, it's part of the cognitive process. Section 4 asks whether states, and in particular beliefs, can extend too.

A note on the example. Clark and Chalmers describe Otto as having Alzheimer's disease, a condition that affects memory. The example concerns how Otto's notebook functions for him. It isn't a claim about people with the condition in general.

Walking Through the Argument

Step 1: Inga remembers the museum's address

Inga hears that there's an exhibition at the Museum of Modern Art. She thinks for a moment, recalls that the museum is on 53rd Street, and walks there.

Notice what Clark and Chalmers say about Inga. She believed the museum was on 53rd Street even before she recalled it. The belief was stored in memory, waiting to be used. Philosophers call such a belief non-occurrent: held, but not currently in mind. Most of our beliefs are like this.

Step 2: Otto, who has Alzheimer's disease, consults his notebook

Otto hears about the same exhibition. Because of his condition, he relies on a notebook in which he writes down information he needs. He carries it everywhere. He looks up the address, reads that the museum is on 53rd Street, and walks there.

The shift here is in where the information sits. For Inga, it's in biological memory. For Otto, it's in the notebook. Everything else in the two stories is the same: the same information, the same use of it, the same result.

Step 3: The notebook plays the same functional role

Clark and Chalmers now apply the parity principle. They argue that the information in Otto's notebook functions just like the information in Inga's memory. It's there when he needs it. He trusts it. It guides his action in the same way. The only difference is that the information lies, in their phrase, "beyond the skin" (Clark and Chalmers 1998, §4).

If location alone isn't a reason to deny that something is cognitive, as the parity principle holds, then this difference shouldn't matter.

Step 4: Therefore the belief extends

The conclusion follows. Otto believed the museum was on 53rd Street before he looked it up, just as Inga did before she recalled it. His belief was stored in the notebook. So the belief is partly constituted by something outside his body. Clark and Chalmers draw the general moral: the mind itself, not just cognitive processing, can extend into the world.

Callout: The "Glue and Trust" Conditions

A notebook in a drawer doesn't extend anyone's mind. Clark and Chalmers set conditions on when an external resource counts, which later discussion summarizes as "glue and trust" (Clark and Chalmers 1998, §5).

ConditionWhat it requiresWhy it matters
Reliable availabilityThe resource is a constant in the person's life and is typically consulted when relevantGlue: the resource is coupled to the person as memory is
Easy accessThe information can be retrieved directly, without difficultyGlue: using it is as easy as recalling
Automatic endorsementThe person "automatically endorses" what they retrieve, without checking itTrust: the information is treated as one's own
Past endorsementThe information was consciously endorsed at some pointThe authors note this condition is less clearly required

The conditions are meant to block cognitive bloat. A library book you consult once doesn't meet them. Otto's notebook does.

Key Considerations

Terms.

TermWhat it names in the article
Active externalismExternalism based on the active role of the environment in driving present cognitive processes
Parity principleThe principle that location alone doesn't decide what counts as cognitive
Coupled systemAn organism and an external resource interacting in both directions
Non-occurrent beliefA belief that's held but not currently in mind

A common misreading. The most common misreading equates the extended mind with enactivism, as if both simply said that the mind isn't in the head.

  • The extended mind is functionalist. It defines cognitive states by their functional roles and allows those roles to be filled outside the body. Cognition remains information processing, relocated.
  • Enactivism is about what cognition is. For autopoietic enactivism, cognition is the sense-making of a living, self-producing system (Thompson 2007).
  • An enactivist would object to Otto's case. The notebook doesn't produce itself or make sense of anything. It belongs to Otto's world of significance, not to the living system that makes sense. The enactivist would locate Otto's mind in his whole embodied activity, including his use of the notebook, without counting the notebook as a part of the mind.

Summary

The passage moves from Inga's stored belief, through Otto's notebook, to the notebook's functional parity with memory, and on to extended belief. Here is a model paraphrase of the argument to check your reading against:

Inga believes the museum is on 53rd Street before she recalls it, because the information is stored in her memory, ready for use. Otto, who has Alzheimer's disease, keeps the same information in a notebook he carries and trusts. When he looks it up, it guides his action exactly as Inga's memory guides hers. By the parity principle, a difference in location alone isn't a reason to treat the cases differently. So Otto, too, believed the address before he looked it up, and his belief is partly constituted by the notebook. The claim holds only when the resource is reliably available, easy to access, and automatically trusted (Clark and Chalmers 1998, §§4–5).

A phenomenological objection targets Step 3. For Inga, the belief is lived as her own knowing. The address comes to mind as something she knows, with no sense of consulting anything. For Otto, the address is given as something read. He perceives the page, and he trusts it as a record. Even if the notebook's functional role matches memory's, the experience of believing differs: one is recall, the other is perception of a trusted source. If believing includes the pre-reflective sense that the belief is one's own, the parity principle misses a difference that matters.

POSITIONS TO KEEP ACCURATE
- Enaction is the view that cognition is embodied action through which an organism brings forth its world.
- Autopoietic enactivism grounds cognition in self-production and sense-making. Sensorimotor enactivism treats perceiving as mastery of sensorimotor contingencies. Radical enactivism holds that basic cognition has no content.
- 4E cognition groups embodied, embedded, extended, and enactive claims, which differ in strength.
- The extended mind thesis rests on the parity principle and on conditions of availability and trust.
- Adams and Aizawa object that coupling isn't constitution. Critics also worry about cognitive bloat.

MISREADINGS TO CORRECT GENTLY
Keep every point tied to my own practice and tools.
- Extended and enactive as the same thesis. The extended mind is functionalist and relocates cognition, while enactivism redefines what cognition is.
- Any tool use as extended mind. Clark and Chalmers's conditions must be met, and embedded cognition may describe the case better.
- Enactivism as behaviorism. Enactivists affirm experience and meaning; they deny only that cognition is the representation of a pregiven world.
- Sensorimotor contingencies as reflexes. They're law-like relations between movement and sensory change, which the perceiver has mastered.
- Coupling as enough. Clarify the coupling-constitution objection.
